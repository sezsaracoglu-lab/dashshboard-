Theme 1 · Passer, Research Methods · Chapter 8 · Single-factor experimental designs

How can a researcher be sure that the one thing they changed caused the difference?

Students with a red number in the corner of their test page solved fewer puzzles, and the colour was the most plausible explanation. That sounds hard to believe. This theme shows how an experiment is built so that such a claim can be trusted, and how to spot the moment it cannot.

The story of this theme

What is this chapter really for, and how do its five sections fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. An experiment is a study in which the researcher changes one thing on purpose, keeps everything else the same, and then measures what happens. The thing that is changed is the independent variable. The thing that is measured is the dependent variable. Chapter 8 is about the simplest kind of experiment: one with a single independent variable. Passer calls this a single-factor design ("factor" is another word for independent variable).

One study runs through the whole chapter. Andrew Elliot and his colleagues (2007) gave students a short puzzle test. Each test page had a participant number in the corner, written in red, green or black ink. Students with a red number solved fewer puzzles. The chapter keeps coming back to this study to show many of the design decisions a researcher has to make.

Five sections, one line of thought. (1) The logic of experimentation explains why an experiment can show cause and effect at all. (2) Manipulating independent variables shows how to create the conditions and how many you need. (3) Between-subjects designs put different people in each condition. (4) Within-subjects designs put the same people in every condition. (5) Examining the results shows how researchers decide whether a difference is more than chance.

Why a psychologist learns this: almost every claim of the form "X causes Y" in psychology rests on an experiment. If you can read the design, you can judge the claim. If you can build the design, you can test your own ideas.

Psychologists run a huge variety of experiments. One researcher switches off a gene in mice to see whether their feeding changes. Another compares different psychotherapies for depression. The topics could not be more different, so the first thing this chapter does is show what all experiments share.

Passer describes the shared method in one sentence. You isolate a factor of interest, you tinker with it, you try to keep other aspects of the situation constant, and then you see what effects your tinkering produces. He calls a well-made experiment "the best scientific tool available to draw clear conclusions about cause–effect relations".

That is a strong claim, and this section explains where the strength comes from. It introduces experimental control, shows how control satisfies the three conditions you need before you may say "X causes Y", and then names the enemy of every experiment: the confounding variable.

The chapter teaches all of this through one study about the colour red. So before the logic, the next step tells that study as a story, and later in this walk-through you will sit the test yourself.

★ Every experiment, whatever its topic, follows the same plan: change one thing, hold the rest still, measure what happens.
STEP2
The study that runs through the chapter

A red number in the corner of the page

❓ This step answers: What did Elliot and his colleagues do, what did they expect, and what did they find?

Step 1 said every experiment changes one thing and holds the rest still. Here is what that looks like in a real laboratory, told from the chair of a participant.

You are asked to solve 15 five-letter anagrams, such as NIDRK (drink). After a 5-minute practice test you get an envelope with the real test: a cover page and five pages with three anagrams each. The experimenter asks you to check that your participant ID code is on every page. It is, handwritten in the upper-right corner. You work for five minutes, get your score, fill in a questionnaire and are debriefed. What you were not told: by random assignment, your code was written in red, green or black ink.

The researchers had a hypothesis: perceiving red would impair achievement on cognitive tasks. They based it on classical conditioning. They reasoned that, from childhood, red goes together with danger and with poor achievement (warning signs, a teacher's red pen). So red may become a signal that, without conscious awareness, raises people's sense of threat in achievement situations and so impairs performance. The result matched: overall, participants with red codes performed more poorly than those with green or black codes.

Could it be a fluke? The team replicated the experiment several times (Elliot et al., 2007, 2011): with American, German and French undergraduates and German high school students, with other comparison colours, with numerical, analogy and memory tasks taken from IQ tests, and with a coloured cover sheet instead of a coloured code. In every replication, participants exposed to red performed worse overall. The question for the rest of this section is why we may believe that colour was the cause.

⚠ The trap✗ Each student solved anagrams three times, once with each colour, and did worst with red.✓ Each student saw only one colour. Three different groups were compared, and chance decided who was in which group.
📝 Exam-style questionIn the first colour experiment of Elliot et al. (2007), what did the researchers manipulate and what did they measure?

Model answer: They manipulated the colour of the ID code on the test pages (red, green or black). They measured cognitive performance as the number of anagrams solved in five minutes.

STEP3
Key concept · experimental control

The researcher holds three levers

❓ This step answers: What exactly does a researcher control in an experiment?

We have the story of the colour study. Now we take it apart. In everyday life, "control" means being able to regulate or direct something. In an experiment the word has a precise meaning with three parts, and each part is something the researcher decides.

First, the researcher can manipulate one or more independent variables. Second, the researcher can choose which dependent variables are measured, and how and when, so that the effect of the independent variable can be assessed. Third, the researcher can regulate other aspects of the research environment, including the way participants are exposed to the conditions. Think of a sound engineer at a mixing desk: one slider is moved on purpose, one meter is watched, and every other slider is taped in place.

Elliot et al. (2007) pulled all three levers. They manipulated the colour of the ID code. They chose to measure cognitive performance with an anagram task and controlled when it was given. And they regulated the rest: the task instructions were always the same, and participants were exposed to the colours by random assignment.

Why go to this trouble? Passer states the goal directly: control enables researchers to conclude that the variable they manipulate, rather than other uncontrolled factors, is the cause of any obtained effects on behaviour. In the colour study, it let the team conclude that ink colour was "the most plausible explanation" for the difference between the groups. The next step shows why these three levers are exactly the right ones.

⚠ The trap✗ Experimental control means the experiment has a control group.✓ Experimental control is the researcher's ability to manipulate, to choose the measurement and to regulate the rest. A control group is a different idea (section 2 of the chapter).
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher plays either fast or slow music while people fill in a maths test, uses the same test and the same room for everyone, and counts the number of correct answers after ten minutes. Name the three components of experimental control in this study.

Model answer: Manipulating the independent variable (music tempo: fast or slow). Choosing the dependent variable and how and when it is measured (number correct after ten minutes). Regulating other aspects of the environment (same test and room for everyone).

STEP4
The logic · from control to cause

Three questions before you may say "X causes Y"

❓ This step answers: How does experimental control help a researcher meet the three criteria for inferring cause and effect?

Step 3 gave the researcher three levers. This step shows what they are for. To conclude that a variable X has a causal influence on a variable Y, three criteria must be met. You have seen them in earlier chapters; here Passer maps each one onto a part of experimental control.

The three criteria are: covariation of X and Y (as X varies, Y varies), temporal order (the variation in X occurs before the variation in Y), and the absence of plausible alternative explanations. The logic behind them is simple: in principle, if X is the only factor in a situation that varies before a change in Y, then the logical conclusion is that the variation in X caused the change in Y. A detective uses the same logic: the suspect must be linked to the crime, must have been there before it happened, and nobody else may fit the facts.

Now the colour study, gate by gate. Covariation is achieved by creating two or more distinct conditions and checking whether the scores differ between them. Elliot et al. created red, green and black conditions, and the number of anagrams solved "differed significantly depending on the color condition". Temporal order is achieved by exposing people to the independent variable before the dependent variable can change: participants saw the colour first and then did the task, so anagram performance could not have caused the colour. The third criterion is pursued by eliminating other factors that might systematically make Y vary.

Notice the difference in wording. Passer says the first two criteria are "achieved". For the third he says researchers "attempt to achieve" it. The design itself fixes the time order (gate 2) and lets you check gate 1: whether the scores really differ is then shown by the data. Gate 3 has to be defended against every other factor one can think of, and that defence fills the rest of this section.

⚠ The trap✗ The red group scored lower, so the three criteria are met.✓ A difference between groups only shows covariation. You still need the time order, and above all you must rule out other explanations for the difference.
📝 Exam-style questionIn a survey, students who drink more coffee report more stress. A journalist writes that coffee causes stress. Which of the three criteria for a causal conclusion are not met, and why?

Model answer: Covariation is met (coffee and stress go together). Temporal order is not established: stressed students may start drinking more coffee. Alternative explanations are not ruled out: for example a heavy workload could raise both. Nothing was manipulated and nothing was held constant.

STEP5
Key concept · extraneous and confounding variables

When a second thing changes together with the first

❓ This step answers: What is a confounding variable, how does it differ from an extraneous variable, and why does it ruin a causal conclusion?
🔬 Be the researcher: choose how the sessions are planned over three weeks, then switch between the two imaginary worlds and watch the bars.

Gate 3 asked for "no plausible alternative explanation". This step shows what such an alternative looks like, with the example Passer builds himself.

Many things can influence how well someone solves anagrams: how hot, humid or noisy the room is, for example. Elliot's team was not interested in those. For them, each one was an extraneous variable: a factor that is not the focus of interest in a particular study, but that could influence the outcome if left uncontrolled. An extraneous variable is a risk. It becomes a real problem when it lines up with the conditions. A confounding variable is a factor that covaries with the independent variable in such a way that we can no longer determine which one has caused the changes in the dependent variable.

Passer's thought experiment: suppose the colour experiment took three weeks, and all red-condition students took part in week 1, all green in week 2 and all black in week 3. Now imagine week 1 was midterm week. Anxiety, distraction or fatigue around the exams could explain why the red group did worst. Table 8.1 shows the problem: the row "colour" and the row "proximity to midterm exams" change together, column by column. In the lab on the left, plan the sessions that way and compare the two worlds. The bars cannot tell you which world you are in.

The remedy is in the design, before any data are collected. Researchers reduce confounding in two ways: by keeping extraneous factors as constant as possible across the conditions, and by balancing extraneous factors that cannot be held constant. Choose "every colour in every week" in the lab: midterm stress is still there, but it now weighs equally on all three colours. The next two steps apply these two remedies to the two places confounds come from: the environment and the participants.

⚠ The trap✗ Midterm stress affects anagram scores, so it is a confounding variable in any anagram study.✓ It is extraneous. It only becomes a confound when it differs systematically between the conditions, as when one colour is tested in midterm week.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher compares a new study app with ordinary note cards. All app users are tested in a quiet library room, all note-card users in a busy cafeteria. The app group scores higher. Why can the researcher not conclude that the app works better?

Model answer: The location (noise level) covaries with the independent variable: it differs between the two conditions in the same way the study method does. It is a confounding variable, so it is impossible to tell whether the app or the quiet room caused the higher scores.

STEP6
Remedy 1 · confounds from the research environment

Hold it constant, or spread it evenly

❓ This step answers: How do researchers stop the room, the clock and the experimenter from becoming confounding variables?

Step 5 ended with two remedies: hold constant, or balance. This step applies them to the first source of confounds, the research environment, which means everything around the participant.

Many environmental factors can simply be held constant, or nearly so. All participants can do the same anagram task, in the same room, on the same chair, and the room's temperature, humidity and noise can be kept reasonably steady. A factor that never changes cannot explain a difference between groups. Other factors cannot be held constant even in principle: not everyone can be tested at the same moment. Time of day, day of the week and time of the year have to be balanced instead, meaning each condition gets an equal share of them.

Passer shows how. On any given day, he would run an equal number of participants from each condition in the morning and in the afternoon. Then nobody can argue that the red group did worst because it was always tested on Friday afternoon at the end of a tiring week. And because every week of the three-week experiment includes students from every colour condition, proximity to midterms cannot explain the result either. Over the whole experiment these factors end up equivalent across the three conditions.

One part of the environment is easy to forget: the experimenter. Experimenters are not robots, so their behaviour varies a little from one participant to the next. That is not the danger. The danger is a systematic bias, where the experimenter consistently behaves differently depending on the condition a participant is in. The tools against it are rigorous training, a standardized set of instructions, automating instructions and data recording, and masking (also called blinding), which chapter 10 covers in detail. With the setting tamed, one source of confounds remains: the people themselves.

⚠ The trap✗ Balancing removes the effect of time of day on the scores.✓ The effect is still there: people may still do worse on Friday afternoon. Balancing makes sure every condition gets the same share of it, so it cannot explain a difference between conditions.
📝 Exam-style questionA study with two conditions runs for four weeks in one lab. The lab cannot be used at the same hour for everyone. What should the researcher do about the time of testing, and why does simply "keeping it constant" not work here?

Model answer: Time of testing cannot be held constant because participants must be tested at different hours and on different days. The researcher should balance it: run equal numbers of participants from each condition in each time slot (morning and afternoon, each weekday, each week), so that time is equivalent across the conditions.

STEP7
Remedy 2 · confounds from participant characteristics

Maybe the red group was just weaker to begin with

❓ This step answers: How do researchers make sure that the groups did not already differ before the experiment started?
🎲 Be the researcher: put 30 volunteers into the three colour groups. Try both ways of assigning, deal a few times, then change the number of volunteers.

Suppose the room, the clock and the experimenter are all under control. Passer says one objection still stands. Perhaps the students in the red condition would have done worst anyway, even with a green or black code. Maybe, as a group, they simply had poorer anagram ability, less confidence or lower intelligence. Then their low score would have nothing to do with red.

How this objection is handled depends on a basic design choice, which the rest of the chapter works out. In a between-subjects design, different participants are assigned to each condition. The usual protection there is random assignment: each participant has an equal probability of being assigned to any one of the conditions. Random assignment does not make people the same. It distributes their individual differences across the conditions in an unbiased, nonsystematic way, so that at the start of the experiment the groups are assumed to be equivalent to one another. Think of shuffling a deck before dealing three hands: nobody chose who gets the aces.

In a within-subjects design, each participant takes part in every condition. Stable characteristics such as general anagram ability and intelligence then essentially remain the same in every condition, because the people are the same, and cannot confound the results. But a new problem appears: boredom, fatigue and experience with the task change as a person moves from one condition to the next. If red were always the last condition, you could not tell whether red or tiredness lowered the scores. The protection is counterbalancing: the order of conditions is varied so that no condition has an overall advantage over the others.

So there are two routes to the same goal, and each one answers the objection that opened this step. Elliot's team took the first route: different students per colour, assigned at random. That is why the low scores of the red group cannot plausibly be blamed on weaker students. You have now seen every piece of the logic. In the next step you run the colour study yourself.

⚠ The trap✗ Random assignment makes the groups identical.✓ It makes them equivalent overall: differences between people are spread over the groups without bias. Small chance differences remain, as you can see when you deal again in the lab.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher lets participants choose whether they want to do a memory task with or without background music. The "with music" group remembers fewer words. Which alternative explanation has the researcher failed to rule out, and which procedure would have ruled it out?

Model answer: The groups may have differed before the task: people who choose music may differ in memory ability, motivation or habits from people who choose silence. Participant characteristics are confounded with the condition. Random assignment to the two conditions would have distributed these differences across the groups in an unbiased way.

STEP9
The section in one page

Why an experiment can say "this caused that"

❓ This step answers: What is the conclusion of this section, in plain words, and what does it prepare you for?

You started with a claim that sounded unlikely: a red number in the corner of a page lowers performance. You now have everything needed to say why the claim deserves to be taken seriously.

The researcher controlled three things: what was changed (the ink colour), what was measured and when (anagrams solved, at a moment they chose), and everything else (same instructions, random assignment). Together, those three levers open the three gates to a causal conclusion: the scores differed with colour, the colour came before the test, and other explanations were made implausible.

The third gate is the one that has to be defended. Any factor that travels together with the independent variable is a confounding variable, and it leaves you unable to say what caused the difference. Confounds come from the setting and from the people. Against the setting you hold constant or balance. Against people differences you assign at random, or you use the same people and counterbalance the order.

This is the foundation for the rest of the chapter. Section 2 looks more closely at the first lever: how to create the conditions of an independent variable. Sections 3 and 4 work out the two routes from step 7, between-subjects and within-subjects designs. Section 5 returns to the first gate and asks how researchers decide that a difference is unlikely to be due to chance.

🧠 Think of it this way. You want to know whether a new fertilizer makes tomato plants grow taller. You give it to the plants by the sunny window and not to the plants in the dark corner. The fertilized plants grow taller. Was it the fertilizer or the sun? You cannot know, because sunlight travelled together with fertilizer. Mix the pots over both spots by the toss of a coin, and the sun can no longer take the credit.

Comprehension check

1 · A room that is a little too warm for everyone in the study: is that an extraneous variable or a confounding variable?

Extraneous, and here it is under control: it is the same for every participant, so it cannot explain a difference between conditions. It would become a confounding variable only if one condition were tested in a warmer room than another.

2 · Which two of the three criteria for cause are handled by manipulating first and measuring afterwards?

Covariation (you create distinct conditions and compare the scores; the data show whether they differ) and temporal order (the exposure comes before the measurement). The third, no plausible alternative explanation, has to be defended by holding constant, balancing, random assignment or counterbalancing.

3 · In a study where every participant does all conditions, why is random assignment to groups not the tool that protects against confounds?

There are no separate groups: the same people are in every condition, so stable differences between people cannot confound the result. The risk is the order of the conditions (fatigue, boredom, experience), and the tool for that is counterbalancing.

Section 1 gave the researcher three levers. The first one was "manipulate the independent variable". In the colour study that looked simple: write a code in red, green or black ink. This section opens that lever up and shows that it contains three separate decisions.

The first decision is what kind of change you make: do your conditions differ in the amount of something or in the type of something? The second is how many conditions you create. Two is the minimum, and the section explains what you gain, and what it costs, to have more. The third is what your conditions are compared with: an experimental condition and a control condition.

Two studies carry the argument. Solomon Asch's conformity experiments show what a researcher can miss with only two conditions. A study on alcohol by Timothy Roehrs and colleagues shows the same point with a twist: people's own judgement of their performance follows a different pattern than their actual performance.

Keep the theme question in mind while you read: how can a researcher be sure that the one thing they changed caused the difference? Here the focus moves to "the one thing they changed". The way you build that change decides what your experiment is able to show.

★ Creating an independent variable means making three choices: amount or type, how many conditions, and what the comparison is.
STEP2
Key concept · quantitative and qualitative manipulation

More of it, or a different kind of it

❓ This step answers: In which two ways can an independent variable be manipulated, and what do researchers typically manipulate?

Step 1 named the first decision: amount or type. This step explains the two options, because you will be asked to recognise them in studies you have never seen.

You create an independent variable by forming two or more conditions. In a quantitative manipulation the conditions differ in the amount of some factor. In a qualitative manipulation they differ in the type of some factor. Think of a coffee machine: a stronger or weaker cup is a change of amount, coffee or tea is a change of type.

Passer's examples make the difference concrete. How does drinking more alcohol at a party affect behaviour? Does the size of a group influence how likely a person is to conform? Both vary an amount: alcohol dose and group size. Which type of psychotherapy treats moderate depression most effectively? Does recognising emotions from faces depend on the type of emotion? Both vary a type. The colour experiments of Elliot and colleagues were qualitative: participants were exposed to one of three colours.

What can be manipulated is wide. The chapter lists common approaches (some overlap): altering the physical environment, such as room temperature or types of sound; altering the social environment, such as leading participants to believe a job applicant is female or male, or varying group size; varying an intervention, such as the type or amount of therapy or training; varying the task, such as longer or shorter word lists; varying the strategy people are told to use, such as rote memorisation or visual imagery; and manipulating an organism's characteristics, such as disabling a gene in laboratory mice, or people's mood. The next step turns to the second decision: how many conditions.

⚠ The trap✗ The colour study has three conditions and counts anagrams, so its independent variable is quantitative.✓ Look at how the conditions differ from each other, not at how many there are or at what is measured. Red, green and black are kinds, not amounts: qualitative.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher has children practise spelling for 5, 15 or 30 minutes a day. A second researcher has children practise with flash cards, with a spelling game or by copying words. Which kind of manipulation does each researcher use?

Model answer: The first varies the amount of practice time, so it is a quantitative manipulation. The second varies the type of practice, so it is a qualitative manipulation.

STEP3
Design decision · the number of levels

Two levels is the minimum; the question decides the rest

❓ This step answers: How many conditions should an independent variable have, and what determines that number?

Once you know what kind of change you make, you have to decide how many versions of it to create. This step gives the vocabulary and the reasoning; the next two steps show the strongest argument for having more than two.

A single-factor design has only one independent variable, and that variable must have at least two conditions. Conditions are also called levels of the independent variable. When the one independent variable has more than two levels, the experiment is often called a single-factor, multilevel design. So "single-factor" counts the independent variables, and "multilevel" counts the conditions. The colour study has one factor with three levels.

What determines the number of levels? Passer names three key factors: the question about behaviour that you ask, your personal preferences, and your assessment of the available resources. If the question is how a full night without sleep affects people, two levels may be enough: 8 hours of deprivation and 0 hours. If the question is how different degrees of sleep loss affect people, you need more. One research team deprived people of 0, 2, 4 or 8 hours of sleep and then measured their daytime sleepiness and cognitive performance (Roehrs et al., 2003). In the same way, a study on age stereotyping could describe a job applicant as 30 or 50 years old, while a study on ethnic stereotyping gains information by describing the applicant as having one of five ethnic backgrounds.

Against the wish for more levels stand practical limits. Will enough participants be available? Is there enough money to pay them? Is the sleep laboratory free for all the hours you need? That is why a researcher's first experiment on a topic may have just two or three conditions, and later experiments add levels or more independent variables. Resources explain why researchers hold back. The next step shows what they risk when they do.

⚠ The trap✗ A single-factor design is an experiment with two conditions.✓ "Single-factor" means one independent variable. That variable can have two levels or many. More than two levels makes it a multilevel design, still with a single factor.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher pays participants nothing, 5 dollars, 10 dollars or 15 dollars for completing a boring task and measures how long they keep going. How many independent variables and how many levels does this experiment have, and what is the design called?

Model answer: One independent variable (monetary incentive) with four levels. It is a single-factor, multilevel design, and the manipulation is quantitative.

STEP4
Run it yourself · Asch (1955) and nonlinear effects

Two points can only ever draw a straight line

❓ This step answers: Why do you need three or more levels to find out whether a relation is nonlinear?
🔬 Be the researcher: you are planning Asch's conformity experiment. Tap the group sizes you want to include. Start with the two that are already chosen, then add more and watch the line.

Step 3 ended with a warning: holding back on levels has a price. This step lets you find that price yourself, with one of the best-known experiments in social psychology.

Some relations are linear: each step up in the independent variable changes the outcome by about the same amount, so the graph is a straight line. Others are nonlinear: the line bends, flattens or turns. Here is the problem. If you manipulate an amount with just two conditions, a line graph of your data can only show a straight line. You cannot know whether the relation really is straight or whether that is an artifact of having only two data points.

In Solomon Asch's (1955) experiments, groups judged the length of lines, a simple visual task. Only one member of each group was a real participant. The others were confederates who, according to plan, gave clearly wrong answers on some trials. Asch manipulated group size through the number of confederates and measured the percentage of trials on which the real participant went along with the wrong answer. His finding: conformity rose as the group grew from about one to four other members, and after that further increases had little effect. In the lab, choose only sizes 1 and 15 and you get the dashed line from Figure 8.2. It suggests a steady rise that Asch did not find.

So the rule Passer draws is this: to examine whether a nonlinear relation exists, and to give a potential linear relation a more thorough test, you need an independent variable with three or more levels. Where you place the levels matters too, as the lab shows: sizes 1, 8 and 15 reveal that the line is not straight, but they hide where the rise stops. The next step shows the same lesson in a study where the bend itself is the discovery.

⚠ The trap✗ With two conditions the line was straight, so the relation is linear.✓ With two points a line is always straight. A two-level design cannot tell a linear relation from a nonlinear one.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher tests memory after 0 or 400 mg of caffeine and finds no difference between the two groups. A colleague says: "So caffeine has no effect on memory at any dose." Why is this conclusion not justified, and how should the design be changed?

Model answer: Two levels can only show a straight line between two points. The relation might be nonlinear, for example memory could improve at a moderate dose and return to baseline at a high dose. The design needs three or more dose levels (a multilevel design) to examine the shape of the relation.

STEP5
The evidence · Roehrs et al. (2003)

When what people feel bends and what they do does not

❓ This step answers: What did the alcohol study find, and why did it need four dose levels to find it?

Asch showed that a relation can flatten. In this study the bend is the finding, and a two-level design would have missed it completely.

Timothy Roehrs and colleagues (2003) manipulated alcohol dose at four levels. Participants drank 0.0, 0.3, 0.6 or 0.9 grams of alcohol per kilogram of body weight, which gave average breath alcohol concentrations of .00, .02, .04 and .09 half an hour later. That is a quantitative manipulation in a multilevel design. One outcome was how much participants believed the alcohol had impaired their performance on cognitive tasks. The researchers also measured daytime sleepiness and actual cognitive performance.

The result for the self-ratings is Figure 8.3. Scores below 0 mean people felt impaired, scores above 0 mean they felt enhanced. Up to the moderate dose the line stays near 0. It was not until the highest dose that participants, overall, believed their performance was impaired. Sleepiness and actual performance told another story: alcohol increased sleepiness and decreased performance in a linear fashion, so the decline in performance did not wait for the highest dose as the self-ratings did.

Putting the linear and the nonlinear patterns together, the researchers concluded that participants failed to recognise alcohol's impairing effects until they had consumed enough, on average, to be legally drunk in most jurisdictions. With only a no-alcohol and a high-dose condition (the dashed line), the graph would show a straight downward line and this gap between feeling and doing would have been masked. You have now seen what the levels can reveal. The last decision is what they are compared with.

⚠ The trap✗ Figure 8.3 shows that performance was fine until the highest dose.✓ Figure 8.3 shows what participants thought about their performance. Actual performance decreased linearly with dose. The whole point is the difference between the two.
📝 Exam-style questionIn Roehrs et al. (2003), why would a design with only a no-alcohol and a high-dose condition have led to a misleading picture of self-perceived impairment?

Model answer: With two levels the graph can only be a straight line from "none" to "high", suggesting that perceived impairment increases steadily with dose. The four-level design shows that perceived impairment stayed near zero at the low and moderate doses and appeared only at the highest dose, a nonlinear relation.

STEP6
Key concept · experimental and control conditions

Every effect needs a standard of comparison

❓ This step answers: What are experimental and control conditions, and does every experiment need a control group?

You know how to make conditions differ and how many to make. One question is left: when a group scores 7 on something, is that high or low? A number means little without something to compare it with. This step is about that comparison.

In many experiments the comparison is between two kinds of conditions. An experimental condition (or experimental group) exposes participants to a treatment or an "active" level of the independent variable. In a control condition (or control group), participants do not receive the treatment of interest, or are exposed to a baseline level of the independent variable. It works like a "before" photo: it shows what things look like without the thing you are testing. Depending on the design, different participants may be in the two conditions, or the same participants may serve in both.

Passer walks through three situations. To test whether traffic noise affects cognitive performance, you manipulate presence versus absence: noise for the experimental group, no noise for the control group. To test high temperature you cannot create an "absence" of temperature, since every room has one, so the control group works at 72 °F (22.2 °C), a temperature most people find comfortable, while the experimental group works at 100 °F (37.8 °C). And in an experiment on how accurately people identify six facial emotions from 60 photographs (10 actors each portraying the six emotions), each emotion is an experimental condition and there is no control condition at all.

The emotion example carries the lesson. If the question became "do people recognise each of these expressions more accurately than a neutral face?", you would need to add a control condition: photos of the same 10 actors with a neutral expression. So whether an experiment includes a control group depends on the question you are examining. Some experiments also need special control groups for confounds that a "no treatment" or baseline group cannot remove; those come in chapter 10.

⚠ The trap✗ A study without a control group is not a real experiment.✓ Some experiments compare experimental conditions only with each other, and the concept of a control group does not apply. What makes it an experiment is manipulation and control, as in section 1.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher wants to know how lighting affects reading speed. One group reads under very dim light and another under ordinary office lighting. Which group is the control condition, and why can the researcher not use a "no lighting" control?

Model answer: The ordinary office lighting group is the control condition: it is the baseline level against which the dim-light (experimental) condition is compared. A true "absence" condition is not meaningful here, because reading without any light is impossible; so the standard of comparison is a normal level of the variable.

STEP7
The section in one page

Building the one thing you change

❓ This step answers: What is the conclusion of this section in plain words, and what comes next?

Section 1 told you to change one thing. This section showed that "changing one thing" is itself a piece of design with three choices in it.

First you choose the kind of change: an amount (quantitative) or a type (qualitative). Then you choose how many levels. The minimum is two, and the question, your preferences and your resources decide the rest. But there is one thing two levels can never do: show the shape of a relation. Asch needed several group sizes to see that conformity levels off, and Roehrs and colleagues needed four doses to see that people only feel impaired at the highest one.

Finally you choose the comparison. Often that is an experimental condition against a control condition, where "control" means no treatment or a sensible baseline level. Sometimes there is no control condition, because the question is only about differences among the experimental conditions.

All of this concerned the conditions themselves. The next two sections are about the people: do you put different participants in each condition (section 3) or the same participants in all of them (section 4)?

Section 2 was about the conditions. Now the people come in. In a single-factor experiment each participant typically either engages in only one condition, or engages in all of them. The first approach is a between-subjects design, the second a within-subjects design. This section takes the first.

Passer uses a question every student knows. Does taking lecture notes by hand, compared with taking them on a laptop, enhance learning, impair it, or make no difference? STEP2
Weighing the design · what you gain

Nothing carries over, and some questions allow nothing else

❓ This step answers: What are the advantages of putting different people in each condition?

Why would a researcher choose different people for each condition, when using the same people sounds more economical? The chapter gives four reasons.

First, because each participant engages in only one condition, effects caused by one condition, such as stress or fatigue, cannot carry over to another. Second, some questions can only be examined this way. Eckhard Hess (1959) studied imprinting, a biologically primed form of attachment in some birds and mammals: it is why young ducks or geese follow an adult in a line. Hess raised mallard ducklings in isolation and manipulated the number of hours after hatching at which each was briefly exposed to a moving wooden model of an adult duck, which it could follow around a circular runway for 10 minutes. Later he tested whether the duckling would follow the original model or a different model it had not seen before. Imprinting was most likely when first exposure came between 13 and 16 hours after hatching; by 32 hours it succeeded for only a small percentage. Hess concluded there is a critical period. The design had to be between-subjects, because each duckling can have only one first exposure.

Third, even when people could do every condition, that may require several different but equivalent versions of a task, and it may tip participants off about the hypothesis. In the note-taking study a between-subjects design needs only one lecture. In the colour study, students could not get the same anagrams three times, so three sets of equal difficulty would be needed; the between-subjects design let Elliot and colleagues give everyone the same single set. And a participant who received a red code, then a green one, then a black one might start to suspect that the study was about colour and change their behaviour. In debriefing, not a single participant in any of their experiments suspected the true purpose.

Fourth, a task may simply be too time-consuming, too fatiguing or too stressful to have people do it in every condition, for practical or ethical reasons. Taken together, a between-subjects design keeps each person's experience clean: one condition, no knowledge of the others. The price of that cleanliness is the subject of the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ Hess used different ducklings per age only because it was easier.✓ He had no choice. A first exposure can happen only once, so age of first exposure cannot be varied within the same animal.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher wants to compare three ways of breaking bad news to patients' relatives, using trained actors, and to measure how distressed participants feel afterwards. Give two reasons why a between-subjects design is the better choice here.

Model answer: (1) Distress from one condition could carry over to the next if the same participants experienced all three. (2) Participants who experience all three ways are likely to work out what the study is about and adjust their responses. A further reason: experiencing the stressful situation three times may be ethically or practically inadvisable.

STEP3
Weighing the design · what it costs

Less sensitive, and hungry for participants

❓ This step answers: What are the two disadvantages of between-subjects designs?
🧮 Be the researcher: set the number of conditions and how many people you want per condition, and compare what the two designs ask of you.

Step 2 listed what a between-subjects design protects you from. This step is the other side of the ledger. There are two costs, and both come from the same fact: the groups are different people.

The first cost is about sensitivity. Between-subjects designs usually are effective at creating equivalent groups, but they are less effective at it than within-subjects designs. Passer puts it as a question: what could be more equivalent than having the same participants in every condition? Imagine weighing a letter on a scale that sits on a slightly wobbly table. The letter's weight is there, but the wobble makes it harder to read.

Here is how that works in a study. If an experimental group and a control group differ on the dependent variable, some portion of that difference may come from overall differences, even if small, in the groups' pre-existing personal characteristics, and not from the independent variable. So in a between-subjects design the independent variable essentially has to "work harder", meaning it must produce a bigger effect, to be noticed. Said another way: if an independent variable truly influences behaviour, between-subjects designs will overall be less sensitive to detecting that effect than within-subjects designs.

The second cost is the number of participants. In Figure 8.4 the between-subjects version gives data from 10 students per condition, while a within-subjects version with the same 20 students would give data from 20 per condition. The gap grows with the number of conditions and the sample size you want. With four conditions and 30 participants per condition you must recruit 120 people between-subjects, but only 30 within-subjects. Use the lab to see how fast that grows. Given these costs, the designs that follow are all about making the groups as equivalent as possible.

⚠ The trap✗ Less sensitive means the results of between-subjects experiments are less trustworthy.✓ It means a true effect is harder to detect, because differences between people add to the comparison. An effect that is found is not less real.
📝 Exam-style questionAn experiment has five conditions and the researcher wants data from 24 participants in each condition. How many participants are needed if each participant engages in one condition, and how many if each participant engages in all conditions? Give whole numbers.

Model answer: One condition each (between-subjects): 5 × 24 = 120 participants. All conditions (within-subjects): 24 participants.

STEP4
Design 1 · independent-groups design

Equivalent does not mean identical

❓ This step answers: What does random assignment really achieve, and why does it work better with more participants?
🪙 Run it yourself: each run flips a set of coins. Choose how many coins, and look at how often a run comes out all heads or badly lopsided.

Section 1 introduced random assignment in two sentences. Here it becomes a design with a name, and Passer is careful about what it does and does not promise.

In an independent-groups design (also called a random-groups design), participants are randomly assigned to the various conditions. The colour experiment is one: each participant saw only red, or green, or black, and that was decided randomly. Random assignment does not erase the fact that every participant is unique, nor that these differences may influence how people respond. What it does is distribute those differences across the conditions in an unbiased way. That lets researchers assume, with a high level of confidence, that before the independent variable is introduced the groups are equivalent overall, both on what the dependent measure assesses and on other characteristics that might influence it.

Equivalent does not mean identical or perfectly matched. It means that any pre-existing differences will likely be too inconsequential to provide a plausible alternative explanation for a statistically significant finding. In the colour experiment we can be highly confident that the three groups were equivalent overall in anagram ability, intelligence and past experience with anagrams, so we can confidently discard those as explanations for the red group's poorer performance. Notice the words "highly confident" and not "absolutely sure". By chance, once in a while, an improbable split does happen. Flip a coin 10 times and the odds of 10 heads are less than 1 in 1,000, but nothing guarantees it will not happen.

This is why the number of participants matters. Flip a coin only twice and the extreme outcome of all heads has odds of 25%. Flip it 20 times and all heads becomes almost impossible, and any large imbalance becomes much less likely. In the same way, the more participants are randomly assigned, the more the laws of chance can even things out, and the less likely it is that most of the weaker anagram solvers end up in the red condition. In practice researchers do not flip coins: they use a random number table or a random number generator. The lab shows the coin version of this argument. One practical weakness of simple random assignment remains, and the next step fixes it.

⚠ The trap✗ Random assignment guarantees that the groups are equal.✓ It makes equivalence very likely, not certain, and it becomes more likely as the number of participants grows.
📝 Exam-style questionTwo researchers run the same two-condition experiment with random assignment. One has 8 participants in total, the other 200. For which researcher is the alternative explanation "the groups differed in ability from the start" more plausible, and why?

Model answer: For the researcher with 8 participants. With few participants, chance can easily put most of the stronger people in one group. With more participants, random assignment is more likely to create equivalent groups, because chance has more opportunity to even out individual differences.

STEP5
A refinement · block randomization

Random, but one full round at a time

❓ This step answers: Which two problems of simple random assignment does block randomization solve, and how does it work?
📅 Be the researcher: you have three days with six one-person sessions each. Assign colours in both ways, deal a few times, and compare the counts.

Step 4 ended with a practical weakness of simple random assignment. It is the same thing that happens when you flip a coin many times: you get streaks.

With basic random assignment, several participants in a row will sometimes be assigned to the same condition. That is not ideal: by chance, too many participants might end up in the red condition on a Friday afternoon or a Monday morning. You are also likely to end with slightly different numbers of participants per condition. Flip a coin 20 times and the odds of exactly 10 heads and 10 tails are only 17.6%. Block randomization addresses both issues. You conduct a single round of all the conditions, then another round, then another, for as many rounds as needed, and within each round the order of conditions is randomly determined. Think of dealing cards one full round at a time, so that nobody is ever more than one card ahead.

Passer's example is based on the first colour experiment. There are three conditions and we want 30 participants per condition. On Monday there are three morning sessions and three afternoon sessions, each with one participant. For Monday morning we randomly order the three conditions, say black, red, green: that is the first block. The 9 a.m. participant gets the black code, the 10 a.m. participant red, the 11 a.m. participant green. For the afternoon we randomly order them again, say green, black, red, for the 1, 2 and 3 p.m. participants. Every new block gets a fresh random order, and 30 blocks give 30 participants per condition.

The result is two kinds of balance at once. By the end, each colour will ideally have been run an equal number of times in total, and also an equal number of times in the morning, in the afternoon, on each weekday and in each week. That second kind should sound familiar: it is the "balancing" of time of day and week from section 1, now built into the assignment procedure. In the lab, compare the counts under the two methods.

⚠ The trap✗ In block randomization the experimenter chooses a fixed order such as red, green, black and repeats it.✓ The order inside every block is random and is drawn fresh for each block. Only the rule "each condition once per block" is fixed.
📝 Exam-style questionAn experiment has four conditions and uses block randomization with one participant per session. After 13 blocks, how many participants have been tested in each condition, and how many in total? Give whole numbers.

Model answer: Each block contains every condition once, so after 13 blocks each condition has 13 participants and the total is 4 × 13 = 52.

STEP6
Design 2 · matched-groups design

Line them up first, then let chance decide

❓ This step answers: How does a matched-groups design create equivalent groups, and when is it worth the trouble?
🧩 Be the researcher: twelve volunteers have taken an earlier anagram test. Match them in sets of three and let chance assign each set over the three colours.

Random assignment leaves the equivalence of the groups to chance. With many participants that works well. But could you help chance a little? That is the idea of the second design.

A matching variable is a characteristic on which sets of individuals are matched as closely as possible. In a matched-groups design, each set of participants that has been matched on one or more attributes is then randomly assigned to the conditions. It is how fair teams are picked: rank the players, then split each pair of similar players over the two teams by a coin toss. With only two conditions, pairs are matched and one member of each pair goes to each condition; this is often called a matched-pairs design.

For a colour experiment, Passer describes it this way. Give all participants an anagram test at an earlier point in time. Take the three with the highest ability and randomly assign one of them to each colour. Take the next three and do the same, and continue down to the three poorest performers. You might think one should always match on the very attribute that the dependent measure will assess, but this is often impossible or impractical, and giving people the same kind of task beforehand might tip them off about the purpose. The alternative is to match on another variable you worry about as a possible confound. If intelligence and verbal aptitude are substantially correlated with anagram performance, match on those. Then if the red group performs worst while being matched with the others on IQ and verbal aptitude, it is difficult to argue that those, and not the colour, produced the result.

Matching has a cost. You have to administer the measures or obtain existing records, which is sometimes not feasible, for instance for reasons of confidentiality. It can be easy, as when two tutoring groups of schoolchildren are matched on school grade and age. Even when feasible, the benefit has to be weighed against the added complexity. Many researchers choose to recruit a sufficiently large number of participants, rely solely on random assignment, and let chance balance out what might otherwise be too many potential matching variables to keep track of. Both designs so far manipulate the independent variable. The next one does not.

⚠ The trap✗ In a matched-groups design the researcher decides who goes into which condition.✓ The researcher only forms the matched sets. Within each set, random assignment still decides who gets which condition.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher compares two reading programmes for children. Before the study she ranks the children on their current reading score, takes them two at a time from the top of the ranking, and tosses a coin to decide which child of each pair gets which programme. Name the design and the role of the reading score.

Model answer: A matched-groups design with two conditions, often called a matched-pairs design. The current reading score is the matching variable. Random assignment is still used, within each matched pair.

STEP7
Design 3 · natural-groups design

Looks like an experiment, is a correlational study

❓ This step answers: What is a natural-groups design, and why is it not an experiment?

The first two designs both start by manipulating an independent variable. Many studies that look just like them do something else, and the difference decides what you are allowed to conclude.

A subject variable is a personal characteristic on which individuals vary from one another, such as self-esteem, age, sex or ethnicity. In a natural-groups design, a researcher measures a subject variable, forms different groups based on people's level of that variable, and then measures how the groups respond on other variables. The conditions are created by selecting people, not by doing something to them. It is the difference between giving half of your friends coffee and comparing your friends who already drink coffee with those who do not.

Passer's example asks whether self-esteem influences how introverted or extraverted people are. Give a self-esteem test to 200 college students. Take the 40 highest and the 40 lowest scorers: they are the high and low self-esteem conditions. Bring each to the lab, let them interact with others, and record how introverted or extraverted their behaviour is. Other studies use the same logic with morning types, intermediate types and evening types (Vollmer & Randler, 2012), or with degrees of religiousness and health (Lutgendorf et al., 2002). Is the subject variable an "independent variable"? Researchers disagree. Some speak of selected or quasi-independent variables; others reserve the term for manipulated variables.

The more important issue is whether this is an experiment. It can feel like one, because groups are created. But a natural-groups design is a correlational study. The researcher measured naturally occurring self-esteem and did not manipulate it; sorting people into "high" and "low" instead of using their exact scores does not change that. So its findings face the two problems of all correlational research. Bidirectionality: did low self-esteem make people introverted, or did being introverted lower their self-esteem? And the third-variable problem: perhaps other personal or environmental factors cause or accompany low self-esteem and are the real reason for introversion. This does not make such studies unimportant. It means researchers must be especially cautious about causal conclusions from them.

⚠ The trap✗ The study compared two groups, so it was an experiment.✓ Ask how the groups were made. If people were sorted by a characteristic they already had, nothing was manipulated and it is a correlational study.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher gives 300 employees an optimism questionnaire, selects the 50 most and the 50 least optimistic, and finds that the optimistic group has fewer sick days. She concludes that optimism protects health. Give two reasons why this conclusion is not justified.

Model answer: Optimism was measured, not manipulated, so this is a natural-groups (correlational) design. (1) Bidirectionality: being healthier may make people more optimistic. (2) Third-variable problem: other factors, for example a less demanding job or a higher income, may lead to both more optimism and fewer sick days.

STEP8
Clearing up a confusion · random sampling and random assignment

Who gets in, and who goes where

❓ This step answers: What is the difference between random sampling and random assignment?

Random assignment is a cornerstone of between-subjects experiments. Passer notes that some students confuse it with another procedure that also has "random" in its name. Both rely on the laws of probability and both are good scientific practice, but they serve different goals.

Random sampling is about who will be asked to participate: each member of a population has an equal probability of being selected into the sample. Its goal is a sample whose characteristics are representative of the broader population. Surveys are the typical case. Random assignment comes later and answers a different question: once someone has agreed or been chosen to participate, which condition do they get? Each participant has an equal probability of being assigned to any condition. Its goal is to take whatever sample you happen to have and place people into the conditions in an unbiased way, so the groups can be assumed equivalent, overall, at the start.

In experiments, random sampling is rarely used. Researchers place notices or post sign-up sheets, and participants are often undergraduates in introductory psychology. Suppose 40 students from a class of 150 sign up for a decision-making experiment. They cannot automatically be considered representative of the adult population; the students on this campus may not represent college students in general; the introductory psychology students may not represent the whole campus; and those who sign up may not represent the whole class. This is convenience sampling: experimenters in most cases take whomever they can get.

Does that ruin the experiment? No. Convenience sampling is usually an adequate way to recruit participants for experiments, and it does not take away an experiment's ability to examine cause and effect. Whatever the sample looks like, random assignment decides who is placed in which condition, so the groups can be considered equivalent to one another, overall, at the start, and you can examine whether the independent variable makes these initially equivalent groups behave differently. Random sampling speaks to whom the results represent. Random assignment speaks to whether the independent variable caused the difference.

⚠ The trap✗ The participants were not randomly sampled, so the experiment cannot show cause and effect.✓ Cause and effect rest on random assignment to conditions. Sampling concerns how representative the participants are of a population.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher recruits volunteers through a poster in the university library. A computer then decides for each volunteer whether they get the training or the waiting list. Which of the two "random" procedures was used, and what does it allow the researcher to assume?

Model answer: Random assignment was used (the computer decides the condition). Random sampling was not: the volunteers are a convenience sample. Random assignment allows the researcher to assume that the training group and the waiting-list group were equivalent overall at the start of the study.

STEP9
The section in one page

Different people per condition: clean, but costly

❓ This step answers: What is the conclusion of this section in plain words, and what comes next?

Section 1 asked how you make sure groups do not differ from the start. This section gave the full answer for the case in which each person meets only one condition.

Using different people keeps every participant's experience clean. Nothing carries over, one set of materials is enough, people are less likely to guess the hypothesis, and some questions, such as Hess's ducklings, cannot be studied any other way. The price is that different groups are never as equivalent as the same people would be, so the design is less sensitive, and it needs more participants, often many more.

Everything else in the section is about protecting the equivalence of the groups. An independent-groups design leaves it to random assignment, which works better with more participants, and block randomization keeps the group sizes and the time slots in step. A matched-groups design helps chance by first matching sets of people on a relevant variable. A natural-groups design looks similar but only sorts people by a characteristic they already have; it is a correlational study and does not support the same causal conclusions.

And random assignment must not be confused with random sampling. Assignment decides which condition a participant gets; sampling decides who is in the study at all. The next section turns the whole design around: what happens when the same people take part in every condition?

Comprehension check

1 · Why is a between-subjects design "less sensitive" than a within-subjects design?

Because some of the difference between conditions may come from pre-existing differences between the groups of people. The independent variable has to produce a bigger effect to stand out against that. With the same people in every condition, those differences are essentially absent.

2 · What are the two things block randomization keeps in balance that simple random assignment does not?

The number of participants per condition (each block contains every condition once), and the spread of conditions over time, such as mornings, afternoons, weekdays and weeks, because long runs of the same condition cannot occur.

3 · A study compares "morning types" and "evening types" on exam grades. Experiment or not, and what follows?

Not an experiment. Morningness is a subject variable that was measured, so this is a natural-groups design, which is correlational. A difference in grades cannot be read as caused by being a morning type: the direction could be reversed and other variables could explain both.

Section 3 ended with two costs of using different people per condition: less sensitivity and more participants, often many more. This section starts from those two costs and asks the obvious question.

Passer returns to the emotion recognition experiment. The independent variable is the type of emotion, with nine conditions: photographs of an actor portraying anger, fear, joy, disgust, interest, surprise, contempt, shame and sadness. Between-subjects, with 20 participants per condition, that is 180 people who each look at one photo for a few seconds. But one actor's face may be unusually easy or hard to read, so it is better to use nine photos from each of, say, 10 actors. Now there are 90 photographs, and 20 judgments per photo means 1,800 participants.

That is an inefficient way to run the study, as Passer expects you to be thinking. Why not ask 20 participants to judge all 90 photographs? It will probably take no more than half an hour each. With that step we have entered the realm of within-subjects designs, also called repeated-measures designs: each participant engages in every condition of the experiment one or more times.

The saving is real, and it comes with one big new problem. When the same person goes through several conditions, the conditions come in some order, and that order can change the results. Most of this section is about that problem and the family of techniques, called counterbalancing, that deals with it.

★ Within-subjects (repeated measures): each participant engages in every condition, one or more times.
STEP2
Weighing the design · what you gain

Fewer people, more data, and questions nothing else can answer

❓ This step answers: What are the advantages of having the same people in every condition?

Step 1 showed the headline saving. The chapter lists four advantages in all, and you will recognise two of them as the disadvantages of between-subjects designs turned around.

First, a within-subjects design needs fewer participants for the same amount of data per condition. That matters most when there are many conditions, when many participants per condition are wanted, when instructions or practice take long, or when participants are hard to find, such as people with uncommon abilities or disorders. Second, you can turn this around: recruit the same number of people and collect more data per condition. In Figure 8.4, 20 students give 10 per condition between-subjects and 20 per condition within-subjects. More participants per condition makes findings more reliable in the sense of less open to unusual chance fluctuations: other things being equal, if the independent variable truly has an influence, an experiment is more likely to detect it as the number of participants increases.

Third, some questions can only be answered within-subjects. Gabriel Kreiman and colleagues (2000) looked for individual neurons in the human brain that fire most to specific kinds of complex pictures. Microelectrodes had been implanted in 427 individual neurons in 11 patients with epilepsy, to help doctors find the source of their seizures. With the patients' informed consent, the researchers recorded each neuron's firing rate while showing pictures from nine categories, several pictures per category, repeatedly: up to 600 one-second presentations for some patients. To see whether a specific neuron responds differently to different stimuli, you have to measure that same neuron under every condition. The same holds in psychophysics for questions such as the smallest change in loudness a person can reliably hear, the just-noticeable difference.

Fourth, within-subjects designs do a better job of creating equivalent groups at the outset. Nothing can be more equivalent than the same participants in all conditions. That is the sensitivity point from section 3 seen from the other side. So far the design looks like a bargain. The next step shows what it costs.

⚠ The trap✗ Within-subjects designs always use fewer participants.✓ They need fewer for the same amount of data. A researcher can also keep the number of participants the same and gain more data per condition.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher has access to 12 people with a rare memory ability and wants to compare their recall under four types of background sound. Why is it sensible to let every participant take part in all four conditions?

Model answer: The participants come from a special population and are hard to obtain. With one condition per person there would be only 3 participants per condition. If each takes part in all four conditions, every condition has data from all 12, and the same people are compared with themselves, so the conditions are as equivalent as possible in participant characteristics.

STEP3
Weighing the design · what it costs

The condition you did before changes the one you do now

❓ This step answers: What are the disadvantages of within-subjects designs, and which kinds of order effects exist?

The same people in every condition: that solved the problem of different groups. It creates a problem that between-subjects designs never have. A person who does condition B after condition A is no longer the same person who started.

Order effects (also called sequence effects) occur when participants' responses are affected by the order of conditions to which they are exposed. In an experiment with conditions A, B and C, if people respond differently in A depending on whether it comes first, second or third, that is an order effect. It is like tasting three desserts in a row: your opinion of the third is not only about the third.

The chapter distinguishes three kinds. Progressive effects reflect cumulative exposure to prior conditions. Driving the same simulated route with a handheld phone, a hands-free phone and no phone, participants become more familiar with the route: a practice effect, improvement through experience. Or they tire, lose attention or motivation: a fatigue effect. Carryover effects occur when responses in one condition are uniquely influenced by the particular condition that came before it: a drink seems more or less sweet depending on whether the previous one was the sweetest or the least sweet, and performance after 4 hours of sleep may depend on whether the night before had 0 or 8 hours. Sensitization occurs when exposure to multiple conditions increases participants' awareness of the variable being manipulated. As Greenwald (1976) notes, in a within-subjects experiment on room illumination and work performance, workers who notice the lighting changing may form their own hypotheses, and those expectations may influence their performance. That differs from a contrast effect, where a room simply looks brighter after a dim one. In studies of maximum sensitivity to changes in a stimulus, sensitization can even help the researcher.

There are further costs. Exposing people to all conditions risks making them aware of the purpose. Some questions do not allow it at all: Hess's ducklings, animals raised from birth in darkness or in light (Crabtree & Riesen, 1979), or a comparison of therapies, where each person can receive only one. And you may need several equivalent sets of materials: two lectures of equal length, difficulty, clarity and appeal, and two tests of similar difficulty, developed and checked with samples of students before the real experiment starts. Among all these, order effects are the potentially huge problem, and the next step shows the main defence.

⚠ The trap✗ A practice effect and a carryover effect are the same thing.✓ A practice effect grows with the number of conditions already done, whichever they were. A carryover effect depends on which specific condition came just before.
📝 Exam-style questionParticipants rate how loud a series of tones is. A medium tone is rated as much quieter when it follows a very loud tone than when it follows a very soft tone. Which type of order effect is this, and why is it not a fatigue effect?

Model answer: A carryover effect: the response to the medium tone is influenced by the particular condition that immediately preceded it. A fatigue effect would be a general decline that builds up with repeated exposure, regardless of which tone came before.

STEP4
The main defence · counterbalancing and temporal spacing

You cannot stop order effects, but you can share them out

❓ This step answers: What is the goal of counterbalancing, and what else can reduce order effects?
🚗 Be the researcher: every participant drives the same simulated route three times. Choose who gets which order, then switch between the two imaginary worlds.

Order effects are a confounding variable in the exact sense of section 1: something that changes together with the condition. This step shows the standard way to break that link.

If all participants go through the conditions in the same order, any difference in the dependent measure might be due to the independent variable or to the order. To rule that out, researchers counterbalance the order of conditions so that no one condition has an advantage or disadvantage relative to any other. Counterbalancing does not stop order effects from happening: people still get tired, bored or more experienced. It balances the sequence of positions so order effects are less likely to work for or against any particular condition. It is like changing ends at half-time in football: the wind still blows, but both teams play with it and against it.

Two examples from the chapter. In the driving study, suppose everyone drives first with a handheld phone, then hands-free, then with no phone, and performance is worst, better and best. Can we conclude that phones impair driving? Clearly not: people also got to know the route. Try it in the lab. In the note-taking study, Figure 8.4 (book p. 237) shows two orders. Of 20 students, 10 are randomly assigned to Order 1 (longhand first, then laptop) and 10 to Order 2 (laptop first, then longhand). The two lectures, say economics and geography, are counterbalanced too: within each order, five students get economics first and five get geography first. Then nobody can plausibly argue that a result such as better conceptual learning with longhand notes came from the order of conditions or of lectures.

A second tool is temporal spacing: letting enough time pass between conditions. For fatigue, the rest interval can range from seconds to weeks depending on the task; in the note-taking study a 15 to 30 minute break after the first lecture would be reasonable. Cedernaes et al. (2014) tested whether short-term sleep deprivation makes people act more impulsively. Each participant spent two nights in a sleep lab, one with normal sleep and one with no sleep, in counterbalanced order determined randomly, and with the two sessions at least 4 weeks apart. Impulsivity was measured each morning with a decision-making task. Sleep deprivation increased impulsivity. The remaining steps show the different ways counterbalancing can be organised.

⚠ The trap✗ Counterbalancing removes practice and fatigue effects.✓ They still occur. Counterbalancing balances the positions of the conditions, so order effects are less likely to work for or against any one of them.
📝 Exam-style questionTwenty participants solve puzzles first in a quiet room and then in a noisy room. They solve more puzzles in the noisy room. Give an alternative explanation for this result and describe how the design should have been set up.

Model answer: A practice effect: participants had more experience with the puzzles by the time they reached the noisy room, and order is confounded with the room condition. The order should be counterbalanced: 10 participants randomly assigned to quiet then noisy, and 10 to noisy then quiet, ideally with different but equivalent puzzle sets whose order is counterbalanced as well.

STEP5
Each condition once · design 1 · complete counterbalancing

Use every order there is

❓ This step answers: How does an all-possible-orders design work, what does it achieve, and why can it rarely be used with many conditions?
🥤 Be the researcher: you run a cola taste test. Choose how many drinks you compare and see every order you would have to use.

Step 4 said "counterbalance the order". With two conditions that is easy: half get A then B, half get B then A. With more conditions there are several ways to do it. Passer explains them all with one example: a taste test of four colas, Pepsi, Coke, Royal Crown (RC) Cola and Shasta, where participants taste and rate each drink.

Within-subjects designs fall into two categories: each participant is exposed to every condition only once, or more than once. The first design in the "once" category is the most thorough. With n conditions there are n! ("n factorial") unique orders: 2 drinks give 2 × 1 = 2 orders, 3 drinks give 3 × 2 × 1 = 6, and 4 drinks give 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. In an all-possible-orders design, also called complete counterbalancing, the conditions are arranged in every possible sequence and an equal number of participants is assigned to each sequence, for example by simple random assignment.

With four drinks you need a minimum of 24 participants, one per order, or any multiple of 24. Because every order is used equally often, every possible confounding effect of sequence is completely counterbalanced. Specifically, three goals are met. Goal 1: every condition appears equally often in each position (each drink is equally often 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th). Goal 2: every condition appears equally often before and after every other condition (Coke comes immediately before Pepsi as often as Pepsi before Coke). Goal 3: the same holds within each pair of positions (in the 1st and 2nd position, Coke before Pepsi as often as Pepsi before Coke, and likewise for the 2nd and 3rd, and the 3rd and 4th).

The disadvantage is how fast the numbers grow. A fifth drink gives 120 orders, six conditions need 720 participants and seven need 5,040. So this design is most commonly feasible with two, three or four conditions, maybe five with a huge subject pool. Tap 5, 6 and 7 in the lab. The next design reaches the two most important goals with far fewer orders.

⚠ The trap✗ With four conditions you need four orders, one starting with each condition.✓ Complete counterbalancing uses all 4! = 24 orders. Four orders can be enough, but only in a specially built Latin square (next step), and that is a different design.
📝 Exam-style questionA within-subjects experiment has five conditions and uses complete counterbalancing with two participants per order. How many participants are needed? Give a whole number.

Model answer: 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120 orders, and 2 participants per order gives 240 participants.

STEP6
Each condition once · design 2 · the Latin square

Four orders that do the work of twenty-four

❓ This step answers: What is a Latin square, which counterbalancing goals does a Williams square reach, and what are its limits?
🔎 Check it yourself: tap a drink to see where it stands in each row and column. Then tap two drinks to count how often one comes right before the other.

Complete counterbalancing is thorough and expensive. The Latin square is the clever economy version, and the best way to understand it is to look at one and check it.

A Latin square is an n × n matrix, with n positions in the series and n orders, in which each condition appears only once in each column and each row. It works like a small sudoku. For four drinks there are several ways to build one. The one in Table 8.5 is sometimes called a Williams square, and its strength is efficiency: with only four orders it accomplishes the two most important counterbalancing goals of the all-possible-orders design.

Check both in the lab. Goal 1: every condition appears equally often in each position. Tap any drink: it is once 1st, once 2nd, once 3rd and once 4th. Goal 2: every condition appears equally often before and after every other condition. Tap Pepsi and Shasta: Pepsi comes immediately before Shasta once, and Shasta immediately before Pepsi once. The one goal it does not reach is Goal 3, balance within each pair of positions: Pepsi before Shasta happens in positions 1 to 2 (row 1), but Shasta before Pepsi happens in positions 3 to 4 (row 3). Passer calls this often minor imbalance a small price for the efficiency.

Two practical notes. You are not limited to four participants: any multiple of four works, and ideally each new set of four gets a different Williams square, to increase the variety of orders. And there is a limit: with an odd number of conditions, a single square cannot achieve Goal 2. In a 5 × 5 square Coke cannot come right before Pepsi two and a half times. The solution is a pair of 5 × 5 squares that balance each other, which doubles the number of participants needed if each is exposed to every condition only once. When even this is too much, there is a third option.

⚠ The trap✗ Any table in which each condition appears once per row and column reaches both goals.✓ Every Latin square reaches Goal 1. Balancing what comes right before what (Goal 2) needs a specially built square, such as the Williams design, and with an odd number of conditions it needs two squares.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher has six conditions and 30 participants and wants each condition to appear equally often in each position. Why is an all-possible-orders design not feasible, and which design fits?

Model answer: Six conditions give 6! = 720 possible orders, far more than 30 participants. A Latin square design fits: a 6 × 6 square has six orders, and 30 participants is a multiple of six (five participants per order, or better, five different squares). With an even number of conditions a Williams square also balances which condition immediately precedes which.

STEP7
Each condition once · design 3 · random-selected orders

Let chance pick the orders, if you have enough people

❓ This step answers: What is a random-selected-orders design, and when does it work?
🎲 Run it yourself: each participant gets one randomly drawn order of the four colas. Choose how many participants, deal a few times, and watch which drink comes first.

The first two designs plan the orders. The third does not plan them at all, and you already know the idea behind it from section 3.

In a random-selected-orders design, a subset of orders is randomly selected from the entire set of all possible orders, and each order is administered to one participant. It is used particularly when the number of conditions is large, so that the total number of possible orders greatly exceeds the number of participants available. It is random assignment applied to orders: you do not control who gets what, you trust the shuffle.

What does the shuffle deliver? Not perfect balance. Just as you are unlikely to get exactly 50 heads in 100 coin flips, it is unlikely that each of the four drinks will be in the first position, or any position, exactly 25% of the time, or will come right before and after every other drink equally often. The researcher relies on the laws of probability to produce a set of orders that is unlikely to give any condition a statistically significant bias or advantage. Drink A may appear in position 1 more often than Drink C, but the size of that difference is unlikely to be statistically significant.

The condition is the same as for random assignment of people: numbers. To be most effective, this approach should not be used when the number of participants is small, because then there are not enough random orders for chance to have a good opportunity to balance order effects. Try 4 participants and then 600 in the lab. That completes the designs in which each person meets each condition once. The last two designs repeat the conditions.

⚠ The trap✗ Random orders guarantee that each condition is first equally often.✓ They make a large imbalance unlikely, and only when there are enough participants. Exact balance is what the all-possible-orders design and the Latin square are for.
📝 Exam-style questionAn experiment has eight conditions and 90 participants, each exposed to every condition once. The researcher gives each participant a randomly drawn order. Why is this a reasonable choice here, and what does the researcher rely on?

Model answer: Eight conditions give 8! = 40,320 possible orders, far more than 90 participants, so complete counterbalancing is impossible. With a random-selected-orders design the researcher relies on the laws of probability: with a fairly large number of participants, the randomly selected orders are unlikely to give any condition a significant advantage.

STEP8
Each condition more than once · design 4 · block randomization

Round after round, each in a fresh random order

❓ This step answers: Why would participants do each condition several times, and how does a block-randomization design organise that?

Until now each participant tasted each cola once. You might wonder, as Passer does on the reader's behalf, why on earth a researcher would expose people to each condition more than once. There are at least three reasons.

The first is practical: with access to only a small number of participants, the designs of the previous steps cannot be used effectively. The second is to examine the reliability (consistency) of responses: if the same people taste the same four drinks again, do they rate them the same way and pick the same favourite? The third is to extend the generalizability of the results by representing each condition with several stimuli. If people prefer Pepsi to 7UP, have you shown that they prefer colas to lemon-lime drinks? Perhaps Coke and Sprite would have turned out differently. So you use several colas and several lemon-lime sodas, expose each participant to both kinds of drink several times with different examples, and counterbalance the order.

In a block-randomization design, every participant is exposed to multiple blocks of trials, and each block for each participant contains a newly randomized order of all the conditions. The first participant, Teresa, gets the four colas in a random order, RC, Shasta, Coke, Pepsi: that is Block 1. Then all four again in a fresh random order, Coke, Shasta, Pepsi, RC: Block 2. And so on. The next participant, Shih-Fen, also gets her blocks, each freshly randomized. You met block randomization in section 3 (book p. 240). The idea of a block is the same, but there each participant took part in one condition within one block. Here each participant does every condition in a block, and several blocks.

How well it works depends on two numbers. If the number of blocks must be small, because each trial costs time or effort, then more participants are needed to give randomization the opportunity to counterbalance order effects over the whole experiment. With many blocks per participant, block randomization can successfully counterbalance order effects even with a small number of participants. A taste test might stretch people's patience beyond three or four blocks, but in some experiments participants do 15, 20 or more blocks of trials. The last design also repeats the conditions, with a mirror in the middle.

⚠ The trap✗ Block randomization means the same thing in between-subjects and within-subjects designs.✓ The block (one full set of conditions in random order) is the same idea. But between-subjects, a participant does one condition from one block. Within-subjects, a participant does all conditions in a block, and several blocks.
📝 Exam-style questionIn a within-subjects experiment with three conditions, each participant completes 12 blocks of trials, and the order of conditions is newly randomized for every block. How many trials does each participant complete, and how often does each participant experience each condition? Give whole numbers.

Model answer: Each block contains all three conditions once, so 12 blocks give 3 × 12 = 36 trials, and each condition is experienced 12 times.

STEP9
Each condition more than once · design 5 · reverse counterbalancing

There and back again

❓ This step answers: How does an ABBA design balance order effects, and when does its logic break down?
🪞 Check it yourself: Zach tastes four colas and then the same four in reverse. Switch between the two ways tiredness of sweet drinks could build up.

Block randomization shuffles every round. The last design shuffles once and then holds up a mirror.

In a reverse-counterbalancing design (also called an ABBA-counterbalancing design), each participant receives a random order of all the conditions and then receives them again in the reverse order. The name ABBA signifies the mirror image, even when there are more than two conditions. For Zach, the random order Pepsi, Shasta, Coke, RC is followed by RC, Coke, Shasta, Pepsi. He can stop there or go through another set that starts from a new random order. The next participant gets his own random orders.

The logic: in any ABBA sequence each condition ends up with the same average position. For Zach, Pepsi is 1st and 8th, Shasta 2nd and 7th, Coke 3rd and 6th, RC 4th and 5th. Each drink has an average position of 4.5. This is a different approach from trying to make each drink appear equally often in each position, and positions can stay unbalanced: in Table 8.7, RC is the first drink in three of the eight sets shown. With more participants or sets, such imbalances become less likely. But more sets bring a new risk: participants may detect the ABBA pattern, start to anticipate the order, or form hypotheses about the purpose. Such anticipation effects might alter their responses and begin to confound the results.

There is also a hidden assumption: that order effects add up in a linear fashion, a touch more "fatigue" from sweetness with every drink. Then the advantage of being tasted first is cancelled by the disadvantage of being tasted last. But suppose Zach is fine for six tastes and then "hits the wall", finding the last two drinks sickeningly sweet. Now positions 7 and 8 carry a much greater disadvantage that positions 2 and 1 do not make up for. These are nonlinear order effects, and with them the central assumption of reverse counterbalancing no longer works. Switch the lab to see who pays. There are strategies to reduce this problem within an ABBA design, but researchers might also consider another approach, such as block randomization.

⚠ The trap✗ In an ABBA design every drink is tasted in every position.✓ No. Each drink gets the same average position, by being tasted once early and once correspondingly late. That only balances order effects if they build up evenly.
📝 Exam-style questionA participant receives three conditions in the order B, C, A and then again in reverse order. Write out the full sequence of six trials and give the average position of condition C.

Model answer: B, C, A, A, C, B. Condition C is in positions 2 and 5, so its average position is (2 + 5) / 2 = 3.5, the same as for B (1 and 6) and A (3 and 4).

STEP10
The section in one page

The same people: efficient, if you tame the order

❓ This step answers: What is the conclusion of this section in plain words, and how do the five designs fit together?

Sections 3 and 4 are two answers to the same question from section 1: how do you keep differences between people from posing as an effect of the independent variable?

The within-subjects answer is to remove the differences: use the same people in every condition. That brings fewer participants or more data, the best possible equivalence, and access to questions that cannot be asked any other way, such as how one neuron responds to different pictures. It is not always possible, it can reveal the purpose to participants, and it may need several equivalent sets of materials.

Above all, it replaces one confound with another. People no longer differ between conditions, but the conditions now differ in their place in the sequence. Practice and fatigue build up, one condition carries over into the next, and people become sensitized to what is varied. Counterbalancing does not stop any of that. It makes it less likely that any condition profits or suffers from its position, and spacing the conditions in time can reduce some effects further.

The five designs are five ways to organise that balance, each with its own price: all possible orders (complete but quickly impossible), the Latin square (efficient, two goals out of three), random-selected orders (simple, needs numbers), block randomization (repeats, flexible) and ABBA (elegant, assumes linear order effects). With the design finished, one question from section 1 is still open: when is a difference between conditions big enough to believe? That is section 5.

Sections 2 to 4 were about building the experiment. Now it has been run and the scores are in. In section 1, the first gate to a causal conclusion was covariation: the scores have to differ between conditions. This closing section explains, in concepts only, how researchers decide whether they do.

There are two jobs. Researchers use descriptive statistics to summarise their data. STEP2
Key concept · statistical significance

How big a difference does chance produce on its own?

❓ This step answers: What does it mean to call a finding statistically significant, and where does the 5% come from?
🎰 Run it yourself: in this imaginary world the treatment does nothing at all. Run the same two-group experiment 1,000 times, then pick the difference "you found" and see how often chance alone matches it.

Step 1 ended with a claim: even when the independent variable does nothing, two groups will not have exactly the same mean. Before the rule, look at how that plays out.

The idea behind an inferential test is a comparison. On one side is the difference you found between your conditions. On the other is the range of differences that chance alone tends to produce. If your difference is of a size that chance produces all the time, you have learned little. If chance would rarely produce one that large, chance becomes a poor explanation. Think of a friend who claims to guess cards. Two right in a row is ordinary luck. Ten in a row makes you look for another explanation.

Run the lab. Each of the 1,000 experiments draws two groups from the same population, so the true difference is zero. Small differences turn up constantly; large ones are rare. Now choose a difference "you found". The lab counts the share of chance-only experiments in which the difference was at least that large. The chapter words what a two-condition analysis tells you as "the probability that the difference between the mean scores of the two conditions is due to chance". What the lab shows, more precisely, is how often chance alone produces a difference at least as large as yours.

The tradition, as Passer states it: if there is less than a 5% probability that chance factors could be solely responsible for the results, the finding is considered statistically significant. Notice what that sentence does not say. It does not say the finding is certain, or large, or important. It says chance alone is an unlikely explanation. Which test produces that probability depends on how many conditions you have, and that is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ Statistically significant means the effect is big, or proven.✓ It means the result is unlikely to be due simply to chance, traditionally a probability below 5%. Size and certainty are separate matters.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher reports that the difference between the two conditions in her experiment was not statistically significant. A journalist writes: "The study proves the treatment has no effect." What is wrong with the journalist's sentence?

Model answer: "Not statistically significant" means the difference found could plausibly be due to chance; it was not unlikely enough (by the traditional 5% standard) to rule chance out. That is not proof that there is no effect. The chapter also explained why a true effect can go undetected, for example in a between-subjects design, which is less sensitive, or with few participants.

STEP3
The procedure · t test, ANOVA and post-hoc tests

Two conditions: one step. Three or more: general first, then specific

❓ This step answers: Which tests are typically used for single-factor experiments, and in which order?

Step 2 gave the idea of significance. This step names the tools that deliver it, and the order in which they are used. The chapter stays with concepts and leaves formulas to the statistics modules.

In most experiments the dependent variable is measured on an interval or ratio scale, and the two most common tests for such data are the t test and the analysis of variance. A t test helps determine whether the difference between the mean scores of two conditions is statistically significant. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) helps determine whether the overall pattern of differences among the mean scores of the conditions is statistically significant. One is a duel between two means; the other asks whether the whole set of means differs more than chance would suggest.

With only two conditions, either test can be used, and there is only one step: you compare the two conditions directly. With three or more conditions the analysis may involve several steps. Typically the first is an ANOVA on the overall pattern. If it does not reveal a statistically significant overall pattern, the analysis typically stops. If it does, researchers often proceed with tests that compare the means of specific conditions. Because these come after looking at the results and at whether the overall pattern is significant, they are called post-hoc tests or post-hoc comparisons.

So the analysis of a multilevel single-factor experiment moves from a general question (do the conditions differ at all?) to specific ones (which conditions differ from which?). It is like a smoke alarm and a search: the alarm tells you there is a fire somewhere in the building, and only then do you go room by room. Passer adds that this is not the only approach researchers can use, but a common one. The next step applies it to an example.

⚠ The trap✗ A significant ANOVA shows that every condition differs from every other condition.✓ It shows that the overall pattern of differences is unlikely to be chance. Which specific conditions differ is the job of the post-hoc tests.
📝 Exam-style questionA single-factor experiment has four conditions. The first analysis of the overall pattern of means is not statistically significant. What does the researcher typically do next?

Model answer: Typically the analysis stops. Post-hoc comparisons between specific conditions are usually carried out only when the ANOVA shows a statistically significant overall pattern.

STEP5
The section and the whole chapter in one page

From one changed thing to a conclusion you can defend

❓ This step answers: What is the conclusion of this section, and how does the whole chapter answer the theme question?

This short section closes the loop that section 1 opened. There, the first criterion for a causal conclusion was covariation. Here you learned how researchers decide that covariation is really there.

They describe first: means per condition, or percentages. Then they test: is the difference, or the pattern of differences, unlikely to be due simply to chance, by tradition a probability below 5%? With two conditions that is one comparison, by t test or ANOVA. With three or more, an ANOVA on the overall pattern typically comes first, and post-hoc tests on specific pairs follow only if that pattern is significant.

Now put the whole chapter together. A single-factor experiment changes one independent variable (section 2), on purpose and under control (section 1). To keep differences between people from posing as its effect, the researcher either assigns different people at random (section 3) or uses the same people and counterbalances the order (section 4). Then the analysis asks whether the difference that remains is more than chance (section 5).

When each link holds, the researcher can say two things at once: the independent variable is the most plausible cause, and the difference is unlikely to be chance. Neither is certainty. Passer himself notes that the experimental method has pitfalls and limitations, which chapter 10 takes up. But you now know what to check, link by link, in any experiment you read.
