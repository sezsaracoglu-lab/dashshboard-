The rules of the game

Clear from the start.

Voluntary, confidential, work-focused.Discuss real challenges without turning mentoring into performance assessment. Agree confidentiality exceptions before sharing sensitive material.
The mentee owns the outcome.The mentee sets goals, schedules meetings, prepares, takes agreed notes, acts and reviews progress. The mentor manages the quality of the relationship and process.
Experience is an offer, not an instruction.Ask and listen first. Advice comes later; the mentee decides what fits. Neither person takes managerial responsibility for the other's work.
Agree boundaries and speak up early.Give advance notice of date changes. Address discomfort, conflicts or an unhelpful match together; use your programme contact where needed. Agree the contact and route in your contract.

Three to five weeks between sessions keeps momentum. Agree the cadence together.

Mentoring works when five things are in place

1. An engaged menteeWilling to grow, invest time and take responsibility.
2. Clear goals and resultsA long term development goal with visible results.
3. PartnershipDifferent roles, equal rights. Trust and openness.
4. A steady processRegular sessions and commitments that are kept.
5. A supportive mentorExperience, plus the skill to share it at the right time.
If one is missingRaise it openly, or take it to mentor supervision.

Your private working space

No notes are sent to a service or stored automatically. Closing or refreshing this tab loses unsaved work. A saved HTML copy contains all your notes; anyone with that file can read them. If your folder syncs to a cloud drive, saved or downloaded files may sync too.

Keep identifying or sensitive details to a minimum. Exports are not encrypted. Agree what may be shared. Clearing this tab does not erase previously saved files, backups or cloud copies.

Between every meeting

Mentee: act on your commitment, record evidence, revisit your goals, and send the next focus. Mentor: reflect on how you helped, prepare relevant experience, and leave ownership with the mentee. Both: make space for direct feedback.

The topics and the six-session arrangement are a suggested sequence, not a prescribed curriculum.
