Theme 1 · Recap of Statistics 1

🧰 The toolkit and the courtroom

How do you know a result is not just luck?

Everything this year stands on last year. You describe data (frequencies, mean, median, SD), turn one score into a z-score, and then go beyond the sample with a test. A test works like a courtroom: H₀ is innocent until the evidence is too unlikely, and you can be wrong in two ways (Type I and Type II errors).

Big idea: choosing a test is classification, not memory. Row = your question, column = your data, cell = your test.
  • ADescribing: levels of measurement, distributions, mean, median, SD
  • BThe z-score and the normal table
  • CHypothesis testing in six steps, the two errors, and last year's t-tests

Theme 2 · Inference for proportions

🪙 Counting yes and no

What does a sample percentage say about the whole population?

Now the data are categories with two outcomes: went to Florida or not, passed or not. You count 812 of 1,500 students: 54.1% is a fact about your sample. The interesting number is the one you cannot see, the true percentage among all students. There are two moves: a confidence interval (estimate ± margin of error) and a significance test (how strange is my sample if the claim were true?).

Big idea: every percentage from a sample is honest only with its margin of error.
  • AThe sampling distribution of a proportion and the interval for one proportion (plus-four)
  • BThe z-test for one proportion and choosing a sample size
  • CTwo proportions: the difference, and pooling only for the test

Theme 3 · Chi-square and two-way tables

🧮 The cross-table

Is the pattern in a table of counts real?

What if a variable has more than two categories, or you have two categorical variables at once? Then you build a cross-table. The whole method is one idea: write down the table you would expect if there were no relationship, measure how far the real table is from it cell by cell, and add up the surprise. That total is χ².

Big idea: χ² = observed vs expected. A big enough total says the variables are related; it never says why.
  • AReading a cross-table: joint, marginal and conditional distributions
  • BThe χ² test of independence: expected counts, df, p-value, when the test is allowed
  • Cχ² goodness of fit: one variable against a claimed distribution
↩ Builds on 2: two proportions was a 2 × 2 table; χ² handles any size of table.

Theme 4 · Correlation and regression

📈 Two scales together

How do two measured variables move together?

Now we leave categories and measure numbers on the same people: time outdoors and motor skills, a selection test and later performance. The picture comes first (the scatterplot: direction, form, strength, outliers). Then two numbers: r says how tightly the points follow a straight line, and the least-squares line ŷ = b₀ + b₁x lets you predict.

Big idea: r² is the share of variation the line explains. r = .30 explains only 9%, and a lurking variable can fake the whole relationship.
  • AScatterplots and the correlation r
  • BThe least-squares line, residuals and r²
  • COutliers, influential points, and causation vs common response vs confounding
↩ A new column of the map: scale data instead of categories, and a relationship instead of a difference.

Theme 5 · Regression inference and multiple regression

🧱 Defending the line

Could this line be nothing, how wrong will it be, and is one predictor enough?

Lecture 4 drew a line; Lecture 5 asks you to defend it. First, could it be nothing? t = b₁ ⁄ SE on the slope and F = MSM ⁄ MSE on the model (in simple regression F = t²). Second, how far off will predictions be? The standard error of the estimate. Third, one predictor is never the real model, so you add more and each b becomes a partial effect.

Big idea: R² can only go up when you add predictors, so test whether the extra block pays (R² change).
  • ATesting the slope and the model; the regression ANOVA table
  • BThe standard error of the estimate; intervals for the mean vs one person
  • CMultiple regression, partial effects and R² change
↩ Builds on 4: the same line, now with a courtroom from Theme 1 around it.

Theme 6 · One-way ANOVA

⚖️ Three or more groups

Do several group means really differ?

A t-test compares two groups. With three or more you would need many t-tests, and the false alarms pile up. ANOVA asks one question at once. The thesis: a difference between group means only means something relative to how much people differ inside the groups. So the total spread is split into between groups and within groups, each is averaged by its df, and their ratio is F.

Big idea: F ≈ 1 when H₀ is true. A large F says the means are further apart than the noise allows; post-hoc tests say which ones.
  • AFactor, levels, the two hypotheses (the Mozart study)
  • BThree sums of squares, df, mean squares and F
  • CR² as effect size, post-hoc tests, and causality from the design
↩ Builds on 1 and 5: a t-test for more than two groups, and the same split of variance as regression.

Theme 7 · Two-way ANOVA

🔀 Two factors at once

Does the effect of one factor depend on the other?

Two factors sort people into cells. The between-groups spread from Theme 6 is now split into three: factor A, factor B and what is left over, the interaction. That gives three F values against one shared MS Within. In the lecture's Michael Jackson example, fandom has no main effect at all, yet it decides whether the music adds two points or twenty-two.

Big idea: read the interaction first. Plot the cell means: lines that are not parallel mean the effect of one factor depends on the other.
  • ADesign: factors, levels, cells, and the four kinds of mean
  • BThree null hypotheses, the split of SS and df, three F values
  • CInterpreting the interaction plot before the main effects
↩ Builds on 6: the same machine, with its between-groups part split into three.

Theme 8 · Repeated-measures ANOVA

🔁 The same people, measured again

What changes when every person appears in every condition?

Now each person is measured in every condition, so each person has their own mean. The differences between persons can be computed and lifted out of the error first. What remains splits into what the conditions explain and the error. In the lecture's fear study this gives F(2, 6) = 9.60; the same twelve scores analysed as three separate groups give F = 4.24, which is not significant.

Big idea: people differ, and repeated measures removes those person differences from the noise, so the same effect is easier to detect.
  • AWhy repeated measures, and counterbalancing
  • BSS between persons, SS model and SS error; df (n − 1)(k − 1)
  • CF, R² and when a causal reading is allowed

Three threads through every theme

Every test is the same story told in different units

Spot these three in a question and you can usually reason your way to the answer. The dots show the themes where each one matters most.

1 · Signal ÷ noise

Every test statistic is a difference divided by how much difference chance would make: z, t = b ⁄ SE, χ² (surprise per expected count), F = between ⁄ within.

12345678

2 · Split the variance

Total spread = the part your model explains + the part it does not. Regression (SSM, SSE), ANOVA (between, within), repeated measures (persons first). R² is the explained share.

12345678

3 · Significant is not the whole answer

After "is it chance?" always ask "how big?" and "what may I conclude?": the margin of error, r² and R², which groups differ, and causation only from the design.
