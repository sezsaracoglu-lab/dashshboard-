Theme 2 · Motivation · Prepared for Sezen

What makes a student start, and what keeps a student going?

Two students sit in the same lesson. One leans in, tries, fails, tries again. The other does the minimum, or nothing. This theme gives you the theories that explain the difference, and what a teacher can do about it.

The story of this theme · 6 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Theme 1 was about how to study so that it sticks. But the best strategy is useless if a student never starts, or quits when it gets hard. Theme 2 is about that engine. Researchers define motivation as the processes that start behaviour and keep it going. They do not sort students into "motivated" and "unmotivated". They ask what a student believes ("can I do this?"), what a student wants ("is it worth it, and is the reason mine?"), what a student aims for ("get better, or look good?") and how a student explains what happened ("why did I fail?").

Three readings, one line. (1) Motivation is a handbook chapter that tours the six major theories and shows how researchers now combine them. It is the map of the whole theme. (2) Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation from a self-determination theory perspective zooms in on one of the six theories and takes it all the way into classrooms and school policy. (3) Instructional practices that support adaptive motivation, engagement, and learning turns the theories around and asks the teacher's question: what can I actually do in my lessons?

Why a psychologist learns this: every teacher, trainer, coach and parent sooner or later asks "how do I motivate them?". The honest answer starts with a better question: which part of this person's motivation needs support?

Motivation

Linnenbrink-Garcia, L., & Patall, E. A. (2015). Motivation. Chapter 7 in the Handbook of Educational Psychology, pp. 91–103.

STEP1
What motivation is, and what this chapter does

Not "motivated or not": what starts behaviour and what keeps it going

❓ This step answers: How do educational psychologists define motivation, and how is the chapter built?
Authors
Lisa Linnenbrink-Garcia (Michigan State University) and Erika A. Patall (University of Texas, Austin)
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 7 of the Handbook of Educational Psychology (2015)
Built for
Overview: a short introduction to each major theory, plus the research advances of the last decade
Main message
Six theories explain student motivation; the field is now learning to combine them

Motivation, in the working definition of this chapter, refers to the processes that both start behaviour and keep it going (the exact wording is in the picture). Two things, then: what gets a student started, and what keeps the student going. The authors add that definitions vary, and that this one is consistent with all the theories they describe.

The chapter does not stop at calling students motivated or unmotivated. It examines how students' self-related beliefs, cognitions (thoughts), goals and experiences shape engagement and learning. These motivational beliefs are thought to be cognitive, conscious, affective (they involve feeling), and often under the control of the individual. That last part is the hopeful part: if motivation lives in beliefs and goals, it can change.

The plan has three parts. First, six major theoretical frameworks: for each, a theoretical overview and then the research on engagement, learning and achievement, with attention to what is new since the previous edition of the handbook. Second, work that integrates across the theories. Third, several avenues for future research.

Because this is a handbook chapter, read it differently from a research article. An article builds one argument from one study. A handbook chapter is a guided tour: it gives you the vocabulary of a whole field and tells you where the evidence is strong, where it is mixed, and where questions are open. The authors are insiders: several of the studies they cite are their own, on interest, achievement goals and choice.

One idea sits underneath all six theories, and it comes from the first one. The theories all emphasize reciprocal determinism: the interplay among the person, behaviour and the environment. A student's beliefs shape what she does; what she does changes her classroom; the classroom shapes her beliefs. And they share the idea of agency: people are not pushed around like billiard balls, they organize, regulate and reflect on themselves. Next: the map of the six theories.

⚠ The trap✗ Motivation research sorts students into motivated and unmotivated ones and then explains the difference.✓ The chapter explicitly goes beyond that. It studies how beliefs, cognitions, goals and experiences shape engagement and learning: processes, not labels.
📝 Exam-style questionGive the chapter's working definition of motivation and explain why it has two parts.

Model answer: Motivation refers to the processes of both initiating and sustaining behaviour (Schunk, Meece & Pintrich, 2014). Initiating is about starting an activity; sustaining is about continuing it over time, also when it becomes difficult. A student can do one without the other, so a full account needs both.

STEP2
The whole chapter in one picture

Six theories, six questions in a student's head

❓ This step answers: Which six theories does the chapter cover, and what is each one about?
🗺️ Build the map: six students, one sentence each. Tap the tile with the question that the sentence answers, and the theory behind it appears.

The chapter names the six frameworks from which much of today's motivation research in education is done: social cognitive theory, expectancy-value theory, interest, self-determination theory, achievement goal theory and attribution theory. Each one studies the same student from a different side. That is why one sentence from a student usually fits one theory best, although the chapter itself says later that several of these ideas overlap.

The next three steps take the theories in pairs. Step 3: can I do it? (self-efficacy, and expectancy-value theory). Step 4: do I want to? (interest, and self-determination theory). Step 5: what am I aiming for, and why did that happen? (achievement goals, and attributions). Step 6 then asks how the six fit together, and what is still unknown. The pairing into steps is ours, to make the chapter easier to walk through; the chapter simply presents the six in a row.

STEP3
Theories 1 and 2 · beliefs about success

"Can I do this?" and "Is it worth it?"

❓ This step answers: What is self-efficacy, where does it come from, and what does it do for learning?
💪 Fill Maya's tank: seven moments, one at a time. Decide first; the picture then shows what the chapter says.

Some words first. Social cognitive theory is Albert Bandura's theory (1986) that people learn and act through the interplay of what they think, what they do and what surrounds them. A construct is a named idea that researchers measure. Adaptive means helpful for learning; maladaptive means harmful. Self-regulatory strategies are the ways students steer their own learning, such as planning and checking. In the picture: Maya and a tank that stands for her self-efficacy. Four cards around it are the places confidence can come from. First you pick out which of three thoughts is self-efficacy. Then four small events happen to Maya: for each, you decide where that confidence comes from, and the card gets its official name. Two studies close the scene.

Self-efficacy is a person's belief about their capacity to carry out behaviours at a particular level. For a student: the belief that you can learn this, develop this skill or master this material. It is the answer to "can I do this, at this level?".

Self-efficacy matters because of what it goes with. Self-efficacy beliefs are related to students' course and career choices, to putting in more effort and persisting even in the face of failure, to more use of adaptive self-regulatory strategies, to more positive and fewer negative emotions, and to higher academic achievement. It grows from four sources: mastery experience, vicarious experience, social persuasion, and affective or physiological states.

What it is not. The chapter separates self-efficacy from two neighbours. An outcome expectation is the belief that a given behaviour will lead to a certain outcome ("if I study, I will pass"). Self-concept is a cognitive evaluation of your ability ("I am good at maths"). You can believe that studying leads to passing and still doubt that you are able to do the studying. That doubt is low self-efficacy.

Recent refinements. Three lines of newer research. First, calibration: the match between how capable you judge yourself and how you actually perform. Chen (2003) found that calibration and self-efficacy each had their own positive effect on adolescents' maths performance, and Klassen (2007) found learning-disabled students to be less calibrated, more overconfident. Second, collective efficacy: the shared belief of a group. Teachers' collective efficacy predicts school-wide achievement and student behaviour, and students' collective efficacy in small groups predicts performance. Third, the sources: Chen and Usher (2013) found that adolescents who drew on several sources at once had the highest science self-efficacy and achievement.

So the first answer to "what makes a student start and keep going?" is a belief: I can do this. It can be fed from four directions, and its accuracy (calibration) seems to matter too. But believing you can is not the same as wanting to. The next tab adds that second question.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-efficacy is the same as general self-confidence, or as believing that effort pays off.✓ Self-efficacy is the belief that you can carry out a specific behaviour at a particular level. "Effort pays off" is an outcome expectation; "I am good at this subject" is self-concept.
📝 Exam-style questionA student says: "I know that practising every day would make me pass, but I cannot make myself do those exercises properly." Which belief is high and which is low?

Model answer: The outcome expectation is high: the student believes the behaviour (daily practice) leads to the outcome (passing). Self-efficacy is low: the student does not believe in their own capacity to carry out that behaviour. The chapter treats the two as distinct.

❓ This step answers: How do expectancies and task values together predict what students choose and achieve?
⚖️ Read the two dials: eight thoughts and studies about Leo. Decide first, then watch the dials and the result.

Some words first. Expectancy-value theory in its modern form comes from Jacquelynne Eccles and colleagues (1983). A proximal predictor is the cause that sits closest to the outcome, the last link in the chain. To control for something means to compare like with like, for example students with the same earlier grades. An adolescent is a teenager. In the picture: two dials. The blue one is expectancy ("will I succeed?"), the gold one is task value ("is it worth it?"). The × between them is the message of the scene. The green bar on the right stands for what follows: choices and achievement. Under the dials, four chips wait for the four kinds of value. You first sort four of Leo's thoughts, then predict what each dial predicts, then meet the twist.

Modern expectancy-value theory assumes that two beliefs are the closest predictors of students' academic choices, achievement-related behaviour and, in the end, learning and achievement. An expectancy is the belief about how well you will do on an upcoming task. Task value is how much the task is worth to you. Both are themselves shaped by many psychological, social and cultural influences.

The two beliefs do different jobs. Expectancies most strongly predict performance, even when previous performance is controlled. Task values most strongly predict choices, such as which activities and courses students pick, sometimes years later. And research with adolescents suggests they work together: the effect of one is stronger when the other is high, and neither can compensate when the other is low (Nagengast et al., 2011; Trautwein et al., 2012).

Four kinds of value. Utility value: the task is useful for other parts of your life. Attainment value: doing well is personally important or relevant to who you are. Intrinsic value: the task is interesting, enjoyable or fun. Cost: the negative aspects of engaging in the task. Children can tell competence beliefs and value beliefs apart in early elementary school, and can differentiate among types of value by fifth grade. A note on wording: the chapter says simply "value" or "task value" when studies looked at several components together or at the construct as a whole. Expectancies are conceptually distinct from beliefs about ability (in theory they are different ideas), but not empirically (in the data they cannot be separated).

What develops, and for whom. Expectancies and values predict persistence, performance and choice of activities in children as young as first grade, and the relations grow stronger with age. At the same time the beliefs themselves decline with age, particularly for language arts in early elementary school and for maths in high school. Girls report higher expectancies and values for language arts; boys for maths, sports, science and engineering. And cost, long overlooked, turns out to matter: it negatively predicted undergraduates' intentions to enter graduate school, and effort cost and opportunity cost predicted intentions to leave science, technology, engineering and mathematics majors (Perez et al., 2014).

So "can I do it?" is half of the story, and the smaller half when it comes to choices. A confident student who sees no worth in the subject will not choose it. The other question, "do I want to?", gets two whole theories of its own. That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ Expectancy and value simply add up, so a very confident student can do without value.✓ Research with adolescents suggests they interact. The effect of expectancy is stronger when value is higher, and the other way round, and neither high expectancy nor high value can compensate when the other is low.
📝 Exam-style questionAccording to the chapter, which belief most strongly predicts students' enrollment choices, and what did Frome et al. (2006) find about women leaving male-dominated fields?

Model answer: Task value most strongly predicts activity choices and enrollment decisions, whereas expectancies most strongly predict performance. Frome et al. (2006): concerns about job flexibility and high time demands when balancing work and family (costs), along with lower intrinsic value of physical science, were the best predictors of women changing their occupational aspirations out of male-dominated fields. Two cautions: expectancies generally precede and predict values, and research with adolescents suggests the two interact, so neither can be ignored.

STEP4
Theories 3 and 4 · wanting, and whose reason it is

"Does this grab me?" and "Is the reason mine?"

❓ This step answers: What kinds of interest are there, how does interest develop, and what does it do for learning?
✨ Walk the path with Noah: four moments in his chemistry years, then two studies to predict.

Some words first. A domain is a subject area, such as chemistry or history. An affective state is a feeling state. Re-engage means to come back to something. An undergraduate is a bachelor student. Task involvement means being absorbed in a task. In the picture: a path with four stones. The two on the left are forms of interest that come from the situation; the two on the right live in the person. Noah has four moments: for each one, you decide which stone he is on, and the stone gets its name. Then two studies ask you to predict a result.

Interest research distinguishes two forms. Situational interest emerges from and is supported by the context: a striking demonstration, a good story, a well-chosen example. Individual interest is relatively stable and resides within the person: a deep personal connection to a domain and a willingness to come back to it over time, with positive feelings, value and personal importance.

Interest supports learning. Research on reading suggests that situational interest, or individual interest that has been activated, supports increased attention, cognitive processing and persistence. In a day-long problem-based learning session, situational interest predicted academic engagement and in turn achievement (Rotgans & Schmidt, 2011). In studies by Harackiewicz and colleagues it was positively related to task involvement and course grades; several years later it predicted course choice more strongly than achievement.

Two forms inside each form. Situational interest comes as triggered situational interest, a relatively short, heightened affective state started by something in the context, and maintained situational interest, where the situation supports more focused involvement, attention and persistence, including finding meaning and personal connections. Individual interest comes as emerging and well-developed, and Hidi and Renninger (2006) propose that knowledge is a key component: deeper stored knowledge acts as a catalyst for the shift from emerging to well-developed. A warning about names: triggered situational interest is also known as "catch" or as emerging situational interest, and maintained situational interest as "hold" or as stabilized situational interest (the chapter's notes). Do not confuse emerging situational interest with emerging individual interest. The chapter notes that there are varying views on interest, and that psychologists have studied it for more than a century, although it was relatively neglected until recent decades.

It is not always simple. The effects vary with perceived competence, initial interest, prior achievement and the type of situational interest. Durik and Harackiewicz (2007) found that triggered situational interest supported task involvement for undergraduates with low individual interest but undermined it for those with high interest, while maintained situational interest helped only those who started with high individual interest. Researchers have also documented shifts from situational to individual interest, and identified contextual supports: autonomy support, an approachable and friendly instructor, opportunities for involvement, and relevant course material.

So a teacher cannot hand a student an individual interest, but can light sparks and keep them alive, and that may in turn support individual interest. Notice how interest overlaps with task value from step 3 (intrinsic value was about a task being interesting, enjoyable or fun): the chapter itself says later that intrinsic motivation, task value and interest are very similar constructs. The next tab looks at wanting from another angle: not how strong it is, but whose it is.

⚠ The trap✗ Attention-grabbing lesson openers help every student stay involved.✓ In Durik and Harackiewicz (2007), triggered situational interest supported involvement for undergraduates with low individual interest, but undermined it for those with high interest.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the difference between triggered and maintained situational interest, and name what distinguishes emerging from well-developed individual interest.

Model answer: Triggered situational interest is a relatively short, heightened affective state initiated by contextual supports. Maintained situational interest is situational support of more focused involvement, attention and persistence, including finding meaning and personal connections to the content; students also develop deeper value and knowledge. Emerging and well-developed individual interest differ in knowledge: deeper levels of stored knowledge act as a catalyst for the shift to well-developed individual interest.

❓ This step answers: How does self-determination theory sort motivation by the reason for acting, and which needs feed it?
🧭 Whose reason is it? Five students do the same homework. Place each reason on the line, then predict three findings.

Some words first. Self-determination theory is the theory of Edward Deci and Richard Ryan (1985, 2000). The chapter calls it a macro theory: a broad theory of motivation and development, not about one narrow topic. Regulation here means what steers your behaviour. Autonomous means it feels like your own choice; controlled means it feels pushed, from outside or from inside. A longitudinal study follows the same people over time. In the picture: a line that runs from "experienced as controlled" on the left to "experienced as autonomous" on the right, with five empty places. Five students each give their reason for doing the same homework. You tap the place where each reason belongs, and the place shows its name. Then three findings to predict.

Self-determination theory distinguishes types of motivation by the reason for acting. The most basic distinction: intrinsic motivation is doing something for the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself; extrinsic motivation is doing something because it leads to a separable outcome, such as praise or money. But extrinsic motivation is not one thing: it varies in how far it has been internalized, and so in whether it is experienced as autonomous or controlled.

Extensive research relates intrinsic motivation and other autonomous forms of motivation to adaptive outcomes: creativity, academic engagement, deep conceptual learning strategies and achievement. More extrinsic forms predict, in many studies, maladaptive learning strategies and attitudes, anxiety, poorer coping, poor achievement and even dropout, though a few studies find extrinsic motivation beneficial at times. And both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation have been found to decline within and across school years.

The forms in between. Between fully extrinsic and intrinsic, motivation may come from feelings of obligation, guilt or pride (introjected), from seeing the behaviour as useful or important for personal goals (identified), or from the behaviour being fully internalized and representative of one's central values (integrated). Underneath sit three innate psychological needs: autonomy (feeling that your actions come from yourself), competence and relatedness. Supporting these needs enhances intrinsic motivation and internalization, and evidence shows their importance in both individualistic and collectivistic cultures.

What the classroom adds. Needs can be supported through teaching practices: meaningful choices, emphasizing personal relevance, non-controlling informational language, letting students express opinions and negative feelings, and providing feedback and structure. Directly controlling behaviour (suppressing perspectives, commands, surveillance) may harm motivation, engagement and learning. But the effects are complex. Choice may work best when it comes without pressure, in manageable amounts, for students who feel competent or interested or who hold an upper-class Western cultural outlook. Autonomy support and structure work together, despite the lay view that they clash. And relatedness, long neglected, is now linked with engagement, learning and achievement.

So this theory changes the question from "how much motivation?" to "what kind?". A student pushed by rewards or guilt and a student who has made the reason her own may work equally hard today, but the chapter's evidence says their learning, well-being and persistence tend to differ. Reading 2 is devoted to this theory. Next step: what students aim for, and how they explain what happens.

⚠ The trap✗ Extrinsic motivation is always controlled and always bad; only intrinsic motivation is autonomous.✓ Extrinsic motivation varies in how internalized it is. Identified and integrated motivation are extrinsic, yet experienced as more autonomous. In one study identified motivation was the form most strongly linked with academic achievement (Burton et al., 2006).
📝 Exam-style questionIn the longitudinal study by Corpus and colleagues (2009), how did intrinsic and extrinsic motivation each relate to classroom grades?

Model answer: Intrinsic motivation and classroom grades mutually influenced one another positively and reciprocally over the academic year. Extrinsic motivation was unrelated to grades, but poor academic performance predicted higher extrinsic motivation. Both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation declined within the school year and across grade levels (third to eighth grade).

STEP5
Theories 5 and 6 · purposes and causes

"What am I aiming for?" and "Why did that happen?"

❓ This step answers: Which achievement goals do students pursue, and how does each relate to learning and achievement?
🎯 Place the goal, then read the chart: four students fill the grid. Then the grid turns into the results of a meta-analysis that you predict bar by bar.

Some words first. Competence means being able to do something well. An achievement goal is the reason or purpose behind a student's effort on school tasks. Approach means moving toward something you want; avoidance means moving away from something you fear. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies into one average. A correlation is a number between −1 and +1 that shows how strongly two things go together; 0 means no link, and values near .10 are small. In the picture: first a grid with two columns (develop competence, or demonstrate it) and two rows (go for success, or dodge failure). Four students each state an aim; you tap the cell where it belongs. After that the picture becomes a bar chart: bars above the line mean "goes with higher achievement", bars below mean "goes with lower achievement".

Achievement goal theory proposes two primary purposes behind students' engagement in achievement activities. With a mastery goal the focus is on developing competence. With a performance goal the focus is on demonstrating competence. Later models crossed this with approach and avoidance, which gives four goals.

Research has established the benefits of mastery-approach goals and the detriments of performance-avoidance goals across educational outcomes. For performance-approach goals the findings remain mixed and controversial. And for achievement in particular, the chapter says a controversy remains about how goals relate to it, especially for mastery-approach and performance-approach goals. In the meta-analysis of Hulleman et al. (2010), the correlations with achievement were small: positive for mastery-approach (.11) and performance-approach (.06), negative for mastery-avoidance (−.12) and performance-avoidance (−.13).

How the model grew. First there were two goals: mastery and performance. The trichotomous model (Elliot, 1999) split performance goals into approach (appearing competent) and avoidance (avoiding appearing incompetent). The 2 × 2 model (Elliot & McGregor, 2001) extended the split to mastery: approaching the development of competence, or avoiding declining competence or not fulfilling one's potential. Mastery-avoidance goals have not been widely studied; so far they relate to negative affect, poor study strategies, avoidant behaviour and lower achievement, with some null or adaptive findings for learning strategies.

Why performance-approach is controversial. It depends on what you measure. With mostly normative items (outperforming others) the correlation with achievement was positive (.14); with items about appearance (looking smart) or evaluation (demonstrating ability) it was negative (−.14). Person-centred research adds that combinations matter: Luo et al. (2011) found a profile with at least moderate mastery, high performance-approach and low performance-avoidance goals most beneficial. And goals do not come only from inside: they are shaped by the goal structure of the classroom or school, and by personal factors such as motives and theories of intelligence.

So the same amount of effort can serve very different purposes, and the purpose colours how a student reacts to difficulty. A student who wants to grow can treat a mistake as information. A student who wants to avoid looking incompetent has every reason to hide it. What the student then tells herself about that mistake is the subject of the next tab.

⚠ The trap✗ Performance goals are bad for achievement, mastery goals are good.✓ It is not that simple. In Hulleman et al. (2010) performance-approach goals had a small positive average correlation with achievement (.06) and mastery-avoidance goals a negative one (−.12). What is established: mastery-approach goals are beneficial and performance-avoidance goals detrimental across outcomes. Performance-approach findings are mixed and depend on how the goal is measured.
📝 Exam-style questionTwo questionnaires both claim to measure performance-approach goals. One finds a positive link with achievement, the other a negative link. Explain how this can happen.

Model answer: Hulleman et al. (2010) found that the content of the items moderates the result. When items were primarily normative (outperforming others), the correlation with achievement was positive (.14). When most items focused on appearance (looking smart) and/or evaluation (demonstrating ability), it was negative (−.14). Framing performance goals as normative standards versus broader orientations about demonstrating competence yields different patterns.

❓ This step answers: How do students explain their successes and failures, and why do those explanations matter?
🔍 Take Emma's explanation apart: eight short steps. Each one switches on a part of the picture.

Some words first. To attribute means to assign a cause: "I failed because…". Attribution theory of achievement is Bernard Weiner's theory (1985) about those explanations. A dimension is one way in which causes can differ, like length or weight for objects. Aptitude is natural ability. Transient means passing, temporary. An intervention is a planned attempt to change something, here the way students explain results. In the picture: Emma with a failed test on the left. In the middle, three questions you can ask about any cause; each turns into the name of a dimension once you have used it. On the right, what each dimension leads to. The steps first build the three dimensions, then their consequences, then three findings.

Attribution theory assumes that people are motivated to understand the outcomes they experience, especially unexpected or negative ones. Looking for an explanation, a student may arrive at many possible causal attributions, such as ability, effort, luck or task difficulty. What matters for motivation is not the cause as such but where it falls on three dimensions: locus, stability and controllability.

Research is generally consistent with the theory: attributions are associated with different emotions, expectancies and academic functioning. Many intervention studies show that training students to see academic successes as controllable and academic failures as unstable has beneficial effects. One prediction does not hold up consistently, though: effort attributions are not always more beneficial than ability attributions.

The three dimensions and their effects. Locus: is the cause internal to the person (ability, effort) or external (luck, task difficulty)? A more internal locus relates to affective reactions such as pride or self-esteem. Stability: will the cause persist (aptitude) or is it transient (effort)? Stability relates most directly to expectancies for success and failure. Controllability: how much influence does the person have on the cause (effort is controllable, luck is not)? Controllability relates to hopefulness, to social emotions such as shame and guilt, and to help giving. The attributions themselves are influenced by many factors.

Connections to the other theories. Attributions now sit inside achievement goal theory and theories about intelligence. The link runs both ways: performance goals or a fixed view of intelligence go with more helpless attributions (internal, stable, uncontrollable causes after failure), mastery goals or a malleable view with more adaptive ones (controllable causes of success, unstable causes of failure). Perry et al. (2008) found the most adaptive motivation and goal striving, in the transition from high school to college, in students who combined changeable internal causes (low effort, bad strategy) with external causes that protect self-worth (test difficulty, poor teaching). And high topic interest shielded adolescents from the negative effects of excessive challenge (Fulmer & Frijters, 2011).

So after every result the student writes a small story about the cause, and that story sets the expectation, the feeling and the next move. This closes the tour of the six theories. You have probably noticed how often they borrowed from each other: goals shape attributions, interest protects against them, mindsets sit under both. Step 6 makes that overlap its subject.

⚠ The trap✗ Ability and effort are both internal causes, so they have the same effect on a student after failure.✓ They share a locus but differ on the other dimensions. In the chapter's examples aptitude is stable and effort is transient and controllable. A stable cause lowers the expectation of future success; a transient, controllable one leaves room for change.
📝 Exam-style questionA student fails and says: "The teacher made the test far too hard, and I also prepared with the wrong strategy." Using Perry et al. (2008), explain why this may be an adaptive explanation.

Model answer: It combines a modifiable, internal, controllable attribution (bad strategy) with an external, uncontrollable attribution that protects self-worth (test difficulty). Perry et al. (2008) found that students using this kind of combination for poor performance showed the most adaptive motivation and goal striving in the transition from high school to college, compared with students using other combinations.

STEP6
Beyond the six · integration · future directions

One student carries many motivations, and the field knows its gaps

❓ This step answers: Which important ideas fall outside the six theories?
🌱 Four cases beyond the six: predict an experiment, find the root, and place two theories in time.

Some words first. Malleable means changeable, able to grow; fixed means unchangeable. To prime someone is to expose them to something (here: words) that activates an idea without their noticing. An anagram is a puzzle of scrambled letters. An appraisal is a judgment about a situation. In the picture: on the left a small experiment with two groups; after you predict, bars show who did better. On the right a plant: the root is the belief about whether your abilities can grow, and after your prediction the leaves show what grows from it.

The authors end their tour by admitting what they left out. Many other theories contribute to understanding motivation, and many of them share and extend the ideas of the six. The one they single out is research on self-theories or mindsets: beliefs about whether personal attributes such as personality and intelligence are malleable or fixed. These beliefs may lie underneath several of the motivation constructs discussed before.

Research suggests that people with a more malleable theory of their attributes are more likely to adopt mastery goals, maintain intrinsic interest, persist in the face of challenge, show higher academic performance, make more adaptive attributions and have greater overall psychological well-being. And outside the reach of all six theories: Bargh et al. (2001) showed that people primed with an achievement goal, without knowing it, outperformed and persisted longer on an anagram task.

The wider family. Theories of personal control overlap with social cognitive theory, self-determination theory and attribution theory. Control-value theory (Pekrun, 2006) explains achievement emotions and performance from perceived control and value appraisals. Research on self-processes covers possible selves, self-concept, self-worth and beliefs about ability and intelligence. Flow theory studies the in-the-moment experience that resembles intrinsic motivation or interest; future time perspective research studies the value of education for the future. And unconscious motivation, rarely included in educational research, may according to the authors provide powerful explanations in educational contexts.

So the six theories are the main roads, not the whole map. The wider family repeats the same themes in new words: competence, control, value, goals. That repetition is a hint. If so many theories keep finding the same things, perhaps they can be combined. That is the next tab.

⚠ The trap✗ All motivation is conscious: students always know what drives their behaviour.✓ The six theories assume conscious, goal-directed action, but there is substantial evidence that much motivation is unconscious and that people are often unaware of what guides their moods, thoughts and behaviour.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors suggest that mindsets may "underlie" several other motivation constructs?

Model answer: Because a belief about the malleability of personal attributes is related to constructs from several theories at once. People with a more malleable (versus fixed) theory are more likely to adopt mastery goals (achievement goal theory), maintain intrinsic interest (interest, self-determination theory), make more adaptive attributions (attribution theory), persist in the face of challenge, perform better and have greater well-being.

❓ This step answers: How do researchers combine the theories, and why?
🧩 Five studies, three lenses: predict what each study found, and watch which lens it used.

Some words first. To integrate theories is to combine them. A variable is anything that is measured and differs between people, such as self-efficacy. A profile is a typical combination of scores within one person, for example "high mastery goals and high value". To mediate means to be the link in between: A changes B, and B changes C. Multiply determined means having many causes at once. In the picture: three cards, one for each way of combining theories. Five studies follow. For each you predict a finding, and the study is then pinned to the lens it used.

In the second half of the twentieth century researchers mostly worked inside one theory at a time. That built strong theories, but it hid something obvious: a real student has self-efficacy and values and goals and explanations, all at once. For more than a decade, researchers have therefore considered how multiple forms of motivation from multiple theories combine to shape engagement and learning. The authors describe three complementary approaches.

The variable-centred approach measures several kinds of motivation together and asks what each contributes. The person-centred approach looks for profiles: Conley (2012) found seven, and needed constructs from two theories to predict affect and achievement. The third approach starts from the educational context and asks how classrooms support several forms of motivation at once.

Variable-centred, in detail. Researchers explore the relative or unique contribution of each variable after controlling for other forms of motivation, and look at interactions among personal and contextual variables. Ciani et al. (2011) joined self-determination and goal theory: need satisfaction in life was linked with adopting mastery goals through autonomous motivation in class, and autonomy support slowed the decline in mastery goals over a semester. Hulleman et al. (2008) joined expectancy-value, interest and goal theory: intrinsic and utility value mediated the effects of mastery-approach goals on later interest and final grade. Others add emotion and cognition, as in Alexander's model of domain learning and Pekrun's work.

Person-centred and context, in detail. Instead of asking how one variable works across everyone and on its own, the person-centred approach identifies adaptive and maladaptive combinations. Shell and Husman (2008) found five groupings of variables along three dimensions. One dimension was a highly motivated, strategic learner; another was more intrinsically motivated and mastery-focused, tied to knowledge-building strategies but not to general cognitive and metacognitive strategies or high study effort. Context work includes Turner et al. (2011), who derived best practices from several theories; Guthrie and Wigfield's concept-oriented reading program; and Nolen (2007), who followed how several forms of reading and writing motivation developed in elementary classrooms.

So the question has shifted from "which theory is right?" to "how do the pieces work together in this student, in this classroom?". The authors single out the move beyond variable-centred analyses, toward how motivations combine within individuals, as a noteworthy advance. It also prepares reading 3, which is organized around what teachers can do for several kinds of motivation. One tab remains: what the field does not know yet.

⚠ The trap✗ A person-centred study measures how strongly one variable, such as self-efficacy, predicts achievement across all students.✓ That is the variable-centred approach. A person-centred study identifies profiles: combinations of several kinds of motivation within individuals, and compares how those profiles function.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat did Conley (2012) show about combining achievement goal theory and expectancy-value theory?

Model answer: She created seven motivational profiles using constructs from both theories and found that combining variables from multiple theories was critical for predicting affect and achievement: neither achievement goals nor subjective task value explained the pattern of findings alone. It is an example of the person-centred approach to integration.

❓ This step answers: What does the chapter present as established, and which questions does it leave open?
❓ Sort the claims: seven claims, two baskets. Then predict one last study about how motivation is measured.

Some words first. A self-report instrument is a questionnaire in which students describe themselves. Parsimony means explaining a lot with few ideas. A mechanism is the process through which one thing leads to another. Sociocultural and situated approaches study a person as part of a setting and a culture, not as an isolated mind. In the picture: two baskets. Seven claims come by, one at a time; you decide whether the chapter treats each one as shown by research or as still open or debated, and it drops into its basket. A final study then asks you to predict which of two measures worked better.

A good overview is honest about its limits. The chapter closes with six directions for future research, and each one points to a weak spot: too many overlapping names, integration that risks becoming too complex, unknown mechanisms, too little attention to context and to culture, and too much reliance on questionnaires.

Some of these gaps come with evidence. Several studies suggest students may not interpret questionnaire items as intended. And Zhou and Winne (2012) found that behavioural "goal traces" predicted test performance while self-reported goals did not, although the two were correlated.

Directions 1 to 3. (1) Names. There is a great deal of similarity among constructs related to value (intrinsic motivation, task value, interest) and competence (self-concept, self-efficacy, expectancies), and the field suffers from the variety of terms for seemingly similar constructs. Readers should pay special attention to how each study defines and measures its terms. (2) Integration with parsimony. Combining perspectives is promising, but complex theories are unlikely to be widely adopted and have limited use for practice. (3) Mechanisms. We know little about how situational interest becomes individual interest, which psychological processes shift with goal structures, or how attribution retraining influences achievement.

Directions 4 to 6. (4) Beyond social cognitive theories. Sociocultural and situated approaches put more emphasis on the person in context; they may help explain how motivation develops and give educators more realistic guidelines. (5) Culture. Research must move beyond identifying racial, ethnic or country-level differences. (6) Methods. There is a heavy reliance on self-report. The authors suggest behavioural, observational, neuroimaging, facial recognition and implicit techniques, and note that classroom observations are effective but underused.

So read every claim about motivation with two questions in mind: how was it measured, and how was it defined? That habit is what this last tab trains, and it is what separates a careful answer from a confident wrong one. The final step gathers the whole chapter.

⚠ The trap✗ Intrinsic motivation, task value and interest are three clearly different things, as are self-efficacy, expectancy and self-concept.✓ The chapter itself says there is a great deal of similarity among these constructs, and that the field suffers from the variety of terms used for seemingly similar constructs. Always check the definition and the measure.
📝 Exam-style questionWhich drawback of self-report does the chapter name, and which study does it use to show what other measures can add?

Model answer: The drawback it names: students may not interpret the items as intended (Karabenick et al., 2007; Koskey et al., 2010; Urdan & Mestas, 2006). The study: Zhou and Winne (2012) used goal traces, a behavioural measure. Goal traces and self-reported goals were correlated, but only goal traces significantly predicted test performance. The authors therefore urge refining self-report measures and also using other techniques.

The authors' conclusion singles out one advance: research has moved beyond variable-centred analyses, toward how several forms of motivation work together within individuals (the person-centred idea). (Conclusion, p. 99, in our words)

Why this matters: the conclusion does not crown one theory. It says the progress lies in seeing how the motivations work together inside one student. That is the thread into readings 2 and 3.

We have walked through the whole chapter: a definition of motivation, six theories taken in pairs, the wider family of ideas, three ways to combine the theories, and six open issues.

Motivation is what starts behaviour and keeps it going. A student is more likely to start and continue when she believes she can do the task (self-efficacy, expectancy), finds it worth doing (task value, interest), acts for reasons that feel like her own (self-determination), aims to develop her competence without being driven by fear of failure (achievement goals), and explains setbacks by causes she can change (attributions). None of these works alone. Expectancy needs value, autonomy needs structure, goals come in combinations, and whole students come in profiles. The classroom can support all of them, and research still has to work out how these motivations develop, how culture shapes them, and how to measure them beyond questionnaires.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why is "the student is unmotivated" a poor explanation in the eyes of this chapter?

Because the chapter goes beyond thinking of students as motivated or unmotivated. It asks which beliefs, cognitions, goals and experiences shape the student's engagement: low self-efficacy, low task value, an avoidance goal and a helpless attribution are four different problems that the single label hides.

2. Name one finding from the chapter in which two kinds of motivation only work in combination.

Expectancy and value: neither high expectancies nor high values can compensate when the other is low (Nagengast et al., 2011; Trautwein et al., 2012). Another: competence support (structure) and autonomy support work synergistically (Jang, Reeve & Deci, 2010). Another: Conley's (2012) profiles needed both goals and values to predict outcomes.

3. Which constructs does the chapter itself call very similar, and what follows from that for a reader?

Constructs related to value (intrinsic motivation, task value, interest) and constructs related to competence (self-concept, self-efficacy, expectancies). A reader should pay special attention to the conceptual and operational definitions when interpreting results: how the study defined its term and how it measured it.
