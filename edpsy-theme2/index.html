Theme 2 · Motivation · Prepared for Sezen

What makes a student start, and what keeps a student going?

Two students sit in the same lesson. One leans in, tries, fails, tries again. The other does the minimum, or nothing. This theme gives you the theories that explain the difference, and what a teacher can do about it.

The story of this theme · 6 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Theme 1 was about how to study so that it sticks. But the best strategy is useless if a student never starts, or quits when it gets hard. Theme 2 is about that engine. Researchers define motivation as the processes that start behaviour and keep it going. They do not sort students into "motivated" and "unmotivated". They ask what a student believes ("can I do this?"), what a student wants ("is it worth it, and is the reason mine?"), what a student aims for ("get better, or look good?") and how a student explains what happened ("why did I fail?").

Three readings, one line. (1) Motivation is a handbook chapter that tours the six major theories and shows how researchers now combine them. It is the map of the whole theme. (2) Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation from a self-determination theory perspective zooms in on one of the six theories and takes it all the way into classrooms and school policy. (3) Instructional practices that support adaptive motivation, engagement, and learning turns the theories around and asks the teacher's question: what can I actually do in my lessons?

Why a psychologist learns this: every teacher, trainer, coach and parent sooner or later asks "how do I motivate them?". The honest answer starts with a better question: which part of this person's motivation needs support?

Motivation

STEP1
What motivation is, and what this chapter does

Not "motivated or not": what starts behaviour and what keeps it going

❓ This step answers: How do educational psychologists define motivation, and how is the chapter built?
Authors
Lisa Linnenbrink-Garcia (Michigan State University) and Erika A. Patall (University of Texas, Austin)
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 7 of the Handbook of Educational Psychology (2015)
Built for
Overview: a short introduction to each major theory, plus the research advances of the last decade
Main message
Six theories explain student motivation; the field is now learning to combine them

Motivation, in the working definition of this chapter, refers to the processes that both start behaviour and keep it going (the exact wording is in the picture). Two things, then: what gets a student started, and what keeps the student going. The authors add that definitions vary, and that this one is consistent with all the theories they describe.

The chapter does not stop at calling students motivated or unmotivated. It examines how students' self-related beliefs, cognitions (thoughts), goals and experiences shape engagement and learning. These motivational beliefs are thought to be cognitive, conscious, affective (they involve feeling), and often under the control of the individual. That last part is the hopeful part: if motivation lives in beliefs and goals, it can change.

The plan has three parts. First, six major theoretical frameworks: for each, a theoretical overview and then the research on engagement, learning and achievement, with attention to what is new since the previous edition of the handbook. Second, work that integrates across the theories. Third, several avenues for future research.

Because this is a handbook chapter, read it differently from a research article. An article builds one argument from one study. A handbook chapter is a guided tour: it gives you the vocabulary of a whole field and tells you where the evidence is strong, where it is mixed, and where questions are open. The authors are insiders: several of the studies they cite are their own, on interest, achievement goals and choice.

One idea sits underneath all six theories, and it comes from the first one. The theories all emphasize reciprocal determinism: the interplay among the person, behaviour and the environment. A student's beliefs shape what she does; what she does changes her classroom; the classroom shapes her beliefs. And they share the idea of agency: people are not pushed around like billiard balls, they organize, regulate and reflect on themselves. Next: the map of the six theories.

⚠ The trap✗ Motivation research sorts students into motivated and unmotivated ones and then explains the difference.✓ The chapter explicitly goes beyond that. It studies how beliefs, cognitions, goals and experiences shape engagement and learning: processes, not labels.
📝 Exam-style questionGive the chapter's working definition of motivation and explain why it has two parts.

Model answer: Motivation refers to the processes of both initiating and sustaining behaviour (Schunk, Meece & Pintrich, 2014). Initiating is about starting an activity; sustaining is about continuing it over time, also when it becomes difficult. A student can do one without the other, so a full account needs both.

STEP2
The whole chapter in one picture

Six theories, six questions in a student's head

❓ This step answers: Which six theories does the chapter cover, and what is each one about?
🗺️ Build the map: six students, one sentence each. Tap the tile with the question that the sentence answers, and the theory behind it appears.

The chapter names the six frameworks from which much of today's motivation research in education is done: social cognitive theory, expectancy-value theory, interest, self-determination theory, achievement goal theory and attribution theory. Each one studies the same student from a different side. That is why one sentence from a student usually fits one theory best, although the chapter itself says later that several of these ideas overlap.

The next three steps take the theories in pairs. Step 3: can I do it? (self-efficacy, and expectancy-value theory). Step 4: do I want to? (interest, and self-determination theory). Step 5: what am I aiming for, and why did that happen? (achievement goals, and attributions). Step 6 then asks how the six fit together, and what is still unknown. The pairing into steps is ours, to make the chapter easier to walk through; the chapter simply presents the six in a row.

STEP3
Theories 1 and 2 · beliefs about success

"Can I do this?" and "Is it worth it?"

❓ This step answers: What is self-efficacy, where does it come from, and what does it do for learning?
💪 Fill Maya's tank: seven moments, one at a time. Decide first; the picture then shows what the chapter says.

Some words first. Social cognitive theory is Albert Bandura's theory (1986) that people learn and act through the interplay of what they think, what they do and what surrounds them. A construct is a named idea that researchers measure. Adaptive means helpful for learning; maladaptive means harmful. Self-regulatory strategies are the ways students steer their own learning, such as planning and checking. In the picture: Maya and a tank that stands for her self-efficacy. Four cards around it are the places confidence can come from. First you pick out which of three thoughts is self-efficacy. Then four small events happen to Maya: for each, you decide where that confidence comes from, and the card gets its official name. Two studies close the scene.

Self-efficacy is a person's belief about their capacity to carry out behaviours at a particular level. For a student: the belief that you can learn this, develop this skill or master this material. It is the answer to "can I do this, at this level?".

Self-efficacy matters because of what it goes with. Self-efficacy beliefs are related to students' course and career choices, to putting in more effort and persisting even in the face of failure, to more use of adaptive self-regulatory strategies, to more positive and fewer negative emotions, and to higher academic achievement. It grows from four sources: mastery experience, vicarious experience, social persuasion, and affective or physiological states.

What it is not. The chapter separates self-efficacy from two neighbours. An outcome expectation is the belief that a given behaviour will lead to a certain outcome ("if I study, I will pass"). Self-concept is a cognitive evaluation of your ability ("I am good at maths"). You can believe that studying leads to passing and still doubt that you are able to do the studying. That doubt is low self-efficacy.

Recent refinements. Three lines of newer research. First, calibration: the match between how capable you judge yourself and how you actually perform. Chen (2003) found that calibration and self-efficacy each had their own positive effect on adolescents' maths performance, and Klassen (2007) found learning-disabled students to be less calibrated, more overconfident. Second, collective efficacy: the shared belief of a group. Teachers' collective efficacy predicts school-wide achievement and student behaviour, and students' collective efficacy in small groups predicts performance. Third, the sources: Chen and Usher (2013) found that adolescents who drew on several sources at once had the highest science self-efficacy and achievement.

So the first answer to "what makes a student start and keep going?" is a belief: I can do this. It can be fed from four directions, and its accuracy (calibration) seems to matter too. But believing you can is not the same as wanting to. The next tab adds that second question.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-efficacy is the same as general self-confidence, or as believing that effort pays off.✓ Self-efficacy is the belief that you can carry out a specific behaviour at a particular level. "Effort pays off" is an outcome expectation; "I am good at this subject" is self-concept.
📝 Exam-style questionA student says: "I know that practising every day would make me pass, but I cannot make myself do those exercises properly." Which belief is high and which is low?

Model answer: The outcome expectation is high: the student believes the behaviour (daily practice) leads to the outcome (passing). Self-efficacy is low: the student does not believe in their own capacity to carry out that behaviour. The chapter treats the two as distinct.

Some words first. Expectancy-value theory in its modern form comes from Jacquelynne Eccles and colleagues (1983). A proximal predictor is the cause that sits closest to the outcome, the last link in the chain. To control for something means to compare like with like, for example students with the same earlier grades. An adolescent is a teenager. In the picture: two dials. The blue one is expectancy ("will I succeed?"), the gold one is task value ("is it worth it?"). The × between them is the message of the scene. The green bar on the right stands for what follows: choices and achievement. Under the dials, four chips wait for the four kinds of value. You first sort four of Leo's thoughts, then predict what each dial predicts, then meet the twist.

Modern expectancy-value theory assumes that two beliefs are the closest predictors of students' academic choices, achievement-related behaviour and, in the end, learning and achievement. An expectancy is the belief about how well you will do on an upcoming task. Task value is how much the task is worth to you. Both are themselves shaped by many psychological, social and cultural influences.

The two beliefs do different jobs. Expectancies most strongly predict performance, even when previous performance is controlled. Task values most strongly predict choices, such as which activities and courses students pick, sometimes years later. And research with adolescents suggests they work together: the effect of one is stronger when the other is high, and neither can compensate when the other is low (Nagengast et al., 2011; Trautwein et al., 2012).

Four kinds of value. Utility value: the task is useful for other parts of your life. Attainment value: doing well is personally important or relevant to who you are. Intrinsic value: the task is interesting, enjoyable or fun. Cost: the negative aspects of engaging in the task. Children can tell competence beliefs and value beliefs apart in early elementary school, and can differentiate among types of value by fifth grade. A note on wording: the chapter says simply "value" or "task value" when studies looked at several components together or at the construct as a whole. Expectancies are conceptually distinct from beliefs about ability (in theory they are different ideas), but not empirically (in the data they cannot be separated).

What develops, and for whom. Expectancies and values predict persistence, performance and choice of activities in children as young as first grade, and the relations grow stronger with age. At the same time the beliefs themselves decline with age, particularly for language arts in early elementary school and for maths in high school. Girls report higher expectancies and values for language arts; boys for maths, sports, science and engineering. And cost, long overlooked, turns out to matter: it negatively predicted undergraduates' intentions to enter graduate school, and effort cost and opportunity cost predicted intentions to leave science, technology, engineering and mathematics majors (Perez et al., 2014).

So "can I do it?" is half of the story, and the smaller half when it comes to choices. A confident student who sees no worth in the subject will not choose it. The other question, "do I want to?", gets two whole theories of its own. That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ Expectancy and value simply add up, so a very confident student can do without value.✓ Research with adolescents suggests they interact. The effect of expectancy is stronger when value is higher, and the other way round, and neither high expectancy nor high value can compensate when the other is low.
📝 Exam-style questionAccording to the chapter, which belief most strongly predicts students' enrollment choices, and what did Frome et al. (2006) find about women leaving male-dominated fields?

Model answer: Task value most strongly predicts activity choices and enrollment decisions, whereas expectancies most strongly predict performance. Frome et al. (2006): concerns about job flexibility and high time demands when balancing work and family (costs), along with lower intrinsic value of physical science, were the best predictors of women changing their occupational aspirations out of male-dominated fields. Two cautions: expectancies generally precede and predict values, and research with adolescents suggests the two interact, so neither can be ignored.

STEP4
Theories 3 and 4 · wanting, and whose reason it is

"Does this grab me?" and "Is the reason mine?"

❓ This step answers: What kinds of interest are there, how does interest develop, and what does it do for learning?
✨ Walk the path with Noah: four moments in his chemistry years, then two studies to predict.

Some words first. A domain is a subject area, such as chemistry or history. An affective state is a feeling state. Re-engage means to come back to something. An undergraduate is a bachelor student. Task involvement means being absorbed in a task. In the picture: a path with four stones. The two on the left are forms of interest that come from the situation; the two on the right live in the person. Noah has four moments: for each one, you decide which stone he is on, and the stone gets its name. Then two studies ask you to predict a result.

Interest research distinguishes two forms. Situational interest emerges from and is supported by the context: a striking demonstration, a good story, a well-chosen example. Individual interest is relatively stable and resides within the person: a deep personal connection to a domain and a willingness to come back to it over time, with positive feelings, value and personal importance.

Interest supports learning. Research on reading suggests that situational interest, or individual interest that has been activated, supports increased attention, cognitive processing and persistence. In a day-long problem-based learning session, situational interest predicted academic engagement and in turn achievement (Rotgans & Schmidt, 2011). In studies by Harackiewicz and colleagues it was positively related to task involvement and course grades; several years later it predicted course choice more strongly than achievement.

Two forms inside each form. Situational interest comes as triggered situational interest, a relatively short, heightened affective state started by something in the context, and maintained situational interest, where the situation supports more focused involvement, attention and persistence, including finding meaning and personal connections. Individual interest comes as emerging and well-developed, and Hidi and Renninger (2006) propose that knowledge is a key component: deeper stored knowledge acts as a catalyst for the shift from emerging to well-developed. A warning about names: triggered situational interest is also known as "catch" or as emerging situational interest, and maintained situational interest as "hold" or as stabilized situational interest (the chapter's notes). Do not confuse emerging situational interest with emerging individual interest. The chapter notes that there are varying views on interest, and that psychologists have studied it for more than a century, although it was relatively neglected until recent decades.

It is not always simple. The effects vary with perceived competence, initial interest, prior achievement and the type of situational interest. Durik and Harackiewicz (2007) found that triggered situational interest supported task involvement for undergraduates with low individual interest but undermined it for those with high interest, while maintained situational interest helped only those who started with high individual interest. Researchers have also documented shifts from situational to individual interest, and identified contextual supports: autonomy support, an approachable and friendly instructor, opportunities for involvement, and relevant course material.

So a teacher cannot hand a student an individual interest, but can light sparks and keep them alive, and that may in turn support individual interest. Notice how interest overlaps with task value from step 3 (intrinsic value was about a task being interesting, enjoyable or fun): the chapter itself says later that intrinsic motivation, task value and interest are very similar constructs. The next tab looks at wanting from another angle: not how strong it is, but whose it is.

⚠ The trap✗ Attention-grabbing lesson openers help every student stay involved.✓ In Durik and Harackiewicz (2007), triggered situational interest supported involvement for undergraduates with low individual interest, but undermined it for those with high interest.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the difference between triggered and maintained situational interest, and name what distinguishes emerging from well-developed individual interest.

Model answer: Triggered situational interest is a relatively short, heightened affective state initiated by contextual supports. Maintained situational interest is situational support of more focused involvement, attention and persistence, including finding meaning and personal connections to the content; students also develop deeper value and knowledge. Emerging and well-developed individual interest differ in knowledge: deeper levels of stored knowledge act as a catalyst for the shift to well-developed individual interest.

❓ This step answers: How does self-determination theory sort motivation by the reason for acting, and which needs feed it?
🧭 Whose reason is it? Five students do the same homework. Place each reason on the line, then predict three findings.

Some words first. Self-determination theory is the theory of Edward Deci and Richard Ryan (1985, 2000). The chapter calls it a macro theory: a broad theory of motivation and development, not about one narrow topic. Regulation here means what steers your behaviour. Autonomous means it feels like your own choice; controlled means it feels pushed, from outside or from inside. A longitudinal study follows the same people over time. In the picture: a line that runs from "experienced as controlled" on the left to "experienced as autonomous" on the right, with five empty places. Five students each give their reason for doing the same homework. You tap the place where each reason belongs, and the place shows its name. Then three findings to predict.

Self-determination theory distinguishes types of motivation by the reason for acting. The most basic distinction: intrinsic motivation is doing something for the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself; extrinsic motivation is doing something because it leads to a separable outcome, such as praise or money. But extrinsic motivation is not one thing: it varies in how far it has been internalized, and so in whether it is experienced as autonomous or controlled.

Extensive research relates intrinsic motivation and other autonomous forms of motivation to adaptive outcomes: creativity, academic engagement, deep conceptual learning strategies and achievement. More extrinsic forms predict, in many studies, maladaptive learning strategies and attitudes, anxiety, poorer coping, poor achievement and even dropout, though a few studies find extrinsic motivation beneficial at times. And both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation have been found to decline within and across school years.

The forms in between. Between fully extrinsic and intrinsic, motivation may come from feelings of obligation, guilt or pride (introjected), from seeing the behaviour as useful or important for personal goals (identified), or from the behaviour being fully internalized and representative of one's central values (integrated). Underneath sit three innate psychological needs: autonomy (feeling that your actions come from yourself), competence and relatedness. Supporting these needs enhances intrinsic motivation and internalization, and evidence shows their importance in both individualistic and collectivistic cultures.

What the classroom adds. Needs can be supported through teaching practices: meaningful choices, emphasizing personal relevance, non-controlling informational language, letting students express opinions and negative feelings, and providing feedback and structure. Directly controlling behaviour (suppressing perspectives, commands, surveillance) may harm motivation, engagement and learning. But the effects are complex. Choice may work best when it comes without pressure, in manageable amounts, for students who feel competent or interested or who hold an upper-class Western cultural outlook. Autonomy support and structure work together, despite the lay view that they clash. And relatedness, long neglected, is now linked with engagement, learning and achievement.

So this theory changes the question from "how much motivation?" to "what kind?". A student pushed by rewards or guilt and a student who has made the reason her own may work equally hard today, but the chapter's evidence says their learning, well-being and persistence tend to differ. Reading 2 is devoted to this theory. Next step: what students aim for, and how they explain what happens.

⚠ The trap✗ Extrinsic motivation is always controlled and always bad; only intrinsic motivation is autonomous.✓ Extrinsic motivation varies in how internalized it is. Identified and integrated motivation are extrinsic, yet experienced as more autonomous. In one study identified motivation was the form most strongly linked with academic achievement (Burton et al., 2006).
📝 Exam-style questionIn the longitudinal study by Corpus and colleagues (2009), how did intrinsic and extrinsic motivation each relate to classroom grades?

Model answer: Intrinsic motivation and classroom grades mutually influenced one another positively and reciprocally over the academic year. Extrinsic motivation was unrelated to grades, but poor academic performance predicted higher extrinsic motivation. Both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation declined within the school year and across grade levels (third to eighth grade).

STEP5
Theories 5 and 6 · purposes and causes

"What am I aiming for?" and "Why did that happen?"

❓ This step answers: Which achievement goals do students pursue, and how does each relate to learning and achievement?
🎯 Place the goal, then read the chart: four students fill the grid. Then the grid turns into the results of a meta-analysis that you predict bar by bar.

Some words first. Competence means being able to do something well. An achievement goal is the reason or purpose behind a student's effort on school tasks. Approach means moving toward something you want; avoidance means moving away from something you fear. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies into one average. A correlation is a number between −1 and +1 that shows how strongly two things go together; 0 means no link, and values near .10 are small. In the picture: first a grid with two columns (develop competence, or demonstrate it) and two rows (go for success, or dodge failure). Four students each state an aim; you tap the cell where it belongs. After that the picture becomes a bar chart: bars above the line mean "goes with higher achievement", bars below mean "goes with lower achievement".

Achievement goal theory proposes two primary purposes behind students' engagement in achievement activities. With a mastery goal the focus is on developing competence. With a performance goal the focus is on demonstrating competence. Later models crossed this with approach and avoidance, which gives four goals.

Research has established the benefits of mastery-approach goals and the detriments of performance-avoidance goals across educational outcomes. For performance-approach goals the findings remain mixed and controversial. And for achievement in particular, the chapter says a controversy remains about how goals relate to it, especially for mastery-approach and performance-approach goals. In the meta-analysis of Hulleman et al. (2010), the correlations with achievement were small: positive for mastery-approach (.11) and performance-approach (.06), negative for mastery-avoidance (−.12) and performance-avoidance (−.13).

How the model grew. First there were two goals: mastery and performance. The trichotomous model (Elliot, 1999) split performance goals into approach (appearing competent) and avoidance (avoiding appearing incompetent). The 2 × 2 model (Elliot & McGregor, 2001) extended the split to mastery: approaching the development of competence, or avoiding declining competence or not fulfilling one's potential. Mastery-avoidance goals have not been widely studied; so far they relate to negative affect, poor study strategies, avoidant behaviour and lower achievement, with some null or adaptive findings for learning strategies.

Why performance-approach is controversial. It depends on what you measure. With mostly normative items (outperforming others) the correlation with achievement was positive (.14); with items about appearance (looking smart) or evaluation (demonstrating ability) it was negative (−.14). Person-centred research adds that combinations matter: Luo et al. (2011) found a profile with at least moderate mastery, high performance-approach and low performance-avoidance goals most beneficial. And goals do not come only from inside: they are shaped by the goal structure of the classroom or school, and by personal factors such as motives and theories of intelligence.

So the same amount of effort can serve very different purposes, and the purpose colours how a student reacts to difficulty. A student who wants to grow can treat a mistake as information. A student who wants to avoid looking incompetent has every reason to hide it. What the student then tells herself about that mistake is the subject of the next tab.

⚠ The trap✗ Performance goals are bad for achievement, mastery goals are good.✓ It is not that simple. In Hulleman et al. (2010) performance-approach goals had a small positive average correlation with achievement (.06) and mastery-avoidance goals a negative one (−.12). What is established: mastery-approach goals are beneficial and performance-avoidance goals detrimental across outcomes. Performance-approach findings are mixed and depend on how the goal is measured.
📝 Exam-style questionTwo questionnaires both claim to measure performance-approach goals. One finds a positive link with achievement, the other a negative link. Explain how this can happen.

Model answer: Hulleman et al. (2010) found that the content of the items moderates the result. When items were primarily normative (outperforming others), the correlation with achievement was positive (.14). When most items focused on appearance (looking smart) and/or evaluation (demonstrating ability), it was negative (−.14). Framing performance goals as normative standards versus broader orientations about demonstrating competence yields different patterns.

❓ This step answers: How do students explain their successes and failures, and why do those explanations matter?
🔍 Take Emma's explanation apart: eight short steps. Each one switches on a part of the picture.

Some words first. To attribute means to assign a cause: "I failed because…". Attribution theory of achievement is Bernard Weiner's theory (1985) about those explanations. A dimension is one way in which causes can differ, like length or weight for objects. Aptitude is natural ability. Transient means passing, temporary. An intervention is a planned attempt to change something, here the way students explain results. In the picture: Emma with a failed test on the left. In the middle, three questions you can ask about any cause; each turns into the name of a dimension once you have used it. On the right, what each dimension leads to. The steps first build the three dimensions, then their consequences, then three findings.

Attribution theory assumes that people are motivated to understand the outcomes they experience, especially unexpected or negative ones. Looking for an explanation, a student may arrive at many possible causal attributions, such as ability, effort, luck or task difficulty. What matters for motivation is not the cause as such but where it falls on three dimensions: locus, stability and controllability.

Research is generally consistent with the theory: attributions are associated with different emotions, expectancies and academic functioning. Many intervention studies show that training students to see academic successes as controllable and academic failures as unstable has beneficial effects. One prediction does not hold up consistently, though: effort attributions are not always more beneficial than ability attributions.

The three dimensions and their effects. Locus: is the cause internal to the person (ability, effort) or external (luck, task difficulty)? A more internal locus relates to affective reactions such as pride or self-esteem. Stability: will the cause persist (aptitude) or is it transient (effort)? Stability relates most directly to expectancies for success and failure. Controllability: how much influence does the person have on the cause (effort is controllable, luck is not)? Controllability relates to hopefulness, to social emotions such as shame and guilt, and to help giving. The attributions themselves are influenced by many factors.

Connections to the other theories. Attributions now sit inside achievement goal theory and theories about intelligence. The link runs both ways: performance goals or a fixed view of intelligence go with more helpless attributions (internal, stable, uncontrollable causes after failure), mastery goals or a malleable view with more adaptive ones (controllable causes of success, unstable causes of failure). Perry et al. (2008) found the most adaptive motivation and goal striving, in the transition from high school to college, in students who combined changeable internal causes (low effort, bad strategy) with external causes that protect self-worth (test difficulty, poor teaching). And high topic interest shielded adolescents from the negative effects of excessive challenge (Fulmer & Frijters, 2011).

So after every result the student writes a small story about the cause, and that story sets the expectation, the feeling and the next move. This closes the tour of the six theories. You have probably noticed how often they borrowed from each other: goals shape attributions, interest protects against them, mindsets sit under both. Step 6 makes that overlap its subject.

⚠ The trap✗ Ability and effort are both internal causes, so they have the same effect on a student after failure.✓ They share a locus but differ on the other dimensions. In the chapter's examples aptitude is stable and effort is transient and controllable. A stable cause lowers the expectation of future success; a transient, controllable one leaves room for change.
📝 Exam-style questionA student fails and says: "The teacher made the test far too hard, and I also prepared with the wrong strategy." Using Perry et al. (2008), explain why this may be an adaptive explanation.

Model answer: It combines a modifiable, internal, controllable attribution (bad strategy) with an external, uncontrollable attribution that protects self-worth (test difficulty). Perry et al. (2008) found that students using this kind of combination for poor performance showed the most adaptive motivation and goal striving in the transition from high school to college, compared with students using other combinations.

STEP6
Beyond the six · integration · future directions

One student carries many motivations, and the field knows its gaps

❓ This step answers: Which important ideas fall outside the six theories?
🌱 Four cases beyond the six: predict an experiment, find the root, and place two theories in time.

Some words first. Malleable means changeable, able to grow; fixed means unchangeable. To prime someone is to expose them to something (here: words) that activates an idea without their noticing. An anagram is a puzzle of scrambled letters. An appraisal is a judgment about a situation. In the picture: on the left a small experiment with two groups; after you predict, bars show who did better. On the right a plant: the root is the belief about whether your abilities can grow, and after your prediction the leaves show what grows from it.

The authors end their tour by admitting what they left out. Many other theories contribute to understanding motivation, and many of them share and extend the ideas of the six. The one they single out is research on self-theories or mindsets: beliefs about whether personal attributes such as personality and intelligence are malleable or fixed. These beliefs may lie underneath several of the motivation constructs discussed before.

Research suggests that people with a more malleable theory of their attributes are more likely to adopt mastery goals, maintain intrinsic interest, persist in the face of challenge, show higher academic performance, make more adaptive attributions and have greater overall psychological well-being. And outside the reach of all six theories: Bargh et al. (2001) showed that people primed with an achievement goal, without knowing it, outperformed and persisted longer on an anagram task.

The wider family. Theories of personal control overlap with social cognitive theory, self-determination theory and attribution theory. Control-value theory (Pekrun, 2006) explains achievement emotions and performance from perceived control and value appraisals. Research on self-processes covers possible selves, self-concept, self-worth and beliefs about ability and intelligence. Flow theory studies the in-the-moment experience that resembles intrinsic motivation or interest; future time perspective research studies the value of education for the future. And unconscious motivation, rarely included in educational research, may according to the authors provide powerful explanations in educational contexts.

So the six theories are the main roads, not the whole map. The wider family repeats the same themes in new words: competence, control, value, goals. That repetition is a hint. If so many theories keep finding the same things, perhaps they can be combined. That is the next tab.

⚠ The trap✗ All motivation is conscious: students always know what drives their behaviour.✓ The six theories assume conscious, goal-directed action, but there is substantial evidence that much motivation is unconscious and that people are often unaware of what guides their moods, thoughts and behaviour.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors suggest that mindsets may "underlie" several other motivation constructs?

Model answer: Because a belief about the malleability of personal attributes is related to constructs from several theories at once. People with a more malleable (versus fixed) theory are more likely to adopt mastery goals (achievement goal theory), maintain intrinsic interest (interest, self-determination theory), make more adaptive attributions (attribution theory), persist in the face of challenge, perform better and have greater well-being.

❓ This step answers: How do researchers combine the theories, and why?
🧩 Five studies, three lenses: predict what each study found, and watch which lens it used.

Some words first. To integrate theories is to combine them. A variable is anything that is measured and differs between people, such as self-efficacy. A profile is a typical combination of scores within one person, for example "high mastery goals and high value". To mediate means to be the link in between: A changes B, and B changes C. Multiply determined means having many causes at once. In the picture: three cards, one for each way of combining theories. Five studies follow. For each you predict a finding, and the study is then pinned to the lens it used.

In the second half of the twentieth century researchers mostly worked inside one theory at a time. That built strong theories, but it hid something obvious: a real student has self-efficacy and values and goals and explanations, all at once. For more than a decade, researchers have therefore considered how multiple forms of motivation from multiple theories combine to shape engagement and learning. The authors describe three complementary approaches.

The variable-centred approach measures several kinds of motivation together and asks what each contributes. The person-centred approach looks for profiles: Conley (2012) found seven, and needed constructs from two theories to predict affect and achievement. The third approach starts from the educational context and asks how classrooms support several forms of motivation at once.

Variable-centred, in detail. Researchers explore the relative or unique contribution of each variable after controlling for other forms of motivation, and look at interactions among personal and contextual variables. Ciani et al. (2011) joined self-determination and goal theory: need satisfaction in life was linked with adopting mastery goals through autonomous motivation in class, and autonomy support slowed the decline in mastery goals over a semester. Hulleman et al. (2008) joined expectancy-value, interest and goal theory: intrinsic and utility value mediated the effects of mastery-approach goals on later interest and final grade. Others add emotion and cognition, as in Alexander's model of domain learning and Pekrun's work.

Person-centred and context, in detail. Instead of asking how one variable works across everyone and on its own, the person-centred approach identifies adaptive and maladaptive combinations. Shell and Husman (2008) found five groupings of variables along three dimensions. One dimension was a highly motivated, strategic learner; another was more intrinsically motivated and mastery-focused, tied to knowledge-building strategies but not to general cognitive and metacognitive strategies or high study effort. Context work includes Turner et al. (2011), who derived best practices from several theories; Guthrie and Wigfield's concept-oriented reading program; and Nolen (2007), who followed how several forms of reading and writing motivation developed in elementary classrooms.

So the question has shifted from "which theory is right?" to "how do the pieces work together in this student, in this classroom?". The authors single out the move beyond variable-centred analyses, toward how motivations combine within individuals, as a noteworthy advance. It also prepares reading 3, which is organized around what teachers can do for several kinds of motivation. One tab remains: what the field does not know yet.

⚠ The trap✗ A person-centred study measures how strongly one variable, such as self-efficacy, predicts achievement across all students.✓ That is the variable-centred approach. A person-centred study identifies profiles: combinations of several kinds of motivation within individuals, and compares how those profiles function.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat did Conley (2012) show about combining achievement goal theory and expectancy-value theory?

Model answer: She created seven motivational profiles using constructs from both theories and found that combining variables from multiple theories was critical for predicting affect and achievement: neither achievement goals nor subjective task value explained the pattern of findings alone. It is an example of the person-centred approach to integration.

A good overview is honest about its limits. The chapter closes with six directions for future research, and each one points to a weak spot: too many overlapping names, integration that risks becoming too complex, unknown mechanisms, too little attention to context and to culture, and too much reliance on questionnaires.

Directions 1 to 3. (1) Names. STEP1
Who wrote it, and why now

The founders of the theory, twenty years on

❓ This step answers: Who are the authors, what kind of article is this, and what is their starting point?
Authors
Richard M. Ryan (Australian Catholic University) and Edward L. Deci (University of Rochester), the founders of self-determination theory
Type
Review article in a special issue of Contemporary Educational Psychology (2020)
Method
No new data: a review of SDT research in education, with a critique of school policies
Main claim

Twenty years earlier, in the same journal, Ryan and Deci reviewed the research on intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, at that time still an emerging field. This article is the follow-up: what has been learned in two decades, mainly inside the framework of self-determination theory (SDT), which they describe as a broad theory of human development and wellness with strong implications for education.

Their starting point is a reversal. Behaviouristic approaches try to shape and control motivation from the outside. SDT puts its emphasis on people's inherent tendencies to learn and grow, and on how these can be supported. The authors say SDT has been part of a Copernican turn in the field. They do not explain the image; our reading is that, as Copernicus moved the centre from the earth to the sun, SDT moves the starting point from the environment to the learner.

SDT is an organismic theory. It assumes that people are by nature prone toward psychological growth and integration, and so toward learning, mastery and connection with others. But the authors add at once that these tendencies are not automatic: they require supportive conditions to be robust. That "but" is the whole research programme. If growth were automatic, schools would not matter; if it did not exist, only rewards could work.

What kind of text is this? A review by the theory's own founders. That has two sides for you as a reader. Nobody knows the evidence better, and the article is a compact tour of hundreds of studies. But it is also an argued case: the authors say openly that they oppose certain policies. Read it the way you would read a lawyer's closing statement: full of real evidence, arranged to convince. Next: the claim itself.

⚠ The trap✗ SDT says people are naturally motivated, so teachers do not have to do anything.✓ SDT says the tendencies to learn and grow are inherent but not automatic. They need supportive conditions, and that is exactly where teachers and schools come in.
📝 Exam-style questionHow does SDT's view of motivation differ from behaviouristic approaches?

Model answer: Behaviouristic approaches attempt to shape and control motivation from the outside. SDT places its emphasis on people's inherent motivational propensities for learning and growing, and on how these can be supported. STEP2
The question and the claim

Three needs, and a paradox about control

❓ This step answers: What does the article set out to show?

SDT argues that healthy development requires support for basic psychological needs, and three are seen as particularly fundamental. Autonomy: a sense of initiative and ownership in one's actions. Competence: the feeling of mastery, a sense that one can succeed and grow. Relatedness: a sense of belonging and connection. Thwarting any of the three is seen as damaging to motivation and wellness, so SDT judges educational settings mainly by one thing: do they meet or frustrate these needs?

The claim of the article has two halves. Need supports enhance intrinsic motivation and internalization, and that results in higher achievement. And, paradoxically, attempting to control achievement outcomes directly through extrinsic rewards, sanctions and evaluations generally backfires, leading to lower-quality motivation and performance. Pushing for results from outside generally backfires.

The article says what feeds each need. Autonomy is supported by experiences of interest and value, and undermined by experiences of being externally controlled, whether by rewards or punishments. Competence is best satisfied in well-structured environments that offer optimal challenges, positive feedback and opportunities for growth. Relatedness is facilitated when respect and caring are conveyed. Notice that structure appears under competence: SDT is not against structure, a point that returns in step 5.

The authors add a value judgment. More important than achievement, in their view, is students' psychological growth and wellness. Not all students can or will excel at the cognitive agenda of schools, but schools should still be supportive contexts for development and, importantly, do no harm. The article then follows this plan: definitions of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (step 3), the classroom evidence (steps 4 and 5), wellness and diversity (step 6), a critique of grades and testing (step 7), teachers' own needs (step 8), and the gap between this knowledge and policy.

⚠ The trap✗ In SDT, autonomy means independence: working alone and doing whatever you want.✓ Autonomy concerns a sense of initiative and ownership in one's actions. A student can follow a teacher's request autonomously, if she accepts its value. It is the opposite of feeling controlled, not the opposite of receiving guidance.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat is the paradox at the centre of Ryan and Deci's argument?

Model answer: Supporting students' basic psychological needs enhances intrinsic motivation and internalization, which results in higher achievement. Paradoxically, trying to control achievement outcomes directly, through extrinsic rewards, sanctions and evaluations, generally backfires and leads to lower-quality motivation and performance.

Key concepts · Figure 1 · the taxonomy of motivation

From no reason at all to pure enjoyment

❓ This step answers: Which kinds of motivation does SDT distinguish, and how are they ordered?

Some words first. A taxonomy is an ordered classification, like the one biologists use for animals. A regulatory style is the way a behaviour is steered: by a reward, by guilt, by a value, by enjoyment. Volition means willingness. Perceived locus of causality means where a person feels the cause of their behaviour lies: outside them or inside them. Ego-involvement means your self-esteem is at stake in the result. At the end you have rebuilt the article's only figure.

SDT started with intrinsic motivation: doing an activity for its own sake, for its inherent interest and enjoyment. Against it stands extrinsic motivation: behaviour done for reasons other than its inherent satisfactions. But the authors call extrinsic motivation a heterogeneous category, a mixed bag. So SDT specifies four subtypes, arranged by how autonomous they are, plus a sixth category for having no motivation at all.

The ordering is not just a drawing. Much research has verified that the categories relate to each other in the order the model predicts. And the more autonomous the motivation, the better the outcomes: greater relative autonomy for learning, or for teaching, predicts a variety of key educational outcomes. At the other end, amotivation has been a strong negative predictor of engagement, learning and wellness.

The four extrinsic styles. External regulation: driven by externally imposed rewards and punishments. Introjected regulation: partially internalized; you reward yourself with self-esteem and punish yourself with anxiety, shame or guilt. Identified regulation: you consciously endorse the value of the activity. Integrated regulation: the value also fits your other core interests and values. The first two are controlled, the last two autonomous. Amotivation, lacking intentionality, can come from a lack of felt competence or from a lack of value or interest.

Two refinements. First, autonomous extrinsic motivation is not the same as intrinsic motivation: both are highly volitional, but intrinsic motivation rests on interest and enjoyment, while identified and integrated motivation rest on a sense of value. Second, the columns are not boxes for people. Most intentional behaviour is multiply motivated, so researchers also work with a relative autonomy score, with summary scores for autonomous and controlled motives, and with person-centred profiles. Intrinsic motivation itself is taken very seriously: the authors think it is likely responsible for the preponderance of human learning across the life span.

So the useful question about a student is not "is she intrinsically motivated?" School cannot always be fun. The question is whether her reasons are autonomous or controlled, and a teacher can help a reason move to the right along the internalization arrow. How, and with what results, is the subject of the next two steps.

⚠ The trap✗ Introjected motivation comes from inside the person, so it is autonomous.✓ Introjection is "internally controlled": the person pressures themself with guilt, shame and outcome-dependent self-esteem. External regulation and introjection are both controlled forms. Autonomy is about willingness, not about where the pressure is located.

Model answer: Identified regulation (or integrated regulation, if being a researcher is congruent with her other core values). She personally endorses the value of the activity and acts with a high degree of volition, although the activity is not enjoyable. How they support it · the classroom research

Two hypotheses, tested from primary school to law school

❓ This step answers: What is the evidence that autonomous motivation and need support matter in education?
🔬 Predict five studies: each one tests a link in the chain. Decide first, then see what was found.

Some words first. A hypothesis is a prediction a theory makes that research can test. Controlling for earlier achievement means comparing students who started at the same level. GPA is a grade point average. A prospective or longitudinal study follows people forward in time. To mediate is to be the link in between. Cortisol is a hormone released under stress. In the picture: three boxes in a row. Hypothesis (b) is the first arrow: need support builds autonomous motivation. Hypothesis (a) is the second arrow: autonomous motivation improves outcomes. After each prediction, the study is pinned under the arrow it tests.

Among the core hypotheses of SDT in education are two. (a) More autonomous forms of motivation will enhance students' engagement, learning and wellness. (b) Basic psychological need support from both teachers and parents facilitates such motivation, whereas need thwarting undermines it.

According to the authors both have been well supported across hundreds of studies, at every level of development, and across varied learning contents and cultural contexts. A large literature shows positive relations of more autonomous classroom motivation with academic outcomes, and need supports have shown robust positive effects on school outcomes.

Evidence for (a). A meta-analysis pointed to a significant role of intrinsic motivation in school achievement (Taylor et al., 2014), and intrinsic motivation predicted engagement and through it achievement (Froiland & Worrell, 2016). In college, autonomous motivation predicted higher performance beyond prior achievement (Manganelli et al., 2019). Why? Probably in part because students put in more effort when autonomously motivated, and because internalized motivation becomes part of identity: need satisfaction went with identifying oneself as a scientist (Skinner et al., 2017). The worry: intrinsic motivation for school tends to decline over the school years.

Evidence for (b). Students of more autonomy-supportive teachers have more intrinsic motivation, perceived competence and self-esteem, better grades, greater internalization and lower dropout. Perceived autonomy support led to greater engagement through autonomous motivation in Spanish college students (Núñez & León, 2019). It applies in advanced education too: medical students' choice of residency area was predicted by their mentors' autonomy support, and law students with autonomy-supportive instructors declined less in well-being and did better on the bar exam (Sheldon & Krieger, 2007). And it shows in the body: controlling teaching raised cortisol, autonomy-supportive teaching lowered it (Reeve & Tseng, 2011). Looking ahead, the authors expect the neuropsychology of autonomy to matter more: brain research on intrinsic motivation already points to how choice and optimal challenge may switch on reward areas (the striatum) linked to enjoyment, and frontal areas needed for engaged learning (see Reeve & Lee, 2018).

So the chain holds at both links. That shifts the practical question from the student to the teacher: if need support is what builds good motivation, what exactly does a need-supportive teacher do? That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ Motivated students simply were the better students already; motivation adds nothing.✓ Several studies controlled for baseline or prior achievement (and in one case entrance test scores) and still found that intrinsic or autonomous motivation, or autonomy support, predicted higher performance.
📝 Exam-style questionState the two core hypotheses of SDT in education and give one study for each.

Model answer: (a) More autonomous forms of motivation enhance students' engagement, learning and wellness: for example, intrinsic motivation was consistently associated with higher performance controlling for baseline achievement (Taylor et al., 2014). (b) Basic need support from teachers and parents facilitates such motivation, and need thwarting undermines it: for example, STEM students with more autonomy-supportive lab instructors showed increases in autonomous motivation and perceived competence and received higher grades (Black & Deci, 2000).

STEP5
What it looks like in class · autonomy support and structure

Structure is not control, and support is not letting go

❓ This step answers: What do need-supportive teachers actually do, and what do controlling teachers do?
🧑‍🏫 Watch Ms Rossi: four moves to place in the right field, then four findings to predict.

Some words first. A rationale is a reason given for a request. Efficacy feedback is feedback that tells you how capable you are becoming. To scaffold is to give temporary support that makes a task doable, like scaffolding around a building. A directive is an order. Permissiveness means letting people do as they please. A diary method asks people to report after every lesson or day. In the picture: four fields. On the right, the two ingredients of need-supportive teaching; on the left, their opposites. Ms Rossi makes four moves: tap the field each belongs to. Then four findings follow.

The ingredients of a need-supportive classroom primarily concern two things the teacher provides: autonomy support and structure. Autonomy support is seen as promoting both autonomy and relatedness satisfactions, and when it occurs along with structure, competence as well. Their opposites are control and chaos.

Observation studies show what this looks like. Eight teacher behaviours were positively associated with students' autonomous motivation, among them listening, making time for independent work, encouraging effort and acknowledging students' perspectives; controlling behaviours such as issuing directives and telling students the answers were negatively related (Reeve & Jang, 2006). And the combination of high autonomy support and high structure has been associated with higher autonomous motivation, more self-regulated learning and lower anxiety.

Autonomy support, in detail. Autonomy-supportive teachers begin by trying to understand, acknowledge and where possible respond to students' perspectives. They give opportunities for ownership and initiative, meaningful choices, tasks that engage interests, and a meaningful rationale when something must be done. Choice needs care: it can be meaningless, or carry subtle pressure, and a student can feel autonomous without any options if she accepts the value of the task. Overall, a meta-analysis supports a positive effect of choice on intrinsic motivation (Patall et al., 2008), and taking students' interests into account works alongside it.

Structure, in detail. SDT strongly distinguishes control from structure. Control pressures students to behave or achieve. Structure means clear expectations and goals, consistency in rules and guidelines, informational supports and rich efficacy feedback. Structure can itself be delivered in a controlling or an autonomy-supportive way. Teachers' autonomy support and structure, as rated by observers, predicted students' engagement in US high schools (Jang et al., 2010), and the same pair in parents predicted children's felt competence and autonomy (Farkas & Grolnick, 2010). A Belgian study mapped teaching styles on a circumplex with eight sub-areas (Aelterman et al., 2019).

So need-supportive teaching is neither soft nor strict: it is clear and it listens. One reassuring detail: teachers who support autonomy typically support competence and relatedness too, because they are tuned in to their students. Up to here the article has been about achievement and engagement. The next step asks about something the authors find even more important: wellness, for every kind of student.

⚠ The trap✗ An autonomy-supportive teacher gives few rules and lets students decide everything.✓ Supporting autonomy is not about permissiveness. The most positive teaching style is high in both autonomy support and structure: clear expectations, consistent rules and feedback, delivered with attention to the students' perspective.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the difference between structure and control in SDT, with one example of each.

Model answer: Controlling behaviours pressure students to think, feel, behave or achieve in particular ways, without responsiveness to their perspective, for example issuing directives or using words such as "should" and "have to". Structure entails setting clear expectations and goals, consistency in rules and guidelines, and informational supports and efficacy feedback, for example explaining what a good report looks like and giving feedback on progress. Structure supports competence and can be delivered in an autonomy-supportive way.

The same needs for every student, everywhere?

❓ This step answers: Does need support also foster well-being, and does that hold across cultures and for diverse learners?

Some words first. Wellness or well-being means how well a person is doing psychologically. A collectivist culture puts the group first; an individualist culture puts the person first. Etic means holding across all cultures; emic means specific to one culture. Cultural competency is being able to work well with people from different backgrounds. An internal frame of reference is how the world looks from inside one person. In the picture: on the left, countries where the effects of autonomy and autonomy support have been tested; they light up after your first prediction. On the right, two cards: what is universal and what varies, and what autonomy support does for inclusion.

The authors argue that schools, though often narrowly focused on achievement, are more importantly contexts for the development of children, adolescents and young adults. Confidence, self-esteem and mental health are all deeply affected by whether school supports or thwarts basic psychological needs. And they claim this holds across age, ethnicity and culture.

Need satisfaction went with school satisfaction and well-being in Chinese adolescents; teacher autonomy support predicted engagement, which was associated with lower anxiety and depression; parent autonomy support helped Finnish students through school transitions. On culture, a meta-analysis found no differences between East Asian and North American samples in the importance of autonomy to well-being (Yu, Levesque-Bristol & Maeda, 2018).

The culture debate. The universality claim is, the authors admit, controversial, especially for autonomy. Critics (the article names Markus, Kitayama and Heiman, 1996) argued that autonomy is not important in traditionalist or collectivist cultures. The authors agree that cultures vary in values and practices, including in how they value autonomy, but argue that the functional importance of autonomy is universal. Their evidence: studies in the USA and Russia, Nigeria and India, Japan, South Korea and China. Their nuance: SDT makes etic claims about the needs and recognizes emic variation in their salience, meaning and dynamics. The same teacher behaviour can be experienced as more or less controlling in different cultures.

Diversity. The authors see an irony in the objection: by respecting autonomy, SDT acknowledges the differences between people. Autonomy support has as its central feature the attempt to appreciate and respect the learner's internal frame of reference, so it is a central element of cultural competency, and it extends to all the ways learners differ: socioeconomic background, temperament, interests, religious values, sexual identity, even neurological processing style. With such support, people who might otherwise feel alienated can feel safer. When teachers become more autonomy supportive, the culture of a whole school can become more accepting and tolerant (Assor et al., 2009). Japan is one example: studies showed that the internalization continuum predicts outcomes for Japanese elementary school students, and the more autonomously motivated pupils were more interested, studied more deeply and felt better about school (Hayamizu, 1997; Yamauchi & Tanaka, 1998). Promoting autonomy and intrinsic motivation also raised Japanese primary pupils' engagement in English lessons (Oga-Baldwin et al., 2017).

So need support is not a technique for raising grades with well-being as a bonus. For the authors it is the other way round: wellness is the main point, and it turns out to bring engagement with it. That makes the next question uncomfortable. If control harms motivation and wellness, what about the controlling tools that every school uses?

⚠ The trap✗ SDT claims that all cultures value autonomy equally and that needs are satisfied in the same way everywhere.✓ SDT claims that the functional importance of the needs is universal (etic). It accepts that cultures vary in how much they value autonomy and in the salience, meaning and dynamics of the needs (emic).
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do Ryan and Deci call autonomy support a central element of cultural competency?

Model answer: Because the central feature of autonomy support is attempting to appreciate and respect the internal frame of reference of the learner. People from diverse backgrounds and value systems have frames of reference that influence their motivations and valuations, so a teacher who starts from the learner's own perspective is by definition responsive to those differences. The same holds for all diversities among learners, not only cultural ones.

STEP7
A needs-based critique · grading, performance goals, high-stakes tests

When ordinary school tools work as pressure

❓ This step answers: What does SDT say about grades, performance goals and high-stakes testing, and on what evidence?
🎛️ Inform or control? Six cases. Start with two ways of giving feedback, then predict what three common school tools do.

Some words first. Structural factors are features of the system, not of one teacher: class size, mandated curricula, testing rules. Criterion based grading compares your work with a standard; comparative grading compares you with other students. Accountability means being held responsible for results. A standardized test is the same test for everyone. An incentive is a reward meant to change behaviour. In the picture: a bar at the top for the key idea, which fills in after the first case. Below it, three school tools. As you predict the studies, each tool shows what the article concludes about it.

Teacher style is not the whole story. Structural factors and policies also affect motivation, sometimes in unintended ways. The authors single out three: an excessive emphasis on grades, performance goals, and pressures from high-stakes tests. Their tool for judging all three is functional significance: is the event experienced as informational, or as controlling?

For grades: an experiment found grading associated with decreased intrinsic motivation and lower conceptual learning (Grolnick & Ryan, 1987), and a Swedish natural experiment linked primary school grading to lower later achievement and lower odds of finishing secondary education, especially for lower-ability students (Klapp, 2015). For high-stakes tests: improved scores do not typically generalize to other, more valid tests.

Grades and performance goals. Grades are typically comparative, so the priority placed on them often sets off performance goals, the desire to outperform others. Grades by themselves typically give little competence-relevant feedback, and used as motivators they will typically be experienced as controlling. In mastery goals the aim is enhancing one's own competence; in performance goals the focus is on outperforming others, and substantial evidence suggests that performance-avoidance goals are the most detrimental. The SDT reading: performance goals are commonly experienced as controlling pressures, mastery goals as informational. And the motives behind a goal predicted outcomes better than the goal itself (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010; Gillet et al., 2015).

High-stakes tests. Policymakers attached incentives and sanctions to standardized test scores, assuming that poor performance is due to poor teacher motivation and that rewards and sanctions will remedy it. The authors call the result remarkably ineffective: teaching narrowed to the test, poor performers kept away from tests, false reporting, and enriching subjects cut, with the quality of teaching compromised especially for disadvantaged students and students learning English as a second language. Their principle: outcome-focused rewards and sanctions reinforce any route to the goal, even bad practice. So they favour policies that support the best processes in classrooms. Why are grades so common, then? The authors fear it is partly because some educators and policy-makers assume that grading rewards effort and gives feedback. Yet grades by themselves typically give little feedback about competence: they mainly tell students where they stand relative to others (Butler, 1987, and the authors' own work). As the alternative to test-based accountability the authors point to school reforms that attend to the psychological needs of teachers and students (e.g., Early et al., 2016). They also note that self-determination theory, although it relies on empirical methods, shares with many constructivist and post-modern approaches a concern with what a culture internalizes and imposes, so it can be both empirically grounded and critical (Ryan & Niemiec, 2009).

So the critique is one argument applied three times: a tool that students or teachers experience as pressure toward an outcome undermines the autonomous motivation that produces the outcome. The step also shows why teachers cannot fix this alone. Much of the pressure does not start in the classroom. It arrives from above, which is where the article goes next.

⚠ The trap✗ Ryan and Deci want schools to stop giving students any feedback on how they perform.✓ They distinguish informational from controlling feedback. Efficacy-related information and authentic feedback about mastery enhance motivation. Their target is grades used as motivators, which mostly rank students, and they accept that grading may sometimes be needed for gatekeeping.
📝 Exam-style questionUsing the concept of functional significance, explain why SDT expects grades to undermine motivation while mastery feedback supports it.

Model answer: Functional significance is the meaning feedback has for the recipient. Feedback is informational when it is efficacy relevant, helping the person improve or highlighting competence; this tends to enhance intrinsic motivation and internalization. It is controlling when experienced as pressure toward specific behaviours or outcomes. Grades as commonly used give little competence-relevant information (they mainly show where a student stands relative to others) and are far too often experienced as controlling, so they diminish autonomous motivation. Authentic feedback about mastery is informational.

Thriving students require thriving teachers

❓ This step answers: What do teachers need in order to be need supportive, and can autonomy support be trained?

Some words first. A superintendent is the official in charge of the schools in a district, the principal's boss. Burnout is exhaustion from long-lasting work stress. An intervention is a planned attempt to change something, here a training for teachers. A control group does not get the training, so that there is something to compare with. Proximal means closest. An effect size says how big a difference is. In the picture: a staircase of four levels, from district policy down to the student. Autonomy-supportive teaching is not always easy, given limits of time, resources and curricula. And teachers, like their students, have basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness. SDT maintains that for teachers to actively support students' needs, they themselves must experience need support.

Pressure from above (accountability policies, controlling administrators) and from below (disengaged students, difficult parents) were both negatively associated with teachers' autonomous motivation and with their autonomy support of students (Pelletier et al., 2002). In the other direction: training works. Across nineteen studies in which educators were trained in autonomy-supportive methods, the effect size was large, 0.63 (Su & Reeve, 2011).

What pressure does to teachers. When teachers experienced work overload or disruptive students, their autonomous motivation and perceived competence were lower, leading in turn to more emotional exhaustion and less sense of accomplishment. Job pressures were associated with burnout through the frustration of teachers' own needs. Pressure to boost student performance predicted lower autonomous motivation, lower vitality and more exhaustion. The authors' summary: when teachers' autonomy is frustrated, from above or from below, they tend to be more controlling with students and less positively engaged. The same pattern holds for principals and their superintendents.

Interventions. SDT-based interventions have targeted teachers as the most proximal influences on students' engagement and learning. Trained teachers were observed to be more autonomy supportive, and their students more engaged (Reeve et al., 2004). Increases in teacher autonomy support were also associated with teachers' own increased feelings of efficacy and the adoption of more intrinsic goals (Cheon et al., 2018). Such studies matter for two reasons the authors name: they demonstrate the causal role of teacher need support, and they show the practical value of motivation research.

So the article's answer to "why don't teachers just do this?" is the same as its answer for students: because their own needs are under pressure. Effective reform is therefore not just about changing teachers' behaviour, but about supporting the needs of teachers and principals as well. The last step gathers the argument and the authors' conclusion.

⚠ The trap✗ Whether a teacher is controlling or autonomy supportive is a matter of personality, so it cannot be changed.✓ It depends heavily on the pressures and support the teacher experiences, and it can be trained: trained teachers were more autonomy supportive than control-group teachers (Reeve et al., 2004), and a meta-analysis of nineteen studies of trained educators found a large effect, 0.63 (Su & Reeve, 2011).
📝 Exam-style questionA government wants better teaching and plans to pay teachers according to their students' test scores. What would Ryan and Deci predict, and why?

Model answer: They would predict that it backfires. Pressure on teachers to boost student performance predicts lower autonomous motivation for teaching, lower vitality and more exhaustion, and teachers whose autonomy is frustrated by pressures from above tend to be more controlling with students, which undermines students' autonomous motivation. Outcome-focused rewards and sanctions also reinforce any route to the goal, including teaching to the test. They would favour supporting teachers' basic psychological needs and the best processes in classrooms.

We have walked through the whole article: who wrote it and why, the three needs and the paradox of control, the taxonomy of Figure 1, the evidence for the two hypotheses, what need-supportive teaching looks like, wellness across cultures and diversity, the critique of grades and testing, and the needs of teachers.

Self-determination theory says that people tend by nature to learn and grow, and that this tendency needs support for autonomy, competence and relatedness. Motivation comes in kinds, from controlled (rewards, punishments, guilt) to autonomous (value, identity, enjoyment), and the autonomous kinds go with better learning and well-being. Teachers build them by combining autonomy support with structure. Control tends to do the opposite, whether it comes from a teacher's words, from grades, or from tests that punish schools. And teachers need support in order to give it: pressured teachers tend to become more controlling.

Where the theory goes next. The authors list several directions: more varied methods at several levels of analysis, including the neuroscience of autonomy and new ways of measuring; learning and technology, such as games and e-learning that satisfy the three needs; a deeper learning theory that connects motivation to specific kinds of thinking and teaching; teacher and leadership motivation; more qualitative studies; globalization, diversity and wellness, studied with an approach that expects universal needs without uniform ways of meeting them; and SDT as a critical psychology that can compare teaching styles, school organizations and even national policies by how far they support or thwart needs. They report that need-supportive conditions foster other inner resources for learning as well: executive functioning, critical thinking and integrative decision-making. They describe how the theory was built step by step on converging evidence and tested against competing models. They also note that SDT now consists of six mini-theories, and they present it as a framework that can unify many of the constructs you met in reading 1. Unlike some psychologists, they defend self-report measures: in education, they argue, experience matters, so it should be measured well and checked against observational, biological and behavioural indicators.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why is "extrinsic motivation is bad for learning" too simple according to this article?

Because extrinsic motivation is a heterogeneous category with four subtypes. External regulation and introjection are controlled, but identified and integrated regulation are autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation: the person endorses the value of the activity and acts willingly. Autonomous motivation, including these two, predicts positive outcomes.

2. How can a teacher be high in structure without being controlling?

By setting clear expectations and goals, being consistent in rules, and giving informational support and efficacy feedback, while staying responsive to students' perspectives, giving a rationale and offering meaningful choice. Structure can be delivered in an autonomy-supportive way; control is pressure to think, feel or behave in particular ways.

3. What do the authors mean when they say SDT can be a "critical" psychology?

That it gives basic and measurable criteria (do curricula, teaching strategies, leadership styles and policies support or thwart the basic psychological needs of learners and teachers?) and so provides a basis for critical comparisons between teaching styles, school organizations and even national policies, instead of treating all practices as equally valid.

The good news: motivation and engagement are activated and enhanced when the classroom is designed to support them. ❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support students' perceived competence?

Some words first. Perceived competence is how capable a student feels, which is not the same as how capable the student is. Several theories stress competence beliefs: self-efficacy in social cognitive theory, expectancies in expectancy-value theory, academic self-concept, the need for competence in self-determination theory, and goal-setting theory. There are subtle differences between them, but the authors take an inclusive approach: all speak to the importance of students believing they can control their own achievement outcomes.

Across these frameworks the evidence is consistent: positive competence beliefs influence students' goals, behaviours, effort, persistence and emotions, their use of self-regulatory strategies and, ultimately, their achievement. Self-efficacy has been shown to predict engagement, school attendance, willingness to set difficult goals, intentions to persist in college and course grades. Students with higher perceived competence have, on average, an advantage across populations and contexts.

Structure, challenge and feedback. A classroom organized through clear, consistent expectations, rules, routines and procedures supports competence beliefs by influencing all four sources of self-efficacy. Tasks should be calibrated to existing skills with challenges that slightly exceed them, with scaffolding and step-by-step increases in difficulty. Feedback should be informational: what the student did successfully, how the strategy helped, and what to improve. Negative feedback is necessary but can reduce perceived competence; it is safer when it concerns the skill and not the person, is given warmly, and comes with tips and options. Wise feedback adds high expectations and confidence.

Models and strategies. Watching a role model complete a task helps students identify strategies to try, especially when the model is a peer or someone they identify with, and when the model shows struggle as part of learning. Self-regulation instruction teaches cognitive strategies (planning, rehearsal, elaboration, organization), metacognitive strategies (monitoring your own progress) and emotional ones (positive self-talk). Self-regulation and competence beliefs are positively and reciprocally associated. Praise should affirm strategy and effort, not traits such as intelligence, so that students attribute results to things they can master. Two more examples from the chapter. In online mathematics modules for Spanish college students, motivational scaffolds that made students aware of how they used their prior knowledge and how well their strategies worked predicted higher self-efficacy at the end of the course (Valencia-Vallejo et al., 2018). And when community college writing instructors added reflection assignments on goal-setting, choosing strategies, self-monitoring and self-evaluation, next to modeling and thinking aloud, they saw gains in motivation and achievement (MacArthur & Philippakos, 2013). One note on scope: some, though limited, research suggests that the link between perceived competence and educational outcomes may vary subtly across grade levels, genders, cultures and races.

So a teacher builds "I can do this" by arranging real, visible progress on work that is hard enough to count. Several cautions in this step concern minoritized students: low challenge, critical feedback without a frame of high expectations, and missing role models weigh most where low expectations are already in the air. Confidence is the first support. The second is about whose learning it is.

⚠ The trap✗ To build students' confidence, give them easy tasks and plenty of help so that they always succeed.✓ Insufficiently challenging or overly scaffolded tasks fail to provide mastery experiences, even when completed successfully, and students can read them as a sign that the teacher lacks confidence in them. Competence beliefs grow from appropriately challenging tasks with support.

Support 2 · autonomy and autonomous motivation · Table 2

"This is my own": take the student's perspective

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support students' autonomy, and when does choice fail?
🗝️ Six cases: when does choice help, when does it backfire, and what else supports autonomy? Predict each one.

Some words first. Autonomy is feeling that your actions come from yourself and fit your values and interests. Volition is willingness. Autonomy thwarting means actively blocking that feeling. Agency is acting to shape your own situation. A diary study asks people to report day after day. Random assignment means chance decides who gets which condition, which allows conclusions about cause. In the picture: the six practices of Table 2 as cards. Three cases are about choice, because choice is the practice with the most conditions attached.

Autonomy support is a personal interaction style: the teacher tries to take the student's perspective to guide the interaction, with an attitude of curiosity, openness and empathic understanding. Its goal is to enhance the student's experience of acting willingly and personally endorsing the behaviour. Any practice that lets students feel they learn because it reflects their own values, preferences, interests and goals counts as autonomy supportive.

Correlational and experimental research gives substantial evidence that students benefit when teachers are more autonomy supportive: more autonomous motivation, more behavioural and emotional engagement and self-regulation, more self-esteem, vitality and well-being, and higher achievement. The benefits seem to hold from preschool to graduate school and across ability levels, cultures and contexts, and may be particularly salient in adolescence.

Choice, with three principles. First, having options, choosing and experiencing autonomy are not the same; only the experience matters. Choices given in a controlling manner (steering toward one option, focusing on outcomes, attached to tangible rewards, or not letting students express their interests) have more limited benefits, and students must care about what they are choosing. Second, choosing can backfire when students lack the competence to choose well, and it has fewer benefits when there are too many or too similar options; in those cases choice needs guidelines or scaffolding. Third, culture and relationships change what choosing means: deferring a choice to a trusted other can itself feel autonomous.

Beyond choice. Perspective-taking: leaving room to hear and respond to students' questions, opinions, criticism and negative feelings. Incorporating perspectives: designing lessons around students' interests or letting them personalize activities. Respectful, informational language ("could", "consider") instead of controlling language ("must", "should"). Minimizing controlling practices, which can promote disengagement, negative feelings, extrinsic motivation or amotivation. And encouraging agentic engagement, where students themselves help shape the class. Rationales also support autonomy; the chapter discusses them under value.

So autonomy support is less a set of tricks than a stance: start from where the student is. Notice how this connects backwards and forwards: choice can backfire for students who lack the competence to choose well (step 3), and a student who chooses freely finds the content more valuable and interesting, which is the next support.

⚠ The trap✗ The more choices a teacher gives, the more autonomy and motivation students have.✓ Choice opportunities, choosing and experiencing autonomy are not synonymous. Too many options, options that are hard to tell apart, choices given in a controlling way, or choices beyond the student's competence have fewer benefits and can backfire.
📝 Exam-style questionGive three conditions under which providing choice is less effective or backfires, according to the chapter.

Model answer: Any three of: the choice is given in a controlling manner (leading students to one option, focusing on outcomes, accompanied by tangible rewards, or not allowing students to express interests); students do not care about the task; students do not know what makes a good choice or lack competence on the task; there are excessive options or options that are hard to discriminate; the student feels indecisive. In a cultural context with a group orientation, deferring the choice to a trusted other may be equally or more motivating.

STEP5
Support 3 · value and interest · Table 3

"This matters, this grabs me": give learning a point and a pull

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices raise the value and interest students find in learning?
✨ Six cases: which reasons work, for whom, and what makes content come alive? Predict each one.

Some words first. Value answers why a person chooses to engage in a task. A rationale is an explanation of why something is useful or important. Prosocial means aimed at helping others. Self-generated means produced by the student. First-generation students are the first in their family to attend college. An asset-based view starts from what students bring, not from what they lack. Cognitive load is the mental effort a task demands. In the picture: the five practices of Table 3. Three cases are about rationales, because the chapter spends most time on when they work.

Four frameworks explain why value and interest matter. Expectancy-value theory: an increase in attainment, intrinsic or utility value (not cost) promotes persistence, performance and future choices. Self-determination theory: students function better when learning behaviours are internalized and endorsed as their own. Interest theories: situational interest is the kind most responsive to the learning environment. And culturally relevant/responsive education, which the next card explains.

Situational interest has been shown to positively influence cognitive and behavioural engagement, memory, self-regulation, use of learning strategies, persistence, achievement, and course and major selection, and repeated arousal of situational interest has a positive effect on the development of individual interest. That is the process reading 1 called poorly understood; this chapter adds at least one finding to it.

Rationales and purpose. At the core of value is understanding why something is useful or important. A rationale gives that reason; it works when it builds on existing knowledge, takes the student's perspective and is specific and concrete, and when it appeals to prosocial or intrinsic goals. Because value is personal, teachers can also let students generate their own: writing how the material is relevant to their lives, class discussions of the value of activities, or lists of reasons made by peers. Which route works depends on the student's initial interest, competence and background.

Four more practices. Contextualized teaching and learning embeds content in meaningful, real-world or exciting situations. Culturally relevant/responsive education uses students' cultural characteristics, experiences and perspectives as mediums for teaching, bridges home and school, and can empower students to examine problems in their own community and act on them. Active learning makes students think about what they are doing. Digital technology can trigger interest because it is novel, and works best combined with the other practices, in collaborative or game contexts, and with designs that keep cognitive load low. For reference, expectancy-value theory names four components of task value: attainment value (the importance of the task for the self), intrinsic value (importance because the task is interesting or enjoyable), utility value (usefulness for other parts of one's life) and cost (the perceived negative aspects of engaging, such as time or anxiety).

So value is not something a teacher can hand over; it has to connect to what the student already cares about. That is why the same advice ("explain why it matters") succeeds with one student and fails with another. The next support deals with what happens when the work gets hard and the first failures arrive.

⚠ The trap✗ Any explanation of why a task matters will raise students' motivation.✓ Not all rationales are effective. Rationales that appeal to extrinsic goals (money, grades), social norms or pressure, or that take only the teacher's perspective, can undermine motivation. And which route fits depends on the student: told rationales tend to be most effective for students with high interest and competence, while self-generated value activities are particularly effective for students with low interest and competence.
📝 Exam-style questionA teacher has one group with high interest and competence in physics and one group with low interest and competence. Which approach to value does the chapter suggest for each?

Model answer: For the high-interest, high-competence group: directly communicated rationales tend to be most effective. For the low-interest, low-competence group (and among first-generation students of colour): self-generated value activities, for example writing how the material is relevant to their own lives, are particularly effective. A focus on relevance for daily life and helping-oriented goals may extend the benefits to students at various interest and ability levels.

STEP6
Support 4 · growth mindset, attributions, mastery goals · Table 4

"I can grow, and I am here to learn": make the class about progress

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support a growth mindset, adaptive attributions and mastery goals, and how strong is the evidence?
🌱 Six cases: mindset lessons, praise, explanations for failure, and how work is graded. Predict each one, and watch the hedges.

Some words first. A mindset is an implicit theory about whether human characteristics such as intelligence can change. Entity or fixed beliefs say ability is innate and unchangeable; incremental or growth beliefs say it can develop. An attribution is the cause you give for a result; causes differ in locus (inside you, like ability or effort, or outside, like luck or task difficulty), stability (lasting, like aptitude, or passing, like effort) and controllability (effort you can control, luck you cannot). Brain plasticity is the brain's ability to change with use. A moderator is something that changes how strong an effect is. To replicate a study is to repeat it and find the same result. Grading on a curve makes your grade depend on how others did. In the picture: the four practices of Table 4.

Three theories share one idea: students benefit when the emphasis is on developing competence. Attribution theory (how students explain success and failure), mindsets (what they believe about intelligence) and achievement goal theory (whether they aim to develop or to demonstrate competence). The authors treat them in one section because the three are closely related and intersecting. The common element: through hard work, persistence and good strategies, people can develop, learn and grow.

Students with incremental beliefs are more likely to persist after failure, feel more positive emotions and less anxiety, and achieve more; those who see intelligence as fixed take failure as evidence of low intelligence and fall into helpless patterns. But when it comes to interventions, the chapter is careful: research on mindset training programmes is mixed, with a small average effect (Sisk et al., 2018), and the classic praise findings have not been fully replicated.

Training beliefs and explanations. Mindset training gives lessons on brain plasticity; the briefest recent programmes present evidence that challenging work builds ability, use quotations from peers, and end with "saying-is-believing" exercises in which students advise a struggling peer. Effects may depend on the school context: the overall achievement level, peer norms about seeking challenge, and what teachers say about effort. Attributional retraining teaches students to see successes as controllable and failures as unstable, typically with videos of older students and a consolidation exercise. Teachers can also simply encourage students to identify effort and strategy as causes of past performance.

Praise and the goal structure. Praise shapes mindsets: process praise for effort, strategies and perseverance, not person praise for being smart. And the classroom itself sends a message. A mastery goal structure has varied, meaningful, appropriately challenging tasks; evaluation that focuses on improvement and absolute standards and keeps comparison out; autonomy support; and a positive socio-emotional climate with caring, high expectations and collaboration. Grades alone were associated with performance goals; comments went with higher mastery goals (Koenka et al., 2021). An example from survey research: undergraduates who perceived a class as focused on grades and performance were more likely to endorse performance goals (Church et al., 2001).

So telling students "you can grow" is the weakest version of this support. The strong version is a classroom in which everything, from praise to grading, says the same thing. Table 4's last row makes the point: a mastery goal structure is built out of the practices from all the other tables. One support remains, and it concerns the people in the room.

⚠ The trap✗ A short growth-mindset lesson reliably raises the achievement of all students.✓ The evidence is mixed. A meta-analysis found a small positive effect, with analyses suggesting benefits for achievement only for academically high-risk and economically disadvantaged students, and the effects may depend on whether the wider school context reinforces the message.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors discuss attributions, mindsets and achievement goals in one section? Describe the two patterns that tend to occur together.

Model answer: Because the three constructs are closely related and intersecting. Performance goals, entity (fixed) beliefs and more helpless attribution patterns after failure (internal, stable, uncontrollable causes such as lack of ability) co-occur. Mastery goals, incremental (growth) beliefs and more adaptive attribution patterns (controllable causes of success, unstable causes of failure) co-occur. The relations are bidirectional.

STEP7
Support 5 · relatedness and belonging · Table 5

"I belong here": connection as a condition for learning

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support relatedness and belonging, and for whom do they matter most?
🤝 Six cases: a myth to check, and four ways to build belonging. Predict each one.

Some words first. Belonging or relatedness is the extent to which a person feels connected to others. Minoritized students belong to groups treated as lesser by society. Microaggressions are small, everyday slights that target someone's group. Retention means staying in a school or programme. A testimonial is a personal story told to others. Positive interdependence means group members need one another to succeed. In the picture: the three practices of Table 5, plus culturally relevant education, which the chapter brings back here.

Belonging is emphasized in several theories: attachment theory, self-determination theory (relatedness as a basic need) and the belongingness hypothesis. Students naturally seek relationships with peers and teachers, and will try to conform to the achievement values of those they feel connected to. That is the mechanism: students internalize the values of teachers they feel close to.

From elementary school to college, relatedness to teachers and peers is positively associated with intrinsic motivation, grades, self-efficacy and behavioural and emotional engagement. Perceived teacher support is positively related to belonging from preschool to postsecondary levels. And belongingness is often more strongly connected to retention, persistence and grade increases for minoritized students than for white students.

Why the focus on minoritized students. Belonging matters for all students, but experiences of the educational system shape what it means to belong. Through experiences with racism, interpersonal and systemic, minoritized students are more prone to lacking feelings of belonging in academic settings. The idea that they do not value school has been consistently dispelled. So the chapter concentrates on practices that support belonging particularly for Black, Latinx, Native and other students of colour: cultivating community and meaningful relationships, and making sure students see themselves as accepted and represented, also in the curriculum.

The practices. Teacher-student connections: caring, friendliness and responsiveness, with psychological safety as the key; teachers can also encourage ties among students. Collaboration and group work: group projects, think-pair-share, peer teaching, designed with common goals, small groups, positive interdependence and individual accountability. Messages that communicate belonging: students are valued members of the community, and feelings of being out of place are common and temporary. And culturally relevant education, which helps students feel they belong in a classroom that learns about and honours their cultures, backgrounds and identities. The chapter calls a classroom where students get chances to connect with each other an "interpersonal opportunity structure" (Gray et al., 2018). Relationships with teachers that range from formal mentoring (Larose et al., 2005) to informal, mutual expressions of respect (Anderman, 2003) go with greater belonging and positive academic outcomes, and the key is psychological safety in the classroom (Anderman, 2003).

So belonging closes the list of five, and, in our reading, it loops back to the first: a student who feels safe and accepted is more likely to try hard things. The final step is about how to use the five together, and where the chapter draws its own limits.

⚠ The trap✗ Students from minoritized groups feel less belonging at school because they value school less.✓ Research has consistently dispelled the notion that minoritized students do not value school. Students of colour may want to do well and still feel disconnected from school as an institution because of its unsupportive environment and experiences with racism.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe a belongingness intervention from the chapter and explain what message makes it work.

Model answer: In one type, first-year students are shown what are presented as belonging survey results from older students that indicate consistent improvement in belonging over time, with no differences between genders or races and ethnicities, accompanied by written material. The message: a lack of belonging is not a function of one's social identity, and concerns about belonging are common and temporary. Variants add reading testimonials, affirming one's values, keeping a diary or recording one's own video testimonial. All affirm students as valued members of the school community.

STEP8
The conclusion · three themes

Combine, tailor, and build bridges

❓ This step answers: How should teachers use the five supports together, and how strong is the evidence?
🎯 Six cases from the conclusion: the same practice, different students. Decide what the chapter would say.

Some words first. Universality means holding for everyone. Precision here means fitting the practice to the student. A randomized experiment assigns people to conditions by chance and gives the strongest evidence for cause. Causal evidence is evidence that one thing produces another, not only that they go together. To co-design is to design together. In the picture: three cards for the three closing themes, and a bar about the strength of the evidence. They fill in as you decide the cases.

The authors end with three themes. First, overlap: practices and kinds of motivation do not sit in isolated categories. Second, universality versus precision: there is some degree of universality in the benefits of most practices, yet they need to be tailored. Third, partnership: researchers must work with educators and policy-makers so that motivating instruction becomes routine.

On the evidence, the authors are explicit. In most cases they chose practices whose support is relatively well established and includes causally rigorous designs such as randomized experiments. Some are promising with more limited causal evidence, or still need research on how to use them in class: encouraging agency, using digital technology, practices to support relatedness, and culturally relevant education. Their overall judgment: there is broad enough support to warrant using the recommended practices.

Overlap, in detail. A single practice may tap several aspects of motivation, and a single aspect may respond to a variety of practices. The advantage: many pathways to adaptive motivation. The other side: one practice is seldom enough for a class or even for one student, and many practices are more effective in combination. Structure goes best with autonomy support and an emotionally supportive climate; collaboration with structures that guide it; technology when it carries other practices.

Precision, in detail. The same practice can be interpreted differently depending on the student or the context. Practices are most effective when selected and tailored to the specific motivational challenge a student is experiencing in a particular environment. Confident students may relish choice and rationales; less confident students may find them demoralizing. Belonging and growth messages can be critical for students whose belonging and competence are continuously questioned, and go unnoticed or feel patronizing for others. If those other students struggle to find value in the content, a teacher would use very different strategies.

So the chapter does not hand teachers a checklist. It hands them a way of thinking: find out which support this student is missing, choose practices for it, and combine them with the others. That is also the answer of the whole theme to its opening question: a student starts and keeps going when someone has taken care of the right supports, for that student.

⚠ The trap✗ Because the practices are evidence-based, a teacher can apply the same ones to every student in the same way.✓ There is some universality, but precision matters: the same practice can be interpreted differently depending on the student and context. Support often needs to be tailored to the motivational challenges students are experiencing, and several practices need to be coordinated.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat do the authors mean by universality versus the need for precision? Give one example from the chapter.

Model answer: Universality: practices such as perspective-taking, incorporating students' interests, clear structures and warm, supportive relationships are likely to have benefits regardless of the student or circumstance. Precision: to maximize benefits, practices must be selected and tailored to the specific motivational challenge of a student in a context. Example: confident students might relish opportunities to choose and benefit from a rationale, whereas less confident students may experience the same choices and value messages as demoralizing; or belonging messages that are critical for minoritized students may feel patronizing to students who never questioned their belonging.

Because insufficiently challenging or overly scaffolded tasks do not provide mastery experiences, even if completed successfully. Encouraging student agency, using digital technology, practices to support relatedness, and culturally relevant education.

Self-determination theory orders reasons by how much they are the student's own, from external regulation to intrinsic motivation, so it looks at the quality of motivation and not only the amount.
🎯 "What am I aiming for, and why did that happen?"
goals and explanations		Achievement goals · attributions · mindsets (a belief that grew out of attribution theory)They tend to occur in two patterns (reading 3): performance goals with a fixed view of intelligence and helpless attributions; mastery goals with a growth view and attributions to controllable causes for success and unstable causes for failure.Goals look forward: develop competence (mastery) or demonstrate it (performance), going toward success (approach) or away from failure (avoidance). Attributions look back: the cause a student gives for a result, placed on three dimensions (locus, stability, controllability).
Readings 1 and 3 do not crown one theory (reading 2, written by the founders of self-determination theory, does argue that it can unify the others). Reading 1 ends on how several motivations work together inside one student; reading 3 ends on combining practices and tailoring them. (in our words)

3 · How do you use this on a real case?

The case clinic: diagnose first, then choose the practice

🎯 Six students, two decisions each. First decide what is going on (the diagnosis), then what a teacher could try (the treatment). The students are our invention; the practices and conditions come from the readings.

How to answer a case question. Read the student's own words and ask three things in order. What is the symptom? (the sentence the student says). What is the diagnosis? (which question in the student's head is going wrong: can I, do I want to, what am I aiming for, why did it happen, is it my own, do I belong). What is the treatment? (the practice that supports that part, with its condition).

A real student often shows more than one of these at once. Reading 3's advice is to combine practices and to fit them to the challenge this student actually has.
