Theme 2 · Put it together

🧩 Compare the theories, then use them on a case

The three readings each teach one piece. This page puts the pieces next to each other, because the theme asks four things of you: what each of the six theories says, how they resemble and differ, which teaching practices follow from each, and how to use those practices on a real case.

1 · How do the six theories resemble each other, and where do they differ?

Three families of questions in a student's head

The grouping into three families is our way of ordering the theories. The overlaps themselves come from the readings: reading 1 points out the similarity among the competence constructs and among the value constructs, and reading 3 describes how goals, mindsets and attributions occur together.

FamilyWhich ideas belong hereHow they resemble each otherHow they differ
💪 "Can I do this?"
competence beliefs		Self-efficacy (social cognitive theory) · expectancy for success (expectancy-value theory) · the need for competence (self-determination theory) · academic self-conceptAll are about the student's belief in being able to succeed. Reading 3 treats them together: there are subtle differences, but all speak to students believing they can control their own achievement outcomes.Self-efficacy is the belief that you can carry out a behaviour, and is kept apart from the outcome expectation (the belief that the behaviour leads to the result). Expectancy is a prediction about how you will do on an upcoming task, and it works together with value. In self-determination theory, competence is a need, next to autonomy and relatedness.
✨ "Do I want to?"
value and reasons		Task value (expectancy-value theory) · interest · intrinsic motivation and internalization (self-determination theory)All are about why the task is worth doing. Reading 1 names intrinsic motivation, task value and interest as similar constructs.Task value has four components: attainment, intrinsic and utility value, and cost. Interest is described by where it lives and how it develops: from triggered and maintained situational interest to emerging and well-developed individual interest. Self-determination theory orders reasons by how much they are the student's own, from external regulation to intrinsic motivation, so it looks at the quality of motivation and not only the amount.
🎯 "What am I aiming for, and why did that happen?"
goals and explanations		Achievement goals · attributions · mindsets (a belief that grew out of attribution theory)They tend to occur in two patterns (reading 3): performance goals with a fixed view of intelligence and helpless attributions; mastery goals with a growth view and attributions to controllable causes for success and unstable causes for failure.Goals look forward: develop competence (mastery) or demonstrate it (performance), going toward success (approach) or away from failure (avoidance). Attributions look back: the cause a student gives for a result, placed on three dimensions (locus, stability, controllability).
Readings 1 and 3 do not crown one theory (reading 2, written by the founders of self-determination theory, does argue that it can unify the others). Reading 1 ends on how several motivations work together inside one student; reading 3 ends on combining practices and tailoring them. (in our words)
2 · Which teaching practices follow from each theory?

From theory to practice, with the condition that makes it work

Reading 3 is organized by five supports, not by theory. The link back to each of the six theories in this table is our mapping, built from the places where the chapter names the theory. The practices, findings and cautions are the chapter's.

TheoryWhat the student needsPractices that followThe condition to rememberWhere it is taught
💪 Social cognitive theoryTo feel "I can do this", fed by the four sources of self-efficacyAppropriate challenge with scaffolding (mastery experiences) · a model the student can identify with (vicarious experience) · informational feedback, praise and expressed confidence (verbal persuasion) · self-regulation instructionToo little challenge or too much help gives no mastery experience and can undermine confidence. Critical feedback works when it is "wise": high expectations plus confidence that the student can meet them.Reading 1, step 3 · Reading 3, step 3
⚖️ Expectancy-value theoryTo expect success and to see the task as worth itThe competence practices above for expectancy · rationales and emphasis on purpose · letting students write about the value for their own lives · contextualized teachingA rationale must take the student's perspective, and works best when it is concrete and appeals to intrinsic or prosocial goals. Whether told or self-generated reasons work better depends on the student; self-generated value is particularly effective for students low in interest and competence.Reading 1, step 3 · Reading 3, step 5
✨ InterestSomething that catches attention, and then a reason to stayActive learning · contextualized and culturally relevant teaching · digital technology · incorporating students' preferencesTechnology can trigger interest because it is novel, but may not maintain it without other practices. In one study (reading 1), triggered situational interest helped students with low individual interest and undermined those with high interest.Reading 1, step 4 · Reading 3, step 5
🧭 Self-determination theoryAutonomy, competence and relatedness, so that the reason for acting becomes the student's ownAutonomy support: choice, perspective-taking, respectful language, minimizing control, rationales · clear structure · teacher-student connection and communityChoice is not the same as autonomy: it can overwhelm or feel controlling. Structure is not control, and works best together with autonomy support.Reading 2, step 5 · Reading 3, step 4
🎯 Achievement goal theoryA classroom where getting better is what countsA mastery goal structure: meaningful tasks, recognition of improvement, feedback on progress toward absolute standards (for example rubrics), private evaluation, collaboration, comments in addition to gradesCurves, rankings and an emphasis on relative ability foster performance goals. In a meta-analysis, grades went with performance goals and comments with higher mastery goals (Koenka et al., 2021).Reading 1, step 5 · Reading 3, step 6

The praise findings have not been fully replicated. In reading 1, effort attributions were not consistently better than ability attributions.Reading 1, step 5 · Reading 3, step 6

The fifth support of reading 3, relatedness and belonging, connects most directly to self-determination theory's need for relatedness: knowing students, building community, and messages that doubts about belonging are common and temporary.

3 · How do you use this on a real case?

The case clinic: diagnose first, then choose the practice

🎯 Six students, two decisions each. First decide what is going on (the diagnosis), then what a teacher could try (the treatment). The students are our invention; the practices and conditions come from the readings.

How to answer a case question. Read the student's own words and ask three things in order. What is the symptom? (the sentence the student says). What is the diagnosis? (which question in the student's head is going wrong: can I, do I want to, what am I aiming for, why did it happen, is it my own, do I belong). What is the treatment? (the practice that supports that part, with its condition).

A real student often shows more than one of these at once. Reading 3's advice is to combine practices and to fit them to the challenge this student actually has.
