Reading 2 of 3 · Article (review) · Zoom in on one theory

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation from a self-determination theory perspective

Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2020). Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation from a self-determination theory perspective: Definitions, theory, practices, and future directions. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 61, 101860.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and why now

The founders of the theory, twenty years on

❓ This step answers: Who are the authors, what kind of article is this, and what is their starting point?
Authors
Richard M. Ryan (Australian Catholic University) and Edward L. Deci (University of Rochester), the founders of self-determination theory
Type
Review article in a special issue of Contemporary Educational Psychology (2020)
Method
No new data: a review of SDT research in education, with a critique of school policies
Main claim
Supporting students' and teachers' basic psychological needs fosters high-quality motivation, learning and wellness; controlling them backfires

Twenty years earlier, in the same journal, Ryan and Deci reviewed the research on intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, at that time still an emerging field. This article is the follow-up: what has been learned in two decades, mainly inside the framework of self-determination theory (SDT), which they describe as a broad theory of human development and wellness with strong implications for education.

Their starting point is a reversal. Behaviouristic approaches try to shape and control motivation from the outside. SDT puts its emphasis on people's inherent tendencies to learn and grow, and on how these can be supported. The authors say SDT has been part of a Copernican turn in the field. They do not explain the image; our reading is that, as Copernicus moved the centre from the earth to the sun, SDT moves the starting point from the environment to the learner.

SDT is an organismic theory. It assumes that people are by nature prone toward psychological growth and integration, and so toward learning, mastery and connection with others. But the authors add at once that these tendencies are not automatic: they require supportive conditions to be robust. That "but" is the whole research programme. If growth were automatic, schools would not matter; if it did not exist, only rewards could work.

What kind of text is this? A review by the theory's own founders. That has two sides for you as a reader. Nobody knows the evidence better, and the article is a compact tour of hundreds of studies. But it is also an argued case: the authors say openly that they oppose certain policies. Read it the way you would read a lawyer's closing statement: full of real evidence, arranged to convince. Next: the claim itself.

⚠ The trap✗ SDT says people are naturally motivated, so teachers do not have to do anything.✓ SDT says the tendencies to learn and grow are inherent but not automatic. They need supportive conditions, and that is exactly where teachers and schools come in.
📝 Exam-style questionHow does SDT's view of motivation differ from behaviouristic approaches?

Model answer: Behaviouristic approaches attempt to shape and control motivation from the outside. SDT places its emphasis on people's inherent motivational propensities for learning and growing, and on how these can be supported. As an organismic theory it assumes people are inherently prone toward growth, learning, mastery and connection, but that these tendencies require supportive conditions.

STEP2
The question and the claim

Three needs, and a paradox about control

❓ This step answers: What does the article set out to show?

SDT argues that healthy development requires support for basic psychological needs, and three are seen as particularly fundamental. Autonomy: a sense of initiative and ownership in one's actions. Competence: the feeling of mastery, a sense that one can succeed and grow. Relatedness: a sense of belonging and connection. Thwarting any of the three is seen as damaging to motivation and wellness, so SDT judges educational settings mainly by one thing: do they meet or frustrate these needs?

The claim of the article has two halves. Need supports enhance intrinsic motivation and internalization, and that results in higher achievement. And, paradoxically, attempting to control achievement outcomes directly through extrinsic rewards, sanctions and evaluations generally backfires, leading to lower-quality motivation and performance. Pushing for results from outside generally backfires.

The article says what feeds each need. Autonomy is supported by experiences of interest and value, and undermined by experiences of being externally controlled, whether by rewards or punishments. Competence is best satisfied in well-structured environments that offer optimal challenges, positive feedback and opportunities for growth. Relatedness is facilitated when respect and caring are conveyed. Notice that structure appears under competence: SDT is not against structure, a point that returns in step 5.

The authors add a value judgment. More important than achievement, in their view, is students' psychological growth and wellness. Not all students can or will excel at the cognitive agenda of schools, but schools should still be supportive contexts for development and, importantly, do no harm. The article then follows this plan: definitions of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (step 3), the classroom evidence (steps 4 and 5), wellness and diversity (step 6), a critique of grades and testing (step 7), teachers' own needs (step 8), and the gap between this knowledge and policy.

⚠ The trap✗ In SDT, autonomy means independence: working alone and doing whatever you want.✓ Autonomy concerns a sense of initiative and ownership in one's actions. A student can follow a teacher's request autonomously, if she accepts its value. It is the opposite of feeling controlled, not the opposite of receiving guidance.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat is the paradox at the centre of Ryan and Deci's argument?

Model answer: Supporting students' basic psychological needs enhances intrinsic motivation and internalization, which results in higher achievement. Paradoxically, trying to control achievement outcomes directly, through extrinsic rewards, sanctions and evaluations, generally backfires and leads to lower-quality motivation and performance.

STEP3
Key concepts · Figure 1 · the taxonomy of motivation

From no reason at all to pure enjoyment

❓ This step answers: Which kinds of motivation does SDT distinguish, and how are they ordered?
🧩 Build Figure 1 yourself: six students explain why they read (or do not read) the book for class. Place each reason in its column. Then three questions about the finished figure.

Some words first. A taxonomy is an ordered classification, like the one biologists use for animals. A regulatory style is the way a behaviour is steered: by a reward, by guilt, by a value, by enjoyment. Volition means willingness. Perceived locus of causality means where a person feels the cause of their behaviour lies: outside them or inside them. Ego-involvement means your self-esteem is at stake in the result. In the picture: six empty columns under three coloured headings. Six students each give a reason about the same book. You tap the column where each reason belongs; the column then shows its official name, its attributes from Figure 1, and its locus of causality. At the end you have rebuilt the article's only figure.

SDT started with intrinsic motivation: doing an activity for its own sake, for its inherent interest and enjoyment. Against it stands extrinsic motivation: behaviour done for reasons other than its inherent satisfactions. But the authors call extrinsic motivation a heterogeneous category, a mixed bag. So SDT specifies four subtypes, arranged by how autonomous they are, plus a sixth category for having no motivation at all.

The ordering is not just a drawing. Much research has verified that the categories relate to each other in the order the model predicts. And the more autonomous the motivation, the better the outcomes: greater relative autonomy for learning, or for teaching, predicts a variety of key educational outcomes. At the other end, amotivation has been a strong negative predictor of engagement, learning and wellness.

The four extrinsic styles. External regulation: driven by externally imposed rewards and punishments. Introjected regulation: partially internalized; you reward yourself with self-esteem and punish yourself with anxiety, shame or guilt. Identified regulation: you consciously endorse the value of the activity. Integrated regulation: the value also fits your other core interests and values. The first two are controlled, the last two autonomous. Amotivation, lacking intentionality, can come from a lack of felt competence or from a lack of value or interest.

Two refinements. First, autonomous extrinsic motivation is not the same as intrinsic motivation: both are highly volitional, but intrinsic motivation rests on interest and enjoyment, while identified and integrated motivation rest on a sense of value. Second, the columns are not boxes for people. Most intentional behaviour is multiply motivated, so researchers also work with a relative autonomy score, with summary scores for autonomous and controlled motives, and with person-centred profiles. Intrinsic motivation itself is taken very seriously: the authors think it is likely responsible for the preponderance of human learning across the life span.

So the useful question about a student is not "is she intrinsically motivated?" School cannot always be fun. The question is whether her reasons are autonomous or controlled, and a teacher can help a reason move to the right along the internalization arrow. How, and with what results, is the subject of the next two steps.

⚠ The trap✗ Introjected motivation comes from inside the person, so it is autonomous.✓ Introjection is "internally controlled": the person pressures themself with guilt, shame and outcome-dependent self-esteem. External regulation and introjection are both controlled forms. Autonomy is about willingness, not about where the pressure is located.
📝 Exam-style questionA student dislikes statistics but studies it willingly because she wants to be a good researcher. Which regulatory style is this, and is it autonomous or controlled?

Model answer: Identified regulation (or integrated regulation, if being a researcher is congruent with her other core values). She personally endorses the value of the activity and acts with a high degree of volition, although the activity is not enjoyable. It is extrinsic, because it is not done for its inherent satisfaction, and autonomous.

STEP4
How they support it · the classroom research

Two hypotheses, tested from primary school to law school

❓ This step answers: What is the evidence that autonomous motivation and need support matter in education?
🔬 Predict five studies: each one tests a link in the chain. Decide first, then see what was found.

Some words first. A hypothesis is a prediction a theory makes that research can test. Controlling for earlier achievement means comparing students who started at the same level. GPA is a grade point average. A prospective or longitudinal study follows people forward in time. To mediate is to be the link in between. Cortisol is a hormone released under stress. In the picture: three boxes in a row. Hypothesis (b) is the first arrow: need support builds autonomous motivation. Hypothesis (a) is the second arrow: autonomous motivation improves outcomes. After each prediction, the study is pinned under the arrow it tests.

Among the core hypotheses of SDT in education are two. (a) More autonomous forms of motivation will enhance students' engagement, learning and wellness. (b) Basic psychological need support from both teachers and parents facilitates such motivation, whereas need thwarting undermines it.

According to the authors both have been well supported across hundreds of studies, at every level of development, and across varied learning contents and cultural contexts. A large literature shows positive relations of more autonomous classroom motivation with academic outcomes, and need supports have shown robust positive effects on school outcomes.

Evidence for (a). A meta-analysis pointed to a significant role of intrinsic motivation in school achievement (Taylor et al., 2014), and intrinsic motivation predicted engagement and through it achievement (Froiland & Worrell, 2016). In college, autonomous motivation predicted higher performance beyond prior achievement (Manganelli et al., 2019). Why? Probably in part because students put in more effort when autonomously motivated, and because internalized motivation becomes part of identity: need satisfaction went with identifying oneself as a scientist (Skinner et al., 2017). The worry: intrinsic motivation for school tends to decline over the school years.

Evidence for (b). Students of more autonomy-supportive teachers have more intrinsic motivation, perceived competence and self-esteem, better grades, greater internalization and lower dropout. Perceived autonomy support led to greater engagement through autonomous motivation in Spanish college students (Núñez & León, 2019). It applies in advanced education too: medical students' choice of residency area was predicted by their mentors' autonomy support, and law students with autonomy-supportive instructors declined less in well-being and did better on the bar exam (Sheldon & Krieger, 2007). And it shows in the body: controlling teaching raised cortisol, autonomy-supportive teaching lowered it (Reeve & Tseng, 2011). Looking ahead, the authors expect the neuropsychology of autonomy to matter more: brain research on intrinsic motivation already points to how choice and optimal challenge may switch on reward areas (the striatum) linked to enjoyment, and frontal areas needed for engaged learning (see Reeve & Lee, 2018).

So the chain holds at both links. That shifts the practical question from the student to the teacher: if need support is what builds good motivation, what exactly does a need-supportive teacher do? That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ Motivated students simply were the better students already; motivation adds nothing.✓ Several studies controlled for baseline or prior achievement (and in one case entrance test scores) and still found that intrinsic or autonomous motivation, or autonomy support, predicted higher performance.
📝 Exam-style questionState the two core hypotheses of SDT in education and give one study for each.

Model answer: (a) More autonomous forms of motivation enhance students' engagement, learning and wellness: for example, intrinsic motivation was consistently associated with higher performance controlling for baseline achievement (Taylor et al., 2014). (b) Basic need support from teachers and parents facilitates such motivation, and need thwarting undermines it: for example, STEM students with more autonomy-supportive lab instructors showed increases in autonomous motivation and perceived competence and received higher grades (Black & Deci, 2000).

STEP5
What it looks like in class · autonomy support and structure

Structure is not control, and support is not letting go

❓ This step answers: What do need-supportive teachers actually do, and what do controlling teachers do?
🧑‍🏫 Watch Ms Rossi: four moves to place in the right field, then four findings to predict.

Some words first. A rationale is a reason given for a request. Efficacy feedback is feedback that tells you how capable you are becoming. To scaffold is to give temporary support that makes a task doable, like scaffolding around a building. A directive is an order. Permissiveness means letting people do as they please. A diary method asks people to report after every lesson or day. In the picture: four fields. On the right, the two ingredients of need-supportive teaching; on the left, their opposites. Ms Rossi makes four moves: tap the field each belongs to. Then four findings follow.

The ingredients of a need-supportive classroom primarily concern two things the teacher provides: autonomy support and structure. Autonomy support is seen as promoting both autonomy and relatedness satisfactions, and when it occurs along with structure, competence as well. Their opposites are control and chaos.

Observation studies show what this looks like. Eight teacher behaviours were positively associated with students' autonomous motivation, among them listening, making time for independent work, encouraging effort and acknowledging students' perspectives; controlling behaviours such as issuing directives and telling students the answers were negatively related (Reeve & Jang, 2006). And the combination of high autonomy support and high structure has been associated with higher autonomous motivation, more self-regulated learning and lower anxiety.

Autonomy support, in detail. Autonomy-supportive teachers begin by trying to understand, acknowledge and where possible respond to students' perspectives. They give opportunities for ownership and initiative, meaningful choices, tasks that engage interests, and a meaningful rationale when something must be done. Choice needs care: it can be meaningless, or carry subtle pressure, and a student can feel autonomous without any options if she accepts the value of the task. Overall, a meta-analysis supports a positive effect of choice on intrinsic motivation (Patall et al., 2008), and taking students' interests into account works alongside it.

Structure, in detail. SDT strongly distinguishes control from structure. Control pressures students to behave or achieve. Structure means clear expectations and goals, consistency in rules and guidelines, informational supports and rich efficacy feedback. Structure can itself be delivered in a controlling or an autonomy-supportive way. Teachers' autonomy support and structure, as rated by observers, predicted students' engagement in US high schools (Jang et al., 2010), and the same pair in parents predicted children's felt competence and autonomy (Farkas & Grolnick, 2010). A Belgian study mapped teaching styles on a circumplex with eight sub-areas (Aelterman et al., 2019).

So need-supportive teaching is neither soft nor strict: it is clear and it listens. One reassuring detail: teachers who support autonomy typically support competence and relatedness too, because they are tuned in to their students. Up to here the article has been about achievement and engagement. The next step asks about something the authors find even more important: wellness, for every kind of student.

⚠ The trap✗ An autonomy-supportive teacher gives few rules and lets students decide everything.✓ Supporting autonomy is not about permissiveness. The most positive teaching style is high in both autonomy support and structure: clear expectations, consistent rules and feedback, delivered with attention to the students' perspective.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the difference between structure and control in SDT, with one example of each.

Model answer: Controlling behaviours pressure students to think, feel, behave or achieve in particular ways, without responsiveness to their perspective, for example issuing directives or using words such as "should" and "have to". Structure entails setting clear expectations and goals, consistency in rules and guidelines, and informational supports and efficacy feedback, for example explaining what a good report looks like and giving feedback on progress. Structure supports competence and can be delivered in an autonomy-supportive way.

STEP6
Beyond achievement · wellness, culture, diversity

The same needs for every student, everywhere?

❓ This step answers: Does need support also foster well-being, and does that hold across cultures and for diverse learners?
🌍 Five cases: a debate about culture, and what autonomy support does for students who differ. Predict each one.

Some words first. Wellness or well-being means how well a person is doing psychologically. A collectivist culture puts the group first; an individualist culture puts the person first. Etic means holding across all cultures; emic means specific to one culture. Cultural competency is being able to work well with people from different backgrounds. An internal frame of reference is how the world looks from inside one person. In the picture: on the left, countries where the effects of autonomy and autonomy support have been tested; they light up after your first prediction. On the right, two cards: what is universal and what varies, and what autonomy support does for inclusion.

The authors argue that schools, though often narrowly focused on achievement, are more importantly contexts for the development of children, adolescents and young adults. Confidence, self-esteem and mental health are all deeply affected by whether school supports or thwarts basic psychological needs. And they claim this holds across age, ethnicity and culture.

Need satisfaction went with school satisfaction and well-being in Chinese adolescents; teacher autonomy support predicted engagement, which was associated with lower anxiety and depression; parent autonomy support helped Finnish students through school transitions. On culture, a meta-analysis found no differences between East Asian and North American samples in the importance of autonomy to well-being (Yu, Levesque-Bristol & Maeda, 2018).

The culture debate. The universality claim is, the authors admit, controversial, especially for autonomy. Critics (the article names Markus, Kitayama and Heiman, 1996) argued that autonomy is not important in traditionalist or collectivist cultures. The authors agree that cultures vary in values and practices, including in how they value autonomy, but argue that the functional importance of autonomy is universal. Their evidence: studies in the USA and Russia, Nigeria and India, Japan, South Korea and China. Their nuance: SDT makes etic claims about the needs and recognizes emic variation in their salience, meaning and dynamics. The same teacher behaviour can be experienced as more or less controlling in different cultures.

Diversity. The authors see an irony in the objection: by respecting autonomy, SDT acknowledges the differences between people. Autonomy support has as its central feature the attempt to appreciate and respect the learner's internal frame of reference, so it is a central element of cultural competency, and it extends to all the ways learners differ: socioeconomic background, temperament, interests, religious values, sexual identity, even neurological processing style. With such support, people who might otherwise feel alienated can feel safer. When teachers become more autonomy supportive, the culture of a whole school can become more accepting and tolerant (Assor et al., 2009). Japan is one example: studies showed that the internalization continuum predicts outcomes for Japanese elementary school students, and the more autonomously motivated pupils were more interested, studied more deeply and felt better about school (Hayamizu, 1997; Yamauchi & Tanaka, 1998). Promoting autonomy and intrinsic motivation also raised Japanese primary pupils' engagement in English lessons (Oga-Baldwin et al., 2017).

So need support is not a technique for raising grades with well-being as a bonus. For the authors it is the other way round: wellness is the main point, and it turns out to bring engagement with it. That makes the next question uncomfortable. If control harms motivation and wellness, what about the controlling tools that every school uses?

⚠ The trap✗ SDT claims that all cultures value autonomy equally and that needs are satisfied in the same way everywhere.✓ SDT claims that the functional importance of the needs is universal (etic). It accepts that cultures vary in how much they value autonomy and in the salience, meaning and dynamics of the needs (emic).
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do Ryan and Deci call autonomy support a central element of cultural competency?

Model answer: Because the central feature of autonomy support is attempting to appreciate and respect the internal frame of reference of the learner. People from diverse backgrounds and value systems have frames of reference that influence their motivations and valuations, so a teacher who starts from the learner's own perspective is by definition responsive to those differences. The same holds for all diversities among learners, not only cultural ones.

STEP7
A needs-based critique · grading, performance goals, high-stakes tests

When ordinary school tools work as pressure

❓ This step answers: What does SDT say about grades, performance goals and high-stakes testing, and on what evidence?
🎛️ Inform or control? Six cases. Start with two ways of giving feedback, then predict what three common school tools do.

Some words first. Structural factors are features of the system, not of one teacher: class size, mandated curricula, testing rules. Criterion based grading compares your work with a standard; comparative grading compares you with other students. Accountability means being held responsible for results. A standardized test is the same test for everyone. An incentive is a reward meant to change behaviour. In the picture: a bar at the top for the key idea, which fills in after the first case. Below it, three school tools. As you predict the studies, each tool shows what the article concludes about it.

Teacher style is not the whole story. Structural factors and policies also affect motivation, sometimes in unintended ways. The authors single out three: an excessive emphasis on grades, performance goals, and pressures from high-stakes tests. Their tool for judging all three is functional significance: is the event experienced as informational, or as controlling?

For grades: an experiment found grading associated with decreased intrinsic motivation and lower conceptual learning (Grolnick & Ryan, 1987), and a Swedish natural experiment linked primary school grading to lower later achievement and lower odds of finishing secondary education, especially for lower-ability students (Klapp, 2015). For high-stakes tests: improved scores do not typically generalize to other, more valid tests.

Grades and performance goals. Grades are typically comparative, so the priority placed on them often sets off performance goals, the desire to outperform others. Grades by themselves typically give little competence-relevant feedback, and used as motivators they will typically be experienced as controlling. In mastery goals the aim is enhancing one's own competence; in performance goals the focus is on outperforming others, and substantial evidence suggests that performance-avoidance goals are the most detrimental. The SDT reading: performance goals are commonly experienced as controlling pressures, mastery goals as informational. And the motives behind a goal predicted outcomes better than the goal itself (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010; Gillet et al., 2015).

High-stakes tests. Policymakers attached incentives and sanctions to standardized test scores, assuming that poor performance is due to poor teacher motivation and that rewards and sanctions will remedy it. The authors call the result remarkably ineffective: teaching narrowed to the test, poor performers kept away from tests, false reporting, and enriching subjects cut, with the quality of teaching compromised especially for disadvantaged students and students learning English as a second language. Their principle: outcome-focused rewards and sanctions reinforce any route to the goal, even bad practice. So they favour policies that support the best processes in classrooms. Why are grades so common, then? The authors fear it is partly because some educators and policy-makers assume that grading rewards effort and gives feedback. Yet grades by themselves typically give little feedback about competence: they mainly tell students where they stand relative to others (Butler, 1987, and the authors' own work). As the alternative to test-based accountability the authors point to school reforms that attend to the psychological needs of teachers and students (e.g., Early et al., 2016). They also note that self-determination theory, although it relies on empirical methods, shares with many constructivist and post-modern approaches a concern with what a culture internalizes and imposes, so it can be both empirically grounded and critical (Ryan & Niemiec, 2009).

So the critique is one argument applied three times: a tool that students or teachers experience as pressure toward an outcome undermines the autonomous motivation that produces the outcome. The step also shows why teachers cannot fix this alone. Much of the pressure does not start in the classroom. It arrives from above, which is where the article goes next.

⚠ The trap✗ Ryan and Deci want schools to stop giving students any feedback on how they perform.✓ They distinguish informational from controlling feedback. Efficacy-related information and authentic feedback about mastery enhance motivation. Their target is grades used as motivators, which mostly rank students, and they accept that grading may sometimes be needed for gatekeeping.
📝 Exam-style questionUsing the concept of functional significance, explain why SDT expects grades to undermine motivation while mastery feedback supports it.

Model answer: Functional significance is the meaning feedback has for the recipient. Feedback is informational when it is efficacy relevant, helping the person improve or highlighting competence; this tends to enhance intrinsic motivation and internalization. It is controlling when experienced as pressure toward specific behaviours or outcomes. Grades as commonly used give little competence-relevant information (they mainly show where a student stands relative to others) and are far too often experienced as controlling, so they diminish autonomous motivation. Authentic feedback about mastery is informational.

STEP8
Supporting teachers to support students · interventions

Thriving students require thriving teachers

❓ This step answers: What do teachers need in order to be need supportive, and can autonomy support be trained?
🏫 Follow the chain: five studies from the superintendent down to the student. Predict each one and watch the school light up.

Some words first. A superintendent is the official in charge of the schools in a district, the principal's boss. Burnout is exhaustion from long-lasting work stress. An intervention is a planned attempt to change something, here a training for teachers. A control group does not get the training, so that there is something to compare with. Proximal means closest. An effect size says how big a difference is. In the picture: a staircase of four levels, from district policy down to the student. On the right, two cards: the pressures on a teacher, and what training achieves. They fill in as you predict.

Autonomy-supportive teaching is not always easy, given limits of time, resources and curricula. And teachers, like their students, have basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness. SDT maintains that for teachers to actively support students' needs, they themselves must experience need support.

Pressure from above (accountability policies, controlling administrators) and from below (disengaged students, difficult parents) were both negatively associated with teachers' autonomous motivation and with their autonomy support of students (Pelletier et al., 2002). In the other direction: training works. Across nineteen studies in which educators were trained in autonomy-supportive methods, the effect size was large, 0.63 (Su & Reeve, 2011).

What pressure does to teachers. When teachers experienced work overload or disruptive students, their autonomous motivation and perceived competence were lower, leading in turn to more emotional exhaustion and less sense of accomplishment. Job pressures were associated with burnout through the frustration of teachers' own needs. Pressure to boost student performance predicted lower autonomous motivation, lower vitality and more exhaustion. The authors' summary: when teachers' autonomy is frustrated, from above or from below, they tend to be more controlling with students and less positively engaged. The same pattern holds for principals and their superintendents.

Interventions. SDT-based interventions have targeted teachers as the most proximal influences on students' engagement and learning. Trained teachers were observed to be more autonomy supportive, and their students more engaged (Reeve et al., 2004). Increases in teacher autonomy support were also associated with teachers' own increased feelings of efficacy and the adoption of more intrinsic goals (Cheon et al., 2018). Such studies matter for two reasons the authors name: they demonstrate the causal role of teacher need support, and they show the practical value of motivation research.

So the article's answer to "why don't teachers just do this?" is the same as its answer for students: because their own needs are under pressure. Effective reform is therefore not just about changing teachers' behaviour, but about supporting the needs of teachers and principals as well. The last step gathers the argument and the authors' conclusion.

⚠ The trap✗ Whether a teacher is controlling or autonomy supportive is a matter of personality, so it cannot be changed.✓ It depends heavily on the pressures and support the teacher experiences, and it can be trained: trained teachers were more autonomy supportive than control-group teachers (Reeve et al., 2004), and a meta-analysis of nineteen studies of trained educators found a large effect, 0.63 (Su & Reeve, 2011).
📝 Exam-style questionA government wants better teaching and plans to pay teachers according to their students' test scores. What would Ryan and Deci predict, and why?

Model answer: They would predict that it backfires. Pressure on teachers to boost student performance predicts lower autonomous motivation for teaching, lower vitality and more exhaustion, and teachers whose autonomy is frustrated by pressures from above tend to be more controlling with students, which undermines students' autonomous motivation. Outcome-focused rewards and sanctions also reinforce any route to the goal, including teaching to the test. They would favour supporting teachers' basic psychological needs and the best processes in classrooms.

The authors' conclusion: there remain "important gaps between dominant policies and practices" in schools and what SDT research shows about best practice, and they urge the field to close them. (Conclusions, p. 9)

Why this matters: the article ends on policy, not on theory. The authors think a great deal is already known about what supports motivation. What is missing, in their view, is its adoption: policies aimed at the needs of teachers and students have not yet been widely adopted, and many teachers must support their students despite mandated curricula, performance pressure, grading requirements and high-stakes tests.

We have walked through the whole article: who wrote it and why, the three needs and the paradox of control, the taxonomy of Figure 1, the evidence for the two hypotheses, what need-supportive teaching looks like, wellness across cultures and diversity, the critique of grades and testing, and the needs of teachers.

Self-determination theory says that people tend by nature to learn and grow, and that this tendency needs support for autonomy, competence and relatedness. Motivation comes in kinds, from controlled (rewards, punishments, guilt) to autonomous (value, identity, enjoyment), and the autonomous kinds go with better learning and well-being. Teachers build them by combining autonomy support with structure. Control tends to do the opposite, whether it comes from a teacher's words, from grades, or from tests that punish schools. And teachers need support in order to give it: pressured teachers tend to become more controlling.

Where the theory goes next. The authors list several directions: more varied methods at several levels of analysis, including the neuroscience of autonomy and new ways of measuring; learning and technology, such as games and e-learning that satisfy the three needs; a deeper learning theory that connects motivation to specific kinds of thinking and teaching; teacher and leadership motivation; more qualitative studies; globalization, diversity and wellness, studied with an approach that expects universal needs without uniform ways of meeting them; and SDT as a critical psychology that can compare teaching styles, school organizations and even national policies by how far they support or thwart needs. They report that need-supportive conditions foster other inner resources for learning as well: executive functioning, critical thinking and integrative decision-making. They describe how the theory was built step by step on converging evidence and tested against competing models. They also note that SDT now consists of six mini-theories, and they present it as a framework that can unify many of the constructs you met in reading 1. Unlike some psychologists, they defend self-report measures: in education, they argue, experience matters, so it should be measured well and checked against observational, biological and behavioural indicators.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why is "extrinsic motivation is bad for learning" too simple according to this article?

Because extrinsic motivation is a heterogeneous category with four subtypes. External regulation and introjection are controlled, but identified and integrated regulation are autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation: the person endorses the value of the activity and acts willingly. Autonomous motivation, including these two, predicts positive outcomes.

2. How can a teacher be high in structure without being controlling?

By setting clear expectations and goals, being consistent in rules, and giving informational support and efficacy feedback, while staying responsive to students' perspectives, giving a rationale and offering meaningful choice. Structure can be delivered in an autonomy-supportive way; control is pressure to think, feel or behave in particular ways.

3. What do the authors mean when they say SDT can be a "critical" psychology?

That it gives basic and measurable criteria (do curricula, teaching strategies, leadership styles and policies support or thwart the basic psychological needs of learners and teachers?) and so provides a basis for critical comparisons between teaching styles, school organizations and even national policies, instead of treating all practices as equally valid.
