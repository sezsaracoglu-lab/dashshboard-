Instructional practices that support adaptive motivation, engagement, and learning

Patall, E. A., Linnenbrink-Garcia, L., Liu, P. P., Zambrano, J., & Yates, N. (2022). Instructional practices that support adaptive motivation, engagement, and learning. In The Oxford Handbook of Educational Psychology.

STEP1
What this chapter is, and the problem it answers

Motivation is something a classroom can change

❓ This step answers: Why was this chapter written, and how is it built?
Authors
Erika A. Patall, Lisa Linnenbrink-Garcia, Pei Pei Liu, Jeanette Zambrano and Nicole Yates
Type
Handbook chapter in The Oxford Handbook of Educational Psychology (published online 2022)
Built for
Researchers, educators and policy-makers: five tables of teaching practices, each with mechanisms and cautions
Main message
Classrooms can be designed to support motivation, through five sets of evidence-based practices that work best combined and tailored

For many teachers, supporting students' motivation and engagement is a critical daily challenge, and the chapter says their concern is well founded: research suggests that between 30 and 60% of secondary students are disengaged from school, and motivation, emotions and engagement are critical intermediaries to learning. The chapter is an overview of classroom practices that can be built into instruction to support students' adaptive motivation, emotion and engagement and, in turn, their learning and achievement.

The good news: motivation and engagement are activated and enhanced when the classroom is designed to support them. Unlike cognitive factors such as intelligence, which explain a good portion of school success but are not easily influenced, motivation and engagement are influenced by the educational environment and by interventions. Students come to school equipped with motivational resources: they naturally seek to explore their interests, live out their values, pursue goals, develop competence and connect with others. Classroom practices sometimes tap those sources and sometimes ignore or thwart them.

The plan: five major sets of adaptive motivational variables and the strategies that support each. (a) Perceived competence. (b) Autonomy and autonomous motivation. (c) Interest and value. (d) Growth mindsets, adaptive causal attributions and mastery goals. (e) Relatedness and belonging. The authors admit the list could be longer or more detailed, for example with strategies for adaptive emotions, but they chose five categories deliberately, and put into them all the strategies for which they see convincing or promising evidence.

How to read it. Each of the five sections has the same build: a short overview of the theories, then a table of practices with, next to the practice itself, three columns (what the teacher does, how it works and what to watch out for, and which other kinds of motivation it can also support), then the evidence. Throughout, the authors point to student characteristics, context and variations in a practice that change how well it works, and they pay special attention to students who have been marginalized or minoritized. The walk-through follows that build: one step per table.

⚠ The trap✗ Motivation is a fixed trait of the student, like intelligence, so there is little a teacher can do.✓ The chapter makes exactly the opposite point: unlike cognitive factors such as intelligence, motivation and engagement are influenced by the educational environment and by interventions.
📝 Exam-style questionWhich five sets of motivational variables organize the chapter?

Model answer: (a) Perceived competence; (b) autonomy and autonomous (versus controlled) motivation; (c) interest and value; (d) growth (versus fixed) mindsets, adaptive causal attributions and mastery (versus performance) goals; (e) feelings of relatedness and belonging.

STEP3
Support 1 · perceived competence · Table 1

"I can do this": build real confidence

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support students' perceived competence?
💪 Six cases: decide or predict first. Each answer lights up one of the five practices of Table 1.

Some words first. Perceived competence is how capable a student feels, which is not the same as how capable the student is. Self-efficacy, expectancy and self-concept (reading 1) are all competence beliefs. Stereotype threat is the worry of confirming a negative stereotype about your group. Vicarious means through watching someone else. Metacognitive means thinking about your own thinking. A matched comparison group is made up of similar students. In the picture: on the left the kind of motivation this step is about; on the right the five practices of Table 1 as cards. Six cases follow. After each one, the practice it belongs to lights up.

Several theories stress competence beliefs: self-efficacy in social cognitive theory, expectancies in expectancy-value theory, academic self-concept, the need for competence in self-determination theory, and goal-setting theory. There are subtle differences between them, but the authors take an inclusive approach: all speak to the importance of students believing they can control their own achievement outcomes.

Across these frameworks the evidence is consistent: positive competence beliefs influence students' goals, behaviours, effort, persistence and emotions, their use of self-regulatory strategies and, ultimately, their achievement. Self-efficacy has been shown to predict engagement, school attendance, willingness to set difficult goals, intentions to persist in college and course grades. Students with higher perceived competence have, on average, an advantage across populations and contexts.

Structure, challenge and feedback. A classroom organized through clear, consistent expectations, rules, routines and procedures supports competence beliefs by influencing all four sources of self-efficacy. Tasks should be calibrated to existing skills with challenges that slightly exceed them, with scaffolding and step-by-step increases in difficulty. Feedback should be informational: what the student did successfully, how the strategy helped, and what to improve. Negative feedback is necessary but can reduce perceived competence; it is safer when it concerns the skill and not the person, is given warmly, and comes with tips and options. Wise feedback adds high expectations and confidence.

Models and strategies. Watching a role model complete a task helps students identify strategies to try, especially when the model is a peer or someone they identify with, and when the model shows struggle as part of learning. Self-regulation instruction teaches cognitive strategies (planning, rehearsal, elaboration, organization), metacognitive strategies (monitoring your own progress) and emotional ones (positive self-talk). Self-regulation and competence beliefs are positively and reciprocally associated. Praise should affirm strategy and effort, not traits such as intelligence, so that students attribute results to things they can master. Two more examples from the chapter. In online mathematics modules for Spanish college students, motivational scaffolds that made students aware of how they used their prior knowledge and how well their strategies worked predicted higher self-efficacy at the end of the course (Valencia-Vallejo et al., 2018). And when community college writing instructors added reflection assignments on goal-setting, choosing strategies, self-monitoring and self-evaluation, next to modeling and thinking aloud, they saw gains in motivation and achievement (MacArthur & Philippakos, 2013). One note on scope: some, though limited, research suggests that the link between perceived competence and educational outcomes may vary subtly across grade levels, genders, cultures and races.

So a teacher builds "I can do this" by arranging real, visible progress on work that is hard enough to count. Several cautions in this step concern minoritized students: low challenge, critical feedback without a frame of high expectations, and missing role models weigh most where low expectations are already in the air. Confidence is the first support. The second is about whose learning it is.

⚠ The trap✗ To build students' confidence, give them easy tasks and plenty of help so that they always succeed.✓ Insufficiently challenging or overly scaffolded tasks fail to provide mastery experiences, even when completed successfully, and students can read them as a sign that the teacher lacks confidence in them. Competence beliefs grow from appropriately challenging tasks with support.
📝 Exam-style questionName the four sources of self-efficacy and give one practice from the chapter that plausibly feeds each.

Model answer: (1) Enactive mastery experiences: appropriately challenging, scaffolded tasks. (2) Vicarious experiences: modeling by peers or others the student identifies with, including models who struggle. (3) Verbal persuasion and social influences: informational feedback, effort-focused praise, wise feedback that expresses high expectations and confidence. (4) Physiological and affective states: emotional self-regulation strategies such as positive self-talk, which target affective states. The chapter says a well-structured classroom influences all four sources; pairing one practice with one source is our own ordering.

STEP4
Support 2 · autonomy and autonomous motivation · Table 2

"This is my own": take the student's perspective

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support students' autonomy, and when does choice fail?
🗝️ Six cases: when does choice help, when does it backfire, and what else supports autonomy? Predict each one.

Some words first. Autonomy is feeling that your actions come from yourself and fit your values and interests. Volition is willingness. Autonomy thwarting means actively blocking that feeling. Agency is acting to shape your own situation. A diary study asks people to report day after day. Random assignment means chance decides who gets which condition, which allows conclusions about cause. In the picture: the six practices of Table 2 as cards. Three cases are about choice, because choice is the practice with the most conditions attached.

Autonomy support is a personal interaction style: the teacher tries to take the student's perspective to guide the interaction, with an attitude of curiosity, openness and empathic understanding. Its goal is to enhance the student's experience of acting willingly and personally endorsing the behaviour. Any practice that lets students feel they learn because it reflects their own values, preferences, interests and goals counts as autonomy supportive.

Correlational and experimental research gives substantial evidence that students benefit when teachers are more autonomy supportive: more autonomous motivation, more behavioural and emotional engagement and self-regulation, more self-esteem, vitality and well-being, and higher achievement. The benefits seem to hold from preschool to graduate school and across ability levels, cultures and contexts, and may be particularly salient in adolescence.

Choice, with three principles. First, having options, choosing and experiencing autonomy are not the same; only the experience matters. Choices given in a controlling manner (steering toward one option, focusing on outcomes, attached to tangible rewards, or not letting students express their interests) have more limited benefits, and students must care about what they are choosing. Second, choosing can backfire when students lack the competence to choose well, and it has fewer benefits when there are too many or too similar options; in those cases choice needs guidelines or scaffolding. Third, culture and relationships change what choosing means: deferring a choice to a trusted other can itself feel autonomous.

Beyond choice. Perspective-taking: leaving room to hear and respond to students' questions, opinions, criticism and negative feelings. Incorporating perspectives: designing lessons around students' interests or letting them personalize activities. Respectful, informational language ("could", "consider") instead of controlling language ("must", "should"). Minimizing controlling practices, which can promote disengagement, negative feelings, extrinsic motivation or amotivation. And encouraging agentic engagement, where students themselves help shape the class. Rationales also support autonomy; the chapter discusses them under value.

So autonomy support is less a set of tricks than a stance: start from where the student is. Notice how this connects backwards and forwards: choice can backfire for students who lack the competence to choose well (step 3), and a student who chooses freely finds the content more valuable and interesting, which is the next support.

⚠ The trap✗ The more choices a teacher gives, the more autonomy and motivation students have.✓ Choice opportunities, choosing and experiencing autonomy are not synonymous. Too many options, options that are hard to tell apart, choices given in a controlling way, or choices beyond the student's competence have fewer benefits and can backfire.
📝 Exam-style questionGive three conditions under which providing choice is less effective or backfires, according to the chapter.

Model answer: Any three of: the choice is given in a controlling manner (leading students to one option, focusing on outcomes, accompanied by tangible rewards, or not allowing students to express interests); students do not care about the task; students do not know what makes a good choice or lack competence on the task; there are excessive options or options that are hard to discriminate; the student feels indecisive. In a cultural context with a group orientation, deferring the choice to a trusted other may be equally or more motivating.

STEP5
Support 3 · value and interest · Table 3

"This matters, this grabs me": give learning a point and a pull

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices raise the value and interest students find in learning?
✨ Six cases: which reasons work, for whom, and what makes content come alive? Predict each one.

Some words first. Value answers why a person chooses to engage in a task. A rationale is an explanation of why something is useful or important. Prosocial means aimed at helping others. Self-generated means produced by the student. First-generation students are the first in their family to attend college. An asset-based view starts from what students bring, not from what they lack. Cognitive load is the mental effort a task demands. In the picture: the five practices of Table 3. Three cases are about rationales, because the chapter spends most time on when they work.

Four frameworks explain why value and interest matter. Expectancy-value theory: an increase in attainment, intrinsic or utility value (not cost) promotes persistence, performance and future choices. Self-determination theory: students function better when learning behaviours are internalized and endorsed as their own. Interest theories: situational interest is the kind most responsive to the learning environment. And culturally relevant/responsive education, which the next card explains.

Situational interest has been shown to positively influence cognitive and behavioural engagement, memory, self-regulation, use of learning strategies, persistence, achievement, and course and major selection, and repeated arousal of situational interest has a positive effect on the development of individual interest. That is the process reading 1 called poorly understood; this chapter adds at least one finding to it.

Rationales and purpose. At the core of value is understanding why something is useful or important. A rationale gives that reason; it works when it builds on existing knowledge, takes the student's perspective and is specific and concrete, and when it appeals to prosocial or intrinsic goals. Because value is personal, teachers can also let students generate their own: writing how the material is relevant to their lives, class discussions of the value of activities, or lists of reasons made by peers. Which route works depends on the student's initial interest, competence and background.

Four more practices. Contextualized teaching and learning embeds content in meaningful, real-world or exciting situations. Culturally relevant/responsive education uses students' cultural characteristics, experiences and perspectives as mediums for teaching, bridges home and school, and can empower students to examine problems in their own community and act on them. Active learning makes students think about what they are doing. Digital technology can trigger interest because it is novel, and works best combined with the other practices, in collaborative or game contexts, and with designs that keep cognitive load low. For reference, expectancy-value theory names four components of task value: attainment value (the importance of the task for the self), intrinsic value (importance because the task is interesting or enjoyable), utility value (usefulness for other parts of one's life) and cost (the perceived negative aspects of engaging, such as time or anxiety).

So value is not something a teacher can hand over; it has to connect to what the student already cares about. That is why the same advice ("explain why it matters") succeeds with one student and fails with another. The next support deals with what happens when the work gets hard and the first failures arrive.

⚠ The trap✗ Any explanation of why a task matters will raise students' motivation.✓ Not all rationales are effective. Rationales that appeal to extrinsic goals (money, grades), social norms or pressure, or that take only the teacher's perspective, can undermine motivation. And which route fits depends on the student: told rationales tend to be most effective for students with high interest and competence, while self-generated value activities are particularly effective for students with low interest and competence.
📝 Exam-style questionA teacher has one group with high interest and competence in physics and one group with low interest and competence. Which approach to value does the chapter suggest for each?

Model answer: For the high-interest, high-competence group: directly communicated rationales tend to be most effective. For the low-interest, low-competence group (and among first-generation students of colour): self-generated value activities, for example writing how the material is relevant to their own lives, are particularly effective. A focus on relevance for daily life and helping-oriented goals may extend the benefits to students at various interest and ability levels.

STEP6
Support 4 · growth mindset, attributions, mastery goals · Table 4

"I can grow, and I am here to learn": make the class about progress

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support a growth mindset, adaptive attributions and mastery goals, and how strong is the evidence?
🌱 Six cases: mindset lessons, praise, explanations for failure, and how work is graded. Predict each one, and watch the hedges.

Some words first. A mindset is an implicit theory about whether human characteristics such as intelligence can change. Entity or fixed beliefs say ability is innate and unchangeable; incremental or growth beliefs say it can develop. An attribution is the cause you give for a result; causes differ in locus (inside you, like ability or effort, or outside, like luck or task difficulty), stability (lasting, like aptitude, or passing, like effort) and controllability (effort you can control, luck you cannot). Brain plasticity is the brain's ability to change with use. A moderator is something that changes how strong an effect is. To replicate a study is to repeat it and find the same result. Grading on a curve makes your grade depend on how others did. In the picture: the four practices of Table 4.

Three theories share one idea: students benefit when the emphasis is on developing competence. Attribution theory (how students explain success and failure), mindsets (what they believe about intelligence) and achievement goal theory (whether they aim to develop or to demonstrate competence). The authors treat them in one section because the three are closely related and intersecting. The common element: through hard work, persistence and good strategies, people can develop, learn and grow.

Students with incremental beliefs are more likely to persist after failure, feel more positive emotions and less anxiety, and achieve more; those who see intelligence as fixed take failure as evidence of low intelligence and fall into helpless patterns. But when it comes to interventions, the chapter is careful: research on mindset training programmes is mixed, with a small average effect (Sisk et al., 2018), and the classic praise findings have not been fully replicated.

Training beliefs and explanations. Mindset training gives lessons on brain plasticity; the briefest recent programmes present evidence that challenging work builds ability, use quotations from peers, and end with "saying-is-believing" exercises in which students advise a struggling peer. Effects may depend on the school context: the overall achievement level, peer norms about seeking challenge, and what teachers say about effort. Attributional retraining teaches students to see successes as controllable and failures as unstable, typically with videos of older students and a consolidation exercise. Teachers can also simply encourage students to identify effort and strategy as causes of past performance.

Praise and the goal structure. Praise shapes mindsets: process praise for effort, strategies and perseverance, not person praise for being smart. And the classroom itself sends a message. A mastery goal structure has varied, meaningful, appropriately challenging tasks; evaluation that focuses on improvement and absolute standards and keeps comparison out; autonomy support; and a positive socio-emotional climate with caring, high expectations and collaboration. Grades alone were associated with performance goals; comments went with higher mastery goals (Koenka et al., 2021). An example from survey research: undergraduates who perceived a class as focused on grades and performance were more likely to endorse performance goals (Church et al., 2001).

So telling students "you can grow" is the weakest version of this support. The strong version is a classroom in which everything, from praise to grading, says the same thing. Table 4's last row makes the point: a mastery goal structure is built out of the practices from all the other tables. One support remains, and it concerns the people in the room.

⚠ The trap✗ A short growth-mindset lesson reliably raises the achievement of all students.✓ The evidence is mixed. A meta-analysis found a small positive effect, with analyses suggesting benefits for achievement only for academically high-risk and economically disadvantaged students, and the effects may depend on whether the wider school context reinforces the message.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors discuss attributions, mindsets and achievement goals in one section? Describe the two patterns that tend to occur together.

Model answer: Because the three constructs are closely related and intersecting. Performance goals, entity (fixed) beliefs and more helpless attribution patterns after failure (internal, stable, uncontrollable causes such as lack of ability) co-occur. Mastery goals, incremental (growth) beliefs and more adaptive attribution patterns (controllable causes of success, unstable causes of failure) co-occur. The relations are bidirectional.

STEP7
Support 5 · relatedness and belonging · Table 5

"I belong here": connection as a condition for learning

❓ This step answers: Which teaching practices support relatedness and belonging, and for whom do they matter most?
🤝 Six cases: a myth to check, and four ways to build belonging. Predict each one.

Some words first. Belonging or relatedness is the extent to which a person feels connected to others. Minoritized students belong to groups treated as lesser by society. Microaggressions are small, everyday slights that target someone's group. Retention means staying in a school or programme. A testimonial is a personal story told to others. Positive interdependence means group members need one another to succeed. In the picture: the three practices of Table 5, plus culturally relevant education, which the chapter brings back here.

Belonging is emphasized in several theories: attachment theory, self-determination theory (relatedness as a basic need) and the belongingness hypothesis. Students naturally seek relationships with peers and teachers, and will try to conform to the achievement values of those they feel connected to. That is the mechanism: students internalize the values of teachers they feel close to.

From elementary school to college, relatedness to teachers and peers is positively associated with intrinsic motivation, grades, self-efficacy and behavioural and emotional engagement. Perceived teacher support is positively related to belonging from preschool to postsecondary levels. And belongingness is often more strongly connected to retention, persistence and grade increases for minoritized students than for white students.

Why the focus on minoritized students. Belonging matters for all students, but experiences of the educational system shape what it means to belong. Through experiences with racism, interpersonal and systemic, minoritized students are more prone to lacking feelings of belonging in academic settings. The idea that they do not value school has been consistently dispelled. So the chapter concentrates on practices that support belonging particularly for Black, Latinx, Native and other students of colour: cultivating community and meaningful relationships, and making sure students see themselves as accepted and represented, also in the curriculum.

The practices. Teacher-student connections: caring, friendliness and responsiveness, with psychological safety as the key; teachers can also encourage ties among students. Collaboration and group work: group projects, think-pair-share, peer teaching, designed with common goals, small groups, positive interdependence and individual accountability. Messages that communicate belonging: students are valued members of the community, and feelings of being out of place are common and temporary. And culturally relevant education, which helps students feel they belong in a classroom that learns about and honours their cultures, backgrounds and identities. The chapter calls a classroom where students get chances to connect with each other an "interpersonal opportunity structure" (Gray et al., 2018). Relationships with teachers that range from formal mentoring (Larose et al., 2005) to informal, mutual expressions of respect (Anderman, 2003) go with greater belonging and positive academic outcomes, and the key is psychological safety in the classroom (Anderman, 2003).

So belonging closes the list of five, and, in our reading, it loops back to the first: a student who feels safe and accepted is more likely to try hard things. The final step is about how to use the five together, and where the chapter draws its own limits.

⚠ The trap✗ Students from minoritized groups feel less belonging at school because they value school less.✓ Research has consistently dispelled the notion that minoritized students do not value school. Students of colour may want to do well and still feel disconnected from school as an institution because of its unsupportive environment and experiences with racism.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe a belongingness intervention from the chapter and explain what message makes it work.

Model answer: In one type, first-year students are shown what are presented as belonging survey results from older students that indicate consistent improvement in belonging over time, with no differences between genders or races and ethnicities, accompanied by written material. The message: a lack of belonging is not a function of one's social identity, and concerns about belonging are common and temporary. Variants add reading testimonials, affirming one's values, keeping a diary or recording one's own video testimonial. All affirm students as valued members of the school community.

STEP8
The conclusion · three themes

Combine, tailor, and build bridges

❓ This step answers: How should teachers use the five supports together, and how strong is the evidence?
🎯 Six cases from the conclusion: the same practice, different students. Decide what the chapter would say.

Some words first. Universality means holding for everyone. Precision here means fitting the practice to the student. A randomized experiment assigns people to conditions by chance and gives the strongest evidence for cause. Causal evidence is evidence that one thing produces another, not only that they go together. To co-design is to design together. In the picture: three cards for the three closing themes, and a bar about the strength of the evidence. They fill in as you decide the cases.

The authors end with three themes. First, overlap: practices and kinds of motivation do not sit in isolated categories. Second, universality versus precision: there is some degree of universality in the benefits of most practices, yet they need to be tailored. Third, partnership: researchers must work with educators and policy-makers so that motivating instruction becomes routine.

On the evidence, the authors are explicit. In most cases they chose practices whose support is relatively well established and includes causally rigorous designs such as randomized experiments. Some are promising with more limited causal evidence, or still need research on how to use them in class: encouraging agency, using digital technology, practices to support relatedness, and culturally relevant education. Their overall judgment: there is broad enough support to warrant using the recommended practices.

Overlap, in detail. A single practice may tap several aspects of motivation, and a single aspect may respond to a variety of practices. The advantage: many pathways to adaptive motivation. The other side: one practice is seldom enough for a class or even for one student, and many practices are more effective in combination. Structure goes best with autonomy support and an emotionally supportive climate; collaboration with structures that guide it; technology when it carries other practices.

Precision, in detail. The same practice can be interpreted differently depending on the student or the context. Practices are most effective when selected and tailored to the specific motivational challenge a student is experiencing in a particular environment. Confident students may relish choice and rationales; less confident students may find them demoralizing. Belonging and growth messages can be critical for students whose belonging and competence are continuously questioned, and go unnoticed or feel patronizing for others. If those other students struggle to find value in the content, a teacher would use very different strategies.

So the chapter does not hand teachers a checklist. It hands them a way of thinking: find out which support this student is missing, choose practices for it, and combine them with the others. That is also the answer of the whole theme to its opening question: a student starts and keeps going when someone has taken care of the right supports, for that student.

⚠ The trap✗ Because the practices are evidence-based, a teacher can apply the same ones to every student in the same way.✓ There is some universality, but precision matters: the same practice can be interpreted differently depending on the student and context. Support often needs to be tailored to the motivational challenges students are experiencing, and several practices need to be coordinated.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat do the authors mean by universality versus the need for precision? Give one example from the chapter.

Model answer: Universality: practices such as perspective-taking, incorporating students' interests, clear structures and warm, supportive relationships are likely to have benefits regardless of the student or circumstance. Precision: to maximize benefits, practices must be selected and tailored to the specific motivational challenge of a student in a context. Example: confident students might relish opportunities to choose and benefit from a rationale, whereas less confident students may experience the same choices and value messages as demoralizing; or belonging messages that are critical for minoritized students may feel patronizing to students who never questioned their belonging.

The authors' conclusion, in our words: there is broad enough research support for these practices to justify using them in class, especially when teachers combine many of them and consider the particular students in front of them. (Conclusions)

Why this matters: the chapter turns the theories of readings 1 and 2 into something a teacher can act on, and it is honest about where the evidence is thinner.

We have walked through the whole chapter: the problem of disengagement and the good news that classrooms can change motivation, the map of five supports, one step for each of the five tables, and the three closing themes.

A teacher can support five things. That students feel able: through clear structure, work that stretches them, informative feedback, models like themselves and learning strategies. That learning feels like their own: through real choice, listening, building in their interests, inviting language and room to shape the class. That it matters and interests them: through good reasons, real-world and culturally familiar contexts, activity and well-used technology. That they can grow and are there to learn: through mindset and attribution messages, process praise and a classroom that rewards progress. And that they belong: through relationships, well-designed collaboration and explicit belonging messages. None of the five works alone, each must fit the student, and some rest on stronger evidence than others.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why can a task that a student completes successfully still fail to build perceived competence?

Because insufficiently challenging or overly scaffolded tasks do not provide mastery experiences, even if completed successfully. Students often notice the lack of challenge and can interpret it as a sign that the teacher lacks confidence in their abilities, which undermines their own perceived competence.

2. What do the authors conclude about mindset interventions?

That there is some benefit, especially given their low cost, but the effects are relatively small, may only be useful for students already at risk, and may only be effective if the broader educational context reinforces messages about the malleability of intelligence.

3. Which practices do the authors flag as promising but with more limited causal evidence?

Encouraging student agency, using digital technology, practices to support relatedness, and culturally relevant education.
