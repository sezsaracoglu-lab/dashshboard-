Meeting 1 · Job demands and resources · prepared for Sezen

Why do some people burn out at work, while others thrive?

Two colleagues can have the same job, and one ends up exhausted while the other comes alive. This theme gives you the most widely used theory to explain why, and to know what leaders, teams and employees themselves can do about it.

The story of this theme · 8 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Every job has two kinds of ingredients. Some take energy: workload, time pressure, difficult clients. These are job demands. Others give energy and motivation: support from colleagues, feedback, autonomy, learning. These are job resources. When demands keep outweighing resources, people slowly drain until they burn out. When resources are rich, people become engaged, even when the work is hard. And it snowballs: drained people make choices that make their work worse, while engaged people shape their job so it gets better.

Each reading looks at one side of that idea. The two JD-R articles explain the theory itself and what is new. Demerouti (2025) compares it with its "parent", conservation of resources (COR) theory. Junker and colleagues move up a level, from the individual to the team. The two chapters show what employees can do themselves: job crafting and playful work design.

Why a psychologist learns this: work fills a large part of adult life. As a work psychologist, HR professional or coach you will be asked why a team is exhausted or disengaged, and what to change. JD-R theory is the go-to framework for that diagnosis, and it works for students and their studies too.

Article 1 of 4 · The core theory

Job Demands–Resources Theory: Frequently Asked Questions

Bakker, A. B., & Demerouti, E. (2024). Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 29(3), 188–200.

Before we look at a single idea, it helps to know who is talking. This article is written by the two people who created the theory. That makes it the most authoritative source on what JD-R says, and also a source you should read with a critical eye, because authors naturally defend their own theory.

Arnold Bakker (Erasmus University Rotterdam) and Evangelia Demerouti (Eindhoven University of Technology) introduced the job demands–resources (JD-R) model in 2001 (Demerouti et al., 2001). Over twenty years it grew from a model into a full theory and became one of the most-cited theories in organizational psychology. Managers, consultants and HR professionals use it for risk checks, workshops, apps and interventions.

The article is built like a long FAQ page. Part 1 sums up the theory in seven propositions. Part 2 answers 13 questions that researchers and practitioners kept asking them, such as "Can a resource also be a demand?" or "Do loss and gain spirals really exist?". Part 3 concludes with four features that make the theory useful. There is no new experiment: every answer is built on earlier studies and meta-analyses.

This matters for the exam. A sentence such as "Bakker and Demerouti (2024) found..." is almost always wrong. They argue, propose and cite studies by others. Next we look at the problem that made them write an FAQ in the first place.

STEP2
What problem does the article try to solve?

A theory that looks simple, but raises recurring questions

❓ This step answers: Why did the authors write an FAQ about their own theory?

We know who wrote the article. Now the obvious question: why would the founders of a successful theory write an FAQ about it? Usually you only write an FAQ when the same questions keep coming in.

That is exactly the problem. JD-R looks simple: some things at work drain you (job demands), other things motivate you (job resources). Because it looks so simple, people apply it quickly, and run into questions along the way. The authors write that "the theory seems simple", but that several issues "need clarification" so that it is applied in the way that "makes the best predictions". Think of a recipe that only says "add salt and heat": everyone thinks they can cook it, and everyone cooks something different.

Scholars and practitioners sometimes turned to Bakker and Demerouti for help when they applied the theory. The 13 questions in the article are the ones they heard most. They cover what the concepts mean, what the evidence shows, how people and time come in, and how far the theory reaches. The article is inspired by an earlier FAQ by Ajzen (2020) about the theory of planned behavior.

So the article asks one overall question: how should JD-R be understood and used so that it predicts well-being and performance as well as possible? To answer it, we first need the theory itself. That is the next step: the seven propositions.

⚠ The trap✗ The article was written because JD-R had been disproven.✓ It was written because the theory is widely used and supported, but users keep running into the same questions. The FAQ clarifies, defends and extends it.
📝 Exam-style questionAn HR consultant says: "JD-R is common sense: stress is bad, support is good. You don't need a theory for that." Which issue raised in the FAQ does this remark touch on, and how do the authors respond?

Model answer: The question whether JD-R can be falsified. Its propositions seem self-evident, but the authors argue that an intuitively reasonable theory is not a problem (Ajzen, 2020), and that JD-R also makes less obvious, testable predictions (mediation and moderation) that could be disconfirmed.

STEP3
Key concepts and definitions · Learning goals 1 and 2

Seven propositions, one model

❓ This step answers: What are job demands and job resources, and what does JD-R claim about them? (Learning goals 1 and 2)
👆 Tap a numbered badge on the model to read that proposition in plain words.
Tap a numbered badge on the model to see that proposition.

We know why the article exists. Now we need the theory itself, because every one of the 13 questions refers back to it. The authors summarize JD-R in seven propositions. Think of them as the seven rules of the game.

Rule 1 is about the ingredients, and it is what makes JD-R different from older job design theories such as job characteristics theory (Hackman & Oldham, 1976): JD-R is flexible. It has no fixed list. Every job has its own job demands, aspects that require effort and therefore cost something physically (heart rate) or psychologically (fatigue, anxiety). And every job has its own job resources: physical, psychological, social or organizational aspects that help you reach work goals, soften the impact of demands, and help you learn and grow, such as constructive feedback, skill variety and support from colleagues.

Rule 2 says the two ingredients start two different processes. In the health impairment process, demands use up your physical, emotional and cognitive energy; over a long time short-term fatigue becomes chronic and health problems can follow. In the motivational process, resources satisfy basic needs (to belong, to do meaningful work, to act autonomously) and spark work engagement, which leads to better performance. Rule 3 adds two interactions, the buffer and the boost (next step). Rules 4 and 5 bring in personal resources, such as optimism and self-efficacy: they go hand in hand with job resources and help people deal with demands. In a weekly diary study among nurses (Bakker & Sanz-Vergel, 2013), emotional demands led to more engagement when self-efficacy and optimism were high, and to less when they were low.

Rules 6 and 7 make the employee an actor instead of a passive receiver. Rule 6: people proactively change their own demands and resources, called job crafting, which raises person-job fit, meaningfulness and engagement. Rule 7: under high demands and strain, people slide into self-undermining (careless mistakes, poor communication, conflicts), which creates new obstacles. Together, rules 6 and 7 are the seeds of the gain and loss spirals you will meet in step 8. Next: how demands and resources combine.

⚠ The trap✗ JD-R lists the job characteristics that matter for every job: workload, autonomy, support and feedback.✓ JD-R is flexible: each job has its own demands and resources. A set such as workload, autonomy and support is common in most jobs, but the theory does not fix the list.
📝 Exam-style questionAccording to JD-R theory, through which process do job resources mainly affect job performance?

Model answer: The motivational process: job resources foster work engagement (by satisfying basic psychological needs and intrinsic motivation), and engagement leads to better performance.

STEP4
Key concepts · Learning goal 2

Resources soften demands, and challenges make resources count

❓ This step answers: How do demands and resources work together, and why do some people thrive under high demands? (Learning goal 2)
👆 Switch between the two graphs to compare the buffer and the boost hypothesis.

We now know the two processes. But they don't run side by side without touching. Proposition 3 says demands and resources have a combined effect on well-being. This is the answer to the opening puzzle: two people with the same high demands can end up in very different places.

The buffer hypothesis says job resources weaken the effect of job demands on strain. Picture two umbrellas in the same storm: the rain (demands) is identical, but one umbrella (resources) is much bigger. Resources may change how the demand is perceived, soften the reaction to it, or limit the damage afterwards. The boost hypothesis is the mirror image: challenging demands make resources more motivating. Feedback and support matter most on the days you face complex work or time pressure. In short, resources become most important for engagement when demands are high.

The evidence comes from diary studies, where the same people report on many days. Tadić et al. (2015) found that teachers were more inspired and engaged on days with hard, complex work, but only when they also had daily resources such as feedback and support (boost). On days with hindrances such as role conflict, they were less engaged, especially when resources were scarce (buffer). Breevaart and Bakker (2018) found the same pattern with leadership: challenge demands went with the most engagement when the leader was transformational (inspiring, considerate, stimulating), and hindrances hurt most when the leader showed little of it.

So "thriving" in JD-R is not the absence of demands. It is high (challenge) demands met by high resources. Anna, the exhausted nurse from the story, had high demands and few resources; Sam had the same demands and many resources. Next we ask how the authors know all this: what kind of evidence stands behind the theory?

⚠ The trap✗ The boost hypothesis says job demands boost burnout.✓ The boost hypothesis says challenging demands strengthen the positive effect of job resources on work engagement. It is about motivation, not strain.
📝 Exam-style questionOn days with a heavy workload, a project manager who gets a lot of support from her team feels hardly more exhausted than on quiet days. Her colleague without support feels much more exhausted. Which JD-R hypothesis does this pattern illustrate?

Model answer: The buffer hypothesis: job resources (social support) weaken the impact of job demands (workload) on strain (exhaustion).

STEP5
Research method · the evidence behind the answers

Meta-analyses, diaries, and a hidden interaction

❓ This step answers: What kind of evidence do the authors use, and why can a study miss the buffer effect?
🔬 Be the researcher: drag the slider to decide which employees end up in your sample. Watch what you would conclude.

We have seen what JD-R claims. Since this article runs no study of its own, the real question is: what evidence stands behind the answers? Three kinds come back again and again, and one figure shows how evidence can fool you.

First, meta-analyses: studies that pool the results of many earlier studies. Crawford et al. (2010) combined 43 studies with almost 27,000 employees; Lesener et al. (2019) combined 57 longitudinal samples. Second, diary studies that follow the same people day by day or week by week, such as the teacher and nurse studies in step 4. They show within-person effects: what happens to you on your worse days. Third, longitudinal studies that measure people at Time 1 and again months or years later, which are needed to test spirals.

Now the trick in Figure 1. Several meta-analyses found that job resources are directly linked to less burnout. JD-R predicts this link only indirectly, through engagement. The authors give four reasons why earlier studies may have tested this badly. One is in the slider game: if almost everyone in your sample has high demands, you never see the low-demand side of the graph. Then the two lines simply look like "more resources, less exhaustion", a main effect, and the interaction (the buffer) is masked. The same happens if the interaction term is left out of the analysis. The other reasons: the indirect path through engagement was not modelled; burnout was measured with motivation items (cynicism, reduced accomplishment); and people with many resources may simply face fewer demands.

So a direct link between resources and strain is not proof that resources work like demands. Still, the authors admit it "cannot be ruled out": energetic colleagues may pass on energy, and resources may build personal resources linked to good health. Exam lesson: always ask what the sample and the analysis could see. Next we look at the theory JD-R grew out of: COR theory.

⚠ The trap✗ Figure 1 shows that job resources have no buffer effect.✓ Figure 1 shows how a buffer effect can be hidden: when only high demands are observed, the interaction looks like a main effect of resources on exhaustion.
📝 Exam-style questionA study among emergency nurses, who all report very high workload, finds that social support is related to lower exhaustion, but finds no support × workload interaction. What explanation from the article could account for this?

Model answer: Restricted variance in job demands: because nearly all nurses have high demands, really low demands are not observed and the demands × resources interaction is masked, so it shows up as a main effect of resources on exhaustion.

STEP6
FAQ 1 · Learning goal 3

COR is the parent, JD-R the specialist

❓ This step answers: What is the difference between COR theory and JD-R theory, and what did JD-R borrow? (Learning goal 3)

The first question in the FAQ is the one your learning goal 3 asks: how is JD-R related to conservation of resources (COR) theory? JD-R "stands on the shoulders of giants": classic job design theories such as Herzberg's two-factor theory, job characteristics theory and Karasek's demand-control model. But its closest relative in the reading list is COR.

COR (Hobfoll, 1989) is a general stress theory. Its central idea is that people need resources to survive, so they try to obtain, retain, foster and protect them. "Resources" is meant broadly: objects (a car), conditions (a marriage, a job), skills and beliefs (self-efficacy), and volatile resources such as energy, attention and time. COR is basically about what people do when they anticipate losing or actually lose resources, and people must invest resources to protect against loss, recover from loss and gain new ones. JD-R is narrower and more precise: it is an organizational theory that says which resources, namely job resources, are needed to deal with job demands and protect well-being. Think of COR as a general map of your whole life's "battery", and JD-R as the detailed map of your workplace.

JD-R borrows three things. COR's gain paradox principle (gains become more important when you are losing) is relevant for the buffer hypothesis and is the direct basis of the boost hypothesis. JD-R's loss and gain spirals also come from COR, but JD-R spells them out concretely: demands → strain → self-undermining → more demands, and resources → engagement → job crafting → more resources. COR adds a paradox JD-R must face: on high-demand days you need to actively seek new resources, exactly when you have the least energy to do so.

So the difference is scope and focus, not opposition. COR is broad, about people and all the resources they value in any life domain. JD-R is specific: it names the job demands and job resources that matter for burnout, engagement and performance. You will meet a fuller comparison in Demerouti (2025), reading 3. Next we look at the questions about where one concept ends and another begins.

⚠ The trap✗ JD-R and COR are rival theories that make opposite predictions.✓ JD-R builds on COR. COR is a broad stress theory about all valued resources; JD-R specifies which job characteristics matter and borrows COR's gain paradox and spirals.
📝 Exam-style questionWhich principle of COR theory forms the direct basis for the boost hypothesis in JD-R theory?

Model answer: The gain paradox principle: resource gain becomes more salient (important) in the context of resource loss. Applied to work, job resources matter most on days with high (challenging) job demands.

STEP7
FAQs on the concepts · Learning goals 1 and 2

Where one concept ends and the next begins

❓ This step answers: Can a resource be a demand? Are all demands bad? And is work engagement just the opposite of burnout?

A theory with two ingredients and two outcomes only works if you can tell them apart. Three of the FAQs are about exactly that: where does a resource end and a demand begin, are all demands the same, and is engagement just "no burnout"?

Can a resource be a demand? The authors' answer is a clear no. Demands cost energy and drive the health impairment process; resources don't create energy but spark motivation. Crucially, a low score on a resource is not a high score on a demand: little support makes you less engaged, but it is not itself a demand. And low demands are not a resource: a job with almost no workload can simply be understimulating. Leadership is the classic tricky case. Transformational leadership acts as a resource, abusive supervision (humiliating people in public) acts as a demand, and a meta-analysis (Pletzer et al., 2024) found that each has its own unique effects. They are two different behaviours, not two ends of one scale.

Are all demands the same? The challenge–hindrance framework splits them. Challenge demands (complexity, time pressure, responsibility) offer growth; hindrance demands (role ambiguity, administrative hassles, interpersonal conflict) block goals. Both cost energy and can cause strain when too high or chronic, but only hindrances undermine engagement; challenges can add positive effects. The split is subjective: nurses saw work pressure as a hindrance but emotional demands as a challenge (Bakker & Sanz-Vergel, 2013). Usually demands and resources are negatively related (busy people have no time to collect feedback or support), although managers often have high demands and high resources. Is engagement redundant? No. Three meta-analyses found correlations of only −.37 (Crawford et al., 2010), −.25 (Nahrgang et al., 2011) and −.36 (Lesener et al., 2019) between engagement and burnout. They also have different predictors, and engagement predicts performance better.

So JD-R needs two separate outcome measures, not one scale from "burned out" to "engaged". Someone low on both is not healthy and happy: they may be indifferent, the attitude behind "quiet quitting". For an intervention this matters: removing a hindrance can reduce burnout without creating engagement. Next we add time: what happens when these processes repeat week after week?

⚠ The trap✗ An employee who gets little feedback has a high job demand.✓ A low score on a job resource is not a job demand. Little feedback lowers motivation and engagement, but it does not drive the health impairment process the way a demand does.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors conclude that work engagement is not simply the opposite of burnout? Give two arguments.

Model answer: (1) Meta-analytic correlations between engagement and burnout are only moderate (about −.25 to −.37), so they share limited variance. (2) They have unique predictors (engagement: abundant resources plus challenge demands; burnout: high demands plus lack of resources) and unique outcomes (engagement predicts performance better). Also, people can be low on both (indifference).

STEP8
FAQ: do loss and gain spirals really exist? · Learning goal 5

Snowballs, and why they are hard to prove

❓ This step answers: How can employees end up in a loss or gain spiral, and how strong is the evidence? (Learning goal 5)
🔁 Run the cycle. Pick a starting point and press "One more round" to see what a spiral means.

So far everything happened at one moment. But work goes on week after week, and JD-R says the processes feed back on themselves. This is learning goal 5, and it is also the FAQ where the authors are most self-critical.

A spiral is a self-reinforcing loop. In the loss cycle, demands cause strain, strained people start self-undermining, and that creates even more demands. In the gain spiral, resources spark engagement, engaged people craft their jobs, and that creates even more resources. A true spiral needs two things (Salanova et al., 2010): the variables must influence each other in both directions (reciprocal relations), and their absolute levels must keep going down (loss) or up (gain) over time. A loop that just stays at the same level is not a spiral. Press "One more round" a few times: the bars keep moving in one direction.

Here are the studies the authors cite. General practitioners who distanced themselves from patients (depersonalization) faced more demanding patients five years later, even after controlling for their earlier patient demands (Bakker et al., 2000). Financial consultants' burnout predicted their burnout two years later through more demands and fewer resources (ten Brummelhuis et al., 2011). Over five working weeks, weekly demands led to self-undermining through burnout symptoms, more strongly for people already high on chronic burnout (Bakker, Xanthopoulou, & Demerouti, 2023). For gain, Hakanen et al. (2008) found reciprocal links between job resources, engagement and personal initiative. But a study showing that positive affect predicts broad-minded coping (Fredrickson & Joiner, 2018) is not spiral evidence, because levels did not necessarily rise.

The authors' honest verdict: the idea is "appealing and intuitive", but there is "relatively little empirical support". Reciprocal effects are challenging to test in terms of research design (floor and ceiling effects, for example), and colleagues passing stress or energy to each other (contagion) can create the same statistics without a spiral. For your tutorial: a student who skips sleep to meet deadlines, makes mistakes and then has to redo work is a loss cycle in miniature. Next we look at the remaining questions: limits and reach.

⚠ The trap✗ Any reciprocal relationship between engagement and job resources proves a gain spiral.✓ A spiral also needs the absolute levels to keep rising (or falling) over time. Reciprocity alone is not enough, and the authors call the evidence for spirals relatively limited.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the loss cycle in JD-R theory and name the behaviour that links strain back to job demands.

Model answer: Job demands lead to job strain (exhaustion); strained employees show self-undermining behaviour (careless mistakes, poor communication, conflicts), which in turn increases future job demands. The loop is stronger for people already high on chronic burnout.

STEP9
The remaining FAQs · limits, people, culture, other domains

Falsifiable, personal, measurable, universal?

❓ This step answers: Can JD-R be proven wrong, how do personality and culture come in, and does it work outside the office?

We have covered the core of the theory and its biggest questions. The last FAQs test the theory's limits: can it be proven wrong, where do people's personalities come in, can it be measured with one questionnaire, and does it hold everywhere?

Can JD-R be falsified? Critics say its predictions are so obvious that studies will always confirm them. The authors reply with Ajzen (2020): "there is nothing wrong with a theory that is intuitively reasonable". They also point out that JD-R changed when evidence demanded it (reciprocal findings led to the self-undermining and spiral propositions), and they list nine less obvious, testable predictions, for example that exhaustion carries the effect of demands on performance, and that job crafting carries the effect of engagement on resources. Sound research that disconfirms these would falsify the theory. Personality, in the multilevel version of JD-R, works as a stable moderator of daily processes: people high in emotional stability react less negatively to momentary demands (Debusscher et al., 2016), and open people get more engagement and creativity out of playful work design (Scharp et al., 2019).

Within or between? A meta-analysis (Downes et al., 2021) found similar effects within people (your good versus bad days) and between people (you versus your colleague), but challenge demands caused more strain when they fluctuated a lot. A standard questionnaire? Common demands and resources can be measured with standard tools, but standardizing risks missing what is specific to a job. The authors recommend two stages: open interviews first, then selecting or building the questionnaires. Culture? Only 4 of 24 cultural moderation tests (17%) were significant, so effects are largely similar across cultures (see the figure). Outside work? Yes: at home (the work–home resources model), in sport and in education. Athlete students with more burnout reported more demands (school stress, poor recovery, disempowering coaching), those with less burnout more resources such as support and empowering coaching (Sorkkila et al., 2020).

That last point is your bridge to learning goals 6 and 7: Study Demands–Resources theory applies the same logic to students, and teachers act as a key resource. The overall picture: the authors defend JD-R as flexible, testable and broadly supported, while admitting open questions (spirals, personality as a perception filter, culture and proactive behaviour). Last step: the article in one minute.

⚠ The trap✗ JD-R cannot be falsified because its predictions are obvious.✓ The authors argue that intuitive is not the same as untestable: JD-R makes specific mediation and moderation predictions that sound research could disconfirm, and the theory has been revised on the basis of findings.

STEP10
Conclusion and main take-aways

The article in one minute

❓ This step answers: What should you remember from the whole article?

We have walked through the whole article: who wrote it and why, the seven propositions, the buffer and boost, the evidence and its traps, the link with COR, the boundaries between concepts, the spirals and the limits. Here is how it fits together.

JD-R explains well-being and performance with two ingredients (demands and resources) and two processes (health impairment and motivation) that interact and, over time, feed back on themselves through self-undermining and job crafting. The authors conclude that four features explain its success: it is flexible enough for any job and country, it predicts both burnout and engagement, it spells out testable links between job design, well-being, behaviour and performance, and meta-analyses support it. They also admit where the evidence is thin: spirals, the direct effect of resources on strain, and the role of culture and personality.

"JD-R theory is commonly used to predict employee well-being, behaviors, and performance and has received considerable empirical support in various meta-analyses."

💬 SAY IT TO A FRIEND"Every job has things that cost you energy and things that give you motivation. Too many demands and too few resources, and you slowly burn out. Lots of resources, and you get engaged, even when the work is hard, because resources soften the pressure and challenges make the resources count. And it snowballs: drained people make mistakes that make work harder, engaged people shape their job to make it better."
🔑 MEMORY HOOKTwo ingredients, two roads, two crossings, two loops: demands and resources; drain and drive; buffer and boost; loss and gain.
✎ TIPIn a case question, first label every job feature as a demand (challenge or hindrance) or a resource. Then ask which process it starts, and whether a buffer or boost is at play. Watch out for "low resource" options pretending to be demands.

💭 Think of it this way

Your energy at work is like a phone battery and its charger. Demands are the apps that drain the battery; resources are the charger and the reasons you want to use the phone at all. A busy day with a charger nearby is fine, even exciting. A busy day without one empties you. And people with a charger use their energy to find more chargers, while people with an empty battery stop looking.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Explain why "a low score on job resources is not the same as a high score on job demands".

Demands cost effort and drive the health impairment process (strain). Resources spark motivation and drive the motivational process. Missing resources lower engagement and intrinsic motivation, but they do not themselves cost effort the way demands do. So low support is a lack of a resource, not a demand.

2. A study finds that resources predict lower exhaustion directly. Give two reasons from the article why this might not mean resources act on the health impairment process.

Any two of: the indirect path through engagement was not modelled; burnout was measured with motivational components (cynicism, reduced accomplishment); people with more resources face (or perceive) fewer demands; the demands × resources interaction was not included or was masked by restricted variance in demands (Figure 1).

3. What exactly does JD-R borrow from COR theory?

The gain paradox principle (relevant for the buffer hypothesis and the direct basis of the boost hypothesis) and the idea of loss and gain spirals, which JD-R models explicitly through self-undermining and job crafting.

⚠️ TrapA demand is not automatically bad. Challenging demands (a complex task, responsibility) can boost engagement when resources are present. Hindering demands (red tape, role conflict) mostly drain.
💬 Tutorial questionWhich of your resources would most effectively buffer your biggest demand, and why?

A good answer names a specific demand, picks a resource that matches it (for example, social support for emotional demands, or autonomy over timing for time pressure), and uses the buffer idea: resources reduce the impact of demands on strain because they give the motivation to deal with them.
