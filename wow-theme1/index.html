Meeting 1 · Job demands and resources · prepared for Sezen

Why do some people burn out at work, while others thrive?

Two colleagues can have the same job, and one ends up exhausted while the other comes alive. This theme gives you the most widely used theory to explain why, and to know what leaders, teams and employees themselves can do about it.

The story of this theme · 8 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Every job has two kinds of ingredients. Some take energy: workload, time pressure, difficult clients. These are job demands. Others give energy and motivation: support from colleagues, feedback, autonomy, learning. These are job resources. When demands keep outweighing resources, people slowly drain until they burn out. When resources are rich, people become engaged, even when the work is hard. And it snowballs: drained people make choices that make their work worse, while engaged people shape their job so it gets better.

Before we look at a single idea, it helps to know who is talking. This article is written by the two people who created the theory. That makes it the most authoritative source on what JD-R says, and also a source you should read with a critical eye, because authors naturally defend their own theory.

Arnold Bakker (Erasmus University Rotterdam) and Evangelia Demerouti (Eindhoven University of Technology) introduced the job demands–resources (JD-R) model in 2001 (Demerouti et al., 2001). Over twenty years it grew from a model into a full theory and became one of the most-cited theories in organizational psychology. Managers, consultants and HR professionals use it for risk checks, workshops, apps and interventions.

The article is built like a long FAQ page. Part 1 sums up the theory in seven propositions. Part 2 answers 13 questions that researchers and practitioners kept asking them, such as "Can a resource also be a demand?" or "Do loss and gain spirals really exist?". Part 3 concludes with four features that make the theory useful. There is no new experiment: every answer is built on earlier studies and meta-analyses.

This matters for the exam. A sentence such as "Bakker and Demerouti (2024) found..." is almost always wrong. They argue, propose and cite studies by others. Next we look at the problem that made them write an FAQ in the first place.

STEP2
What problem does the article try to solve?

A theory that looks simple, but raises recurring questions

❓ This step answers: Why did the authors write an FAQ about their own theory?

We know who wrote the article. Now the obvious question: why would the founders of a successful theory write an FAQ about it? Usually you only write an FAQ when the same questions keep coming in.

That is exactly the problem. JD-R looks simple: some things at work drain you (job demands), other things motivate you (job resources). Because it looks so simple, people apply it quickly, and run into questions along the way. The authors write that "the theory seems simple", but that several issues "need clarification" so that it is applied in the way that "makes the best predictions". Think of a recipe that only says "add salt and heat": everyone thinks they can cook it, and everyone cooks something different.

Scholars and practitioners sometimes turned to Bakker and Demerouti for help when they applied the theory. The 13 questions in the article are the ones they heard most. They cover what the concepts mean, what the evidence shows, how people and time come in, and how far the theory reaches. The article is inspired by an earlier FAQ by Ajzen (2020) about the theory of planned behavior.

So the article asks one overall question: how should JD-R be understood and used so that it predicts well-being and performance as well as possible? To answer it, we first need the theory itself. That is the next step: the seven propositions.

⚠ The trap✗ The article was written because JD-R had been disproven.✓ It was written because the theory is widely used and supported, but users keep running into the same questions. The FAQ clarifies, defends and extends it.
📝 Exam-style questionAn HR consultant says: "JD-R is common sense: stress is bad, support is good. You don't need a theory for that." Which issue raised in the FAQ does this remark touch on, and how do the authors respond?

Model answer: The question whether JD-R can be falsified. Its propositions seem self-evident, but the authors argue that an intuitively reasonable theory is not a problem (Ajzen, 2020), and that JD-R also makes less obvious, testable predictions (mediation and moderation) that could be disconfirmed.

STEP3
Key concepts and definitions · Learning goals 1 and 2

Seven propositions, one model

❓ This step answers: What are job demands and job resources, and what does JD-R claim about them? (Learning goals 1 and 2)
👆 Tap a numbered badge on the model to read that proposition in plain words.
Tap a numbered badge on the model to see that proposition.

We know why the article exists. Now we need the theory itself, because every one of the 13 questions refers back to it. The authors summarize JD-R in seven propositions. Think of them as the seven rules of the game.

Rule 1 is about the ingredients, and it is what makes JD-R different from older job design theories such as job characteristics theory (Hackman & Oldham, 1976): JD-R is flexible. It has no fixed list. Every job has its own job demands, aspects that require effort and therefore cost something physically (heart rate) or psychologically (fatigue, anxiety). And every job has its own job resources: physical, psychological, social or organizational aspects that help you reach work goals, soften the impact of demands, and help you learn and grow, such as constructive feedback, skill variety and support from colleagues.

Rule 2 says the two ingredients start two different processes. In the health impairment process, demands use up your physical, emotional and cognitive energy; over a long time short-term fatigue becomes chronic and health problems can follow. In the motivational process, resources satisfy basic needs (to belong, to do meaningful work, to act autonomously) and spark work engagement, which leads to better performance. Rule 3 adds two interactions, the buffer and the boost (next step). Rules 4 and 5 bring in personal resources, such as optimism and self-efficacy: they go hand in hand with job resources and help people deal with demands. In a weekly diary study among nurses (Bakker & Sanz-Vergel, 2013), emotional demands led to more engagement when self-efficacy and optimism were high, and to less when they were low.

Rules 6 and 7 make the employee an actor instead of a passive receiver. Rule 6: people proactively change their own demands and resources, called job crafting, which raises person-job fit, meaningfulness and engagement. Rule 7: under high demands and strain, people slide into self-undermining (careless mistakes, poor communication, conflicts), which creates new obstacles. Together, rules 6 and 7 are the seeds of the gain and loss spirals you will meet in step 8. Next: how demands and resources combine.

⚠ The trap✗ JD-R lists the job characteristics that matter for every job: workload, autonomy, support and feedback.✓ JD-R is flexible: each job has its own demands and resources. A set such as workload, autonomy and support is common in most jobs, but the theory does not fix the list.
📝 Exam-style questionAccording to JD-R theory, through which process do job resources mainly affect job performance?

Model answer: The motivational process: job resources foster work engagement (by satisfying basic psychological needs and intrinsic motivation), and engagement leads to better performance.

STEP4
Key concepts · Learning goal 2

Resources soften demands, and challenges make resources count

❓ This step answers: How do demands and resources work together, and why do some people thrive under high demands? (Learning goal 2)
👆 Switch between the two graphs to compare the buffer and the boost hypothesis.

We now know the two processes. But they don't run side by side without touching. Proposition 3 says demands and resources have a combined effect on well-being. This is the answer to the opening puzzle: two people with the same high demands can end up in very different places.

The buffer hypothesis says job resources weaken the effect of job demands on strain. Picture two umbrellas in the same storm: the rain (demands) is identical, but one umbrella (resources) is much bigger. Resources may change how the demand is perceived, soften the reaction to it, or limit the damage afterwards. The boost hypothesis is the mirror image: challenging demands make resources more motivating. Feedback and support matter most on the days you face complex work or time pressure. In short, resources become most important for engagement when demands are high.

The evidence comes from diary studies, where the same people report on many days. Tadić et al. (2015) found that teachers were more inspired and engaged on days with hard, complex work, but only when they also had daily resources such as feedback and support (boost). On days with hindrances such as role conflict, they were less engaged, especially when resources were scarce (buffer). Breevaart and Bakker (2018) found the same pattern with leadership: challenge demands went with the most engagement when the leader was transformational (inspiring, considerate, stimulating), and hindrances hurt most when the leader showed little of it.

So "thriving" in JD-R is not the absence of demands. It is high (challenge) demands met by high resources. Anna, the exhausted nurse from the story, had high demands and few resources; Sam had the same demands and many resources. Next we ask how the authors know all this: what kind of evidence stands behind the theory?

⚠ The trap✗ The boost hypothesis says job demands boost burnout.✓ The boost hypothesis says challenging demands strengthen the positive effect of job resources on work engagement. It is about motivation, not strain.
📝 Exam-style questionOn days with a heavy workload, a project manager who gets a lot of support from her team feels hardly more exhausted than on quiet days. Her colleague without support feels much more exhausted. Which JD-R hypothesis does this pattern illustrate?

Model answer: The buffer hypothesis: job resources (social support) weaken the impact of job demands (workload) on strain (exhaustion).

STEP5
Research method · the evidence behind the answers

Meta-analyses, diaries, and a hidden interaction

❓ This step answers: What kind of evidence do the authors use, and why can a study miss the buffer effect?
🔬 Be the researcher: drag the slider to decide which employees end up in your sample. Watch what you would conclude.

We have seen what JD-R claims. Since this article runs no study of its own, the real question is: what evidence stands behind the answers? Three kinds come back again and again, and one figure shows how evidence can fool you.

First, meta-analyses: studies that pool the results of many earlier studies. Crawford et al. (2010) combined 43 studies with almost 27,000 employees; Lesener et al. (2019) combined 57 longitudinal samples. Second, diary studies that follow the same people day by day or week by week, such as the teacher and nurse studies in step 4. They show within-person effects: what happens to you on your worse days. Third, longitudinal studies that measure people at Time 1 and again months or years later, which are needed to test spirals.

Now the trick in Figure 1. Several meta-analyses found that job resources are directly linked to less burnout. JD-R predicts this link only indirectly, through engagement. The authors give four reasons why earlier studies may have tested this badly. One is in the slider game: if almost everyone in your sample has high demands, you never see the low-demand side of the graph. Then the two lines simply look like "more resources, less exhaustion", a main effect, and the interaction (the buffer) is masked. The same happens if the interaction term is left out of the analysis. The other reasons: the indirect path through engagement was not modelled; burnout was measured with motivation items (cynicism, reduced accomplishment); and people with many resources may simply face fewer demands.

So a direct link between resources and strain is not proof that resources work like demands. Still, the authors admit it "cannot be ruled out": energetic colleagues may pass on energy, and resources may build personal resources linked to good health. Exam lesson: always ask what the sample and the analysis could see. Next we look at the theory JD-R grew out of: COR theory.

⚠ The trap✗ Figure 1 shows that job resources have no buffer effect.✓ Figure 1 shows how a buffer effect can be hidden: when only high demands are observed, the interaction looks like a main effect of resources on exhaustion.
📝 Exam-style questionA study among emergency nurses, who all report very high workload, finds that social support is related to lower exhaustion, but finds no support × workload interaction. What explanation from the article could account for this?

Model answer: Restricted variance in job demands: because nearly all nurses have high demands, really low demands are not observed and the demands × resources interaction is masked, so it shows up as a main effect of resources on exhaustion.

STEP6
FAQ 1 · Learning goal 3

COR is the parent, JD-R the specialist

❓ This step answers: What is the difference between COR theory and JD-R theory, and what did JD-R borrow? (Learning goal 3)

The first question in the FAQ is the one your learning goal 3 asks: how is JD-R related to conservation of resources (COR) theory? JD-R "stands on the shoulders of giants": classic job design theories such as Herzberg's two-factor theory, job characteristics theory and Karasek's demand-control model. But its closest relative in the reading list is COR.

COR (Hobfoll, 1989) is a general stress theory. Its central idea is that people need resources to survive, so they try to obtain, retain, foster and protect them. "Resources" is meant broadly: objects (a car), conditions (a marriage, a job), skills and beliefs (self-efficacy), and volatile resources such as energy, attention and time. COR is basically about what people do when they anticipate losing or actually lose resources, and people must invest resources to protect against loss, recover from loss and gain new ones. JD-R is narrower and more precise: it is an organizational theory that says which resources, namely job resources, are needed to deal with job demands and protect well-being. Think of COR as a general map of your whole life's "battery", and JD-R as the detailed map of your workplace.

JD-R borrows three things. COR's gain paradox principle (gains become more important when you are losing) is relevant for the buffer hypothesis and is the direct basis of the boost hypothesis. JD-R's loss and gain spirals also come from COR, but JD-R spells them out concretely: demands → strain → self-undermining → more demands, and resources → engagement → job crafting → more resources. COR adds a paradox JD-R must face: on high-demand days you need to actively seek new resources, exactly when you have the least energy to do so.

So the difference is scope and focus, not opposition. COR is broad, about people and all the resources they value in any life domain. JD-R is specific: it names the job demands and job resources that matter for burnout, engagement and performance. You will meet the full comparison in the next reading, Demerouti (2025). Next we look at the questions about where one concept ends and another begins.

⚠ The trap✗ JD-R and COR are rival theories that make opposite predictions.✓ JD-R builds on COR. COR is a broad stress theory about all valued resources; JD-R specifies which job characteristics matter and borrows COR's gain paradox and spirals.
📝 Exam-style questionWhich principle of COR theory forms the direct basis for the boost hypothesis in JD-R theory?

Model answer: The gain paradox principle: resource gain becomes more salient (important) in the context of resource loss. Applied to work, job resources matter most on days with high (challenging) job demands.

STEP7
FAQs on the concepts · Learning goals 1 and 2

Where one concept ends and the next begins

❓ This step answers: Can a resource be a demand? Are all demands bad? And is work engagement just the opposite of burnout?

A theory with two ingredients and two outcomes only works if you can tell them apart. Three of the FAQs are about exactly that: where does a resource end and a demand begin, are all demands the same, and is engagement just "no burnout"?

Can a resource be a demand? The authors' answer is a clear no. Demands cost energy and drive the health impairment process; resources don't create energy but spark motivation. Crucially, a low score on a resource is not a high score on a demand: little support makes you less engaged, but it is not itself a demand. And low demands are not a resource: a job with almost no workload can simply be understimulating. Leadership is the classic tricky case. Transformational leadership acts as a resource, abusive supervision (humiliating people in public) acts as a demand, and a meta-analysis (Pletzer et al., 2024) found that each has its own unique effects. They are two different behaviours, not two ends of one scale.

Are all demands the same? The challenge–hindrance framework splits them. Challenge demands (complexity, time pressure, responsibility) offer growth; hindrance demands (role ambiguity, administrative hassles, interpersonal conflict) block goals. Both cost energy and can cause strain when too high or chronic, but only hindrances undermine engagement; challenges can add positive effects. The split is subjective: nurses saw work pressure as a hindrance but emotional demands as a challenge (Bakker & Sanz-Vergel, 2013). Usually demands and resources are negatively related (busy people have no time to collect feedback or support), although managers often have high demands and high resources. Is engagement redundant? No. Three meta-analyses found correlations of only −.37 (Crawford et al., 2010), −.25 (Nahrgang et al., 2011) and −.36 (Lesener et al., 2019) between engagement and burnout. They also have different predictors, and engagement predicts performance better.

So JD-R needs two separate outcome measures, not one scale from "burned out" to "engaged". Someone low on both is not healthy and happy: they may be indifferent, the attitude behind "quiet quitting". For an intervention this matters: removing a hindrance can reduce burnout without creating engagement. Next we add time: what happens when these processes repeat week after week?

⚠ The trap✗ An employee who gets little feedback has a high job demand.✓ A low score on a job resource is not a job demand. Little feedback lowers motivation and engagement, but it does not drive the health impairment process the way a demand does.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors conclude that work engagement is not simply the opposite of burnout? Give two arguments.

Model answer: (1) Meta-analytic correlations between engagement and burnout are only moderate (about −.25 to −.37), so they share limited variance. (2) They have unique predictors (engagement: abundant resources plus challenge demands; burnout: high demands plus lack of resources) and unique outcomes (engagement predicts performance better). Also, people can be low on both (indifference).

STEP8
FAQ: do loss and gain spirals really exist? · Learning goal 5

Snowballs, and why they are hard to prove

❓ This step answers: How can employees end up in a loss or gain spiral, and how strong is the evidence? (Learning goal 5)
🔁 Run the cycle. Pick a starting point and press "One more round" to see what a spiral means.

So far everything happened at one moment. But work goes on week after week, and JD-R says the processes feed back on themselves. This is learning goal 5, and it is also the FAQ where the authors are most self-critical.

A spiral is a self-reinforcing loop. In the loss cycle, demands cause strain, strained people start self-undermining, and that creates even more demands. In the gain spiral, resources spark engagement, engaged people craft their jobs, and that creates even more resources. A true spiral needs two things (Salanova et al., 2010): the variables must influence each other in both directions (reciprocal relations), and their absolute levels must keep going down (loss) or up (gain) over time. A loop that just stays at the same level is not a spiral. Press "One more round" a few times: the bars keep moving in one direction.

Here are the studies the authors cite. General practitioners who distanced themselves from patients (depersonalization) faced more demanding patients five years later, even after controlling for their earlier patient demands (Bakker et al., 2000). Financial consultants' burnout predicted their burnout two years later through more demands and fewer resources (ten Brummelhuis et al., 2011). Over five working weeks, weekly demands led to self-undermining through burnout symptoms, more strongly for people already high on chronic burnout (Bakker, Xanthopoulou, & Demerouti, 2023). For gain, Hakanen et al. (2008) found reciprocal links between job resources, engagement and personal initiative. But a study showing that positive affect predicts broad-minded coping (Fredrickson & Joiner, 2018) is not spiral evidence, because levels did not necessarily rise.

The authors' honest verdict: the idea is "appealing and intuitive", but there is "relatively little empirical support". Reciprocal effects are challenging to test in terms of research design (floor and ceiling effects, for example), and colleagues passing stress or energy to each other (contagion) can create the same statistics without a spiral. For your tutorial: a student who skips sleep to meet deadlines, makes mistakes and then has to redo work is a loss cycle in miniature. Next we look at the remaining questions: limits and reach.

⚠ The trap✗ Any reciprocal relationship between engagement and job resources proves a gain spiral.✓ A spiral also needs the absolute levels to keep rising (or falling) over time. Reciprocity alone is not enough, and the authors call the evidence for spirals relatively limited.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the loss cycle in JD-R theory and name the behaviour that links strain back to job demands.

Model answer: Job demands lead to job strain (exhaustion); strained employees show self-undermining behaviour (careless mistakes, poor communication, conflicts), which in turn increases future job demands. The loop is stronger for people already high on chronic burnout.

STEP9
The remaining FAQs · limits, people, culture, other domains

Falsifiable, personal, measurable, universal?

❓ This step answers: Can JD-R be proven wrong, how do personality and culture come in, and does it work outside the office?

We have covered the core of the theory and its biggest questions. The last FAQs test the theory's limits: can it be proven wrong, where do people's personalities come in, can it be measured with one questionnaire, and does it hold everywhere?

Can JD-R be falsified? Critics say its predictions are so obvious that studies will always confirm them. The authors reply with Ajzen (2020): "there is nothing wrong with a theory that is intuitively reasonable". They also point out that JD-R changed when evidence demanded it (reciprocal findings led to the self-undermining and spiral propositions), and they list nine less obvious, testable predictions, for example that exhaustion carries the effect of demands on performance, and that job crafting carries the effect of engagement on resources. Sound research that disconfirms these would falsify the theory. Personality, in the multilevel version of JD-R, works as a stable moderator of daily processes: people high in emotional stability react less negatively to momentary demands (Debusscher et al., 2016), and open people get more engagement and creativity out of playful work design (Scharp et al., 2019).

Within or between? A meta-analysis (Downes et al., 2021) found similar effects within people (your good versus bad days) and between people (you versus your colleague), but challenge demands caused more strain when they fluctuated a lot. A standard questionnaire? Common demands and resources can be measured with standard tools, but standardizing risks missing what is specific to a job. The authors recommend two stages: open interviews first, then selecting or building the questionnaires. Culture? Only 4 of 24 cultural moderation tests (17%) were significant, so effects are largely similar across cultures (see the figure). Outside work? Yes: at home (the work–home resources model), in sport and in education. Athlete students with more burnout reported more demands (school stress, poor recovery, disempowering coaching), those with less burnout more resources such as support and empowering coaching (Sorkkila et al., 2020).

That last point is your bridge to learning goals 6 and 7: Study Demands–Resources theory applies the same logic to students, and teachers act as a key resource. The overall picture: the authors defend JD-R as flexible, testable and broadly supported, while admitting open questions (spirals, personality as a perception filter, culture and proactive behaviour). STEP10
Conclusion and main take-aways

The article in one minute

❓ This step answers: What should you remember from the whole article?

We have walked through the whole article: who wrote it and why, the seven propositions, the buffer and boost, the evidence and its traps, the link with COR, the boundaries between concepts, the spirals and the limits. Here is how it fits together.

JD-R explains well-being and performance with two ingredients (demands and resources) and two processes (health impairment and motivation) that interact and, over time, feed back on themselves through self-undermining and job crafting. The authors conclude that four features explain its success: it is flexible enough for any job and country, it predicts both burnout and engagement, it spells out testable links between job design, well-being, behaviour and performance, and meta-analyses support it. They also admit where the evidence is thin: spirals, the direct effect of resources on strain, and the role of culture and personality.

"JD-R theory is commonly used to predict employee well-being, behaviors, and performance and has received considerable empirical support in various meta-analyses."

💭 Think of it this way

Your energy at work is like a phone battery and its charger. Demands are the apps that drain the battery; resources are the charger and the reasons you want to use the phone at all. A busy day with a charger nearby is fine, even exciting. A busy day without one empties you. And people with a charger use their energy to find more chargers, while people with an empty battery stop looking.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Explain why "a low score on job resources is not the same as a high score on job demands".

Demands cost effort and drive the health impairment process (strain). Resources spark motivation and drive the motivational process. Missing resources lower engagement and intrinsic motivation, but they do not themselves cost effort the way demands do. So low support is a lack of a resource, not a demand.

2. A study finds that resources predict lower exhaustion directly. Give two reasons from the article why this might not mean resources act on the health impairment process.

Any two of: the indirect path through engagement was not modelled; burnout was measured with motivational components (cynicism, reduced accomplishment); people with more resources face (or perceive) fewer demands; the demands × resources interaction was not included or was masked by restricted variance in demands (Figure 1).

3. What exactly does JD-R borrow from COR theory?

The gain paradox principle (relevant for the buffer hypothesis and the direct basis of the boost hypothesis) and the idea of loss and gain spirals, which JD-R models explicitly through self-undermining and job crafting.

In the previous reading, the very first FAQ asked how JD-R relates to COR theory, and got a one-page answer. This article is the full answer. So the first thing to know is who is answering.

Evangelia Demerouti, of Eindhoven University of Technology, is the first author of the papers that introduced JD-R theory (Demerouti et al., 2000, 2001). Here she writes on her own, for a business journal rather than a psychology journal. That tells you the audience: researchers and practitioners who design jobs and organizations, and who need to know which of the two theories to use and what they predict for the jobs of the future.

The article is a conceptual paper. It runs no study. It sums up the core of COR theory and of JD-R, lists eight similarities and a table of differences, digs into what we still don't know about demands and resources, and then looks ahead: how technology will change jobs, what researchers should study next and what organizations should do.

Two things to keep in mind while you read. First, the author is the first author of the original JD-R papers, so watch how she judges the other theory. Second, many points repeat the FAQ, which tells you they are core. The next step looks at why the comparison is needed at all.

STEP2
What question does the article ask?

Two good theories, still open questions, and jobs that are changing fast

❓ This step answers: Why compare COR and JD-R now, and what does the paper want to achieve?

We know who wrote it. Now the question: if both theories are well established, why compare them? Because work itself is changing, and the theories have to keep up.

Demerouti starts from an everyday observation: our work requires effort and often strains our energy, and organizations try to help people do their job without harming their well-being. COR and JD-R are both "crucial frameworks" for understanding this. But jobs are changing, with more uncertainty, more complexity, remote work, the gig economy and new technology. To give good advice about such jobs, you need to know what each theory can and cannot predict.

She also names gaps that neither theory has closed. For example: are the same job resources equally important for raising engagement and for preventing burnout? And which job resources matter most for well-being and performance? By looking at both theories together, researchers may be able to refine them and make more specific predictions.

So the paper's aim is to use the propositions of COR and JD-R to discuss how job demands and job resources matter for the design of future jobs, and to raise unresolved issues. To do that, we first need COR theory itself, in more detail than the FAQ gave us. That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ The paper argues that COR should replace JD-R in job design.✓ It treats both as complementary, compares them, and uses both to predict future job design. The author even stresses that JD-R is not a subset of COR.

STEP3
Key concepts · COR theory · Learning goal 3

Four principles and three corollaries: life as a resource bank

❓ This step answers: What exactly does COR theory claim? (Learning goal 3)
👆 Tap a principle to see the author's own everyday example.
Tap a principle to see the example the author gives.

The comparison only works if you know COR on its own terms. In the FAQ it got one paragraph. Here Demerouti lays it out as a set of principles and corollaries, each with an everyday example.

In COR (Hobfoll, 1989), resources are objects, states and conditions that people value. They come in three kinds: personal (skills, self-esteem), social (support, good relationships) and structural (money, facilities). The basic premise: people try to acquire, maintain, nurture and protect them. Think of a bank account you guard carefully. Four principles follow. (1) Losing resources hurts more than gaining them helps, so people first try to prevent loss. (2) You must invest resources to protect against loss, recover and gain more, like spending effort on new skills to stay employable. (3) The gain paradox: gains matter most when you are losing, like time feeling most precious as a deadline nears. (4) When resources run low, people become defensive, aggressive or irrational, like an overworked colleague who snaps.

Hobfoll (2011) added that resources travel together in resource caravans, and that environments act as caravan passageways: supportive leadership, a positive culture and a manageable workload let resources grow, while a toxic workplace drains them. Three corollaries describe the spirals: people with more resources are less vulnerable to loss and better at gaining (1); because loss weighs more, losses snowball into loss spirals (2); gains also spiral, but more slowly and weakly (3). A six-year study with seven measurements (Jiang et al., 2023) tested such reciprocal links: self-esteem and sense of belonging went with less job insecurity later, but job insecurity did not change them in return; perceived social support also predicted lower job insecurity.

The author's verdict is critical: COR explains a lot, but it needs to be more specific in how its principles can be operationalized (how do you measure a "resource caravan"?), tested (how long do you wait to see a gain spiral?) and falsified (what result would show something is not a resource gain?). Keep that criticism in mind: it comes back as a key difference with JD-R in step 5. Next, what the two theories share.

⚠ The trap✗ According to COR, gaining a resource has the same impact as losing one.✓ Principle 1: loss is more distressing than gain. That is also why loss spirals are stronger and faster than gain spirals (corollaries 2 and 3).
📝 Exam-style questionA junior analyst who has lost her mentor and her flexible schedule in the same month starts snapping at colleagues in meetings. Which COR principle describes her behaviour?

Model answer: Principle 4: when resources are depleted or strained, people may become defensive, aggressive or irrational. The irritable reaction is a coping response to resource scarcity.

STEP4
Similarities · Learning goal 3

Eight things the two theories agree on

❓ This step answers: What do COR and JD-R have in common?

We now know COR in detail and JD-R from the previous reading. Before the differences, Demerouti lists what they share. This matters because an exam question can ask for similarities as well as differences.

First and second, both put resources at the centre and both explain stress: in COR, stress follows a threat of loss, actual loss, or no gain after investing; in JD-R, impaired health follows when demands outweigh resources. Third, in both theories resources buffer: COR says people with more resources recover better from setbacks, JD-R says job resources soften the harm of job demands. Fourth, both are dynamic: resources change over time, from days to at least 13 years in JD-R studies. Fifth, both say people can optimize resources, although COR does not say how; JD-R names job crafting.

The sixth similarity is easy to miss and worth knowing. Both theories stress the objective side of stress: real events (COR) and real demands and resources (JD-R), not only how people perceive them. Why? You cannot predict in advance what someone will find stressful, and if you only look at perceptions, you shift the blame onto the person instead of the environment. So both favour interventions that change the situation. Seventh, both predict that resources are related over time in spirals, with JD-R more specific about how. Eighth, both have practical implications: COR says provide enough resources to prevent loss; JD-R says optimize job design by reducing harmful demands and adding resources.

So the two theories are family: same core ingredient, same worry about stress, same belief that things snowball. But family members still differ in personality. The next step is Table 1, the differences, which is the heart of learning goal 3.

⚠ The trap✗ Both theories say that stress is only in the eye of the beholder, so interventions should change how employees appraise their work.✓ Both stress the objective nature of events, demands and resources. Focusing only on appraisals risks blaming the victim, so both favour changing the environment.

Differences · Table 1 · Learning goal 3

COR guards the person's bank, JD-R redesigns the job

❓ This step answers: What is the difference between COR theory and JD-R theory? (Learning goal 3)

We have seen what the theories share. Now the question your learning goal asks directly: how do they differ? The author's one-line summary is that COR focuses on preserving resources and personal factors, while JD-R looks at how the external work environment, through demands and resources, affects well-being and performance.

Table 1 breaks this into eight rows. COR is about the individual's effort to keep and build resources; JD-R about the role of job characteristics. COR's scope is broader: it is used for natural disasters, political conflict and health, while JD-R starts from work and adds the work–family link. In COR, stress comes from (threatened) resource loss; in JD-R, burnout comes from high demands plus low resources, "not from resource loss per se". COR works at the individual level; JD-R at both individual and organizational levels, with roles for leaders, teams and family. COR defines resources as anything people value (so broad that critics say nearly anything good counts); JD-R defines them by their function: reaching goals, coping with demands, growing.

Two rows are the most important. "Perhaps the most significant difference" is the relevance of demands: in COR, job demands are "virtually absent", they are just resources you have to spend; in JD-R, demands are one of the two main ingredients, starting the health impairment process and boosting the value of resources. And specificity: COR's abstract principles make specific hypotheses hard to formulate and the theory hard to test and falsify, while JD-R tells researchers what predicts what and in which direction. Finally, on behaviour, COR says people self-regulate and become "strategically defensive" without saying how; JD-R names job crafting (constructive) and self-undermining (destructive).

The author ends with a clear claim: JD-R "should not be construed as a subset of the COR theory". It adds elements COR lacks: job demands, feedback loops, specific mediators (burnout, engagement), moderators (personal resources) and the active role of employees. Remember that this is JD-R's own lead author speaking. Next: the spirals, where the two theories tell a different story about hope.

⚠ The trap✗ JD-R is simply COR applied to the workplace, a subset of COR.✓ The author explicitly rejects this. JD-R adds job demands as a key ingredient, specific mediators and moderators, feedback loops, and active employee behaviours (job crafting, self-undermining).

In JD-R theory job demands are one of the two main characteristics: they start the health impairment process and can boost the motivating effect of job resources.

STEP6
Loss and gain spirals in both theories · Learning goal 5

COR is fatalistic, JD-R leaves a way out

❓ This step answers: How do the two theories explain loss and gain spirals differently? (Learning goal 5)

Both theories include spirals, as we saw in step 4. But they disagree about what drives them, and that disagreement has a big practical consequence. This links straight to learning goal 5.

In COR, the driver is your resource pool itself. People with more resources are more likely to experience gain spirals, people with fewer resources more likely to fall into loss spirals. In JD-R, the driver is behaviour. In the loss cycle, demands lead to strain, strain leads to self-undermining, and self-undermining raises future demands. In the gain cycle, resources lead to engagement, engagement leads to job crafting, and crafting raises future resources. Think of two explanations for why a student falls behind: "she started with less", versus "she started skipping classes, which made it harder to catch up".

JD-R research adds that the loops are stronger for people already high in chronic burnout (loss) or already high in engagement (gain). But because behaviour drives the loops, the author argues that people can use strategies to "turn a loss into a gain spiral". COR, in contrast, "has a more fatalistic view on spirals, making it difficult for those with few resources to gain any resources".

So for an intervention, JD-R points to a lever: change the behaviours (stop self-undermining, start crafting). COR points to a warning: protect those who already have little. That warning comes back strongly in step 8, about who wins and who loses in future jobs. But first, step 7 asks what we still don't know about demands and resources themselves.

⚠ The trap✗ Both theories predict that a person low on resources is stuck in a loss spiral.✓ That is COR's more fatalistic view. In JD-R, the loops run through behaviours (self-undermining, job crafting), so changing behaviour can turn a loss cycle into a gain spiral.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy does Demerouti (2025) describe COR theory as more "fatalistic" about spirals than JD-R theory?

Model answer: In COR, spirals depend on the resources a person already has: those with few resources are more prone to losses and find it hard to gain. In JD-R, spirals run through behaviours (job crafting, self-undermining), so employees can use strategies to change the dynamics and turn a loss into a gain spiral.

STEP7
On the role of demands and resources · open issues

Which demand, measured how, and which resource fits it?

❓ This step answers: What do both theories still leave unclear about demands and resources, and what does the evidence say?
🧩 Be the job designer: in the game below, pick the resource that matches each demand.

Both theories say demands and resources should be in balance, but, the author admits, neither says what that balance looks like or how to restore it. This section asks the questions you need to answer before you can design a job.

First, which demands? Quantitative demands are about the amount and speed of work (long hours, deadlines). Qualitative demands are about the kind of effort: physical, cognitive or emotional. In real work they come together, like the ICU nurse dealing with a worried family, lifting a patient and reading monitors during overtime. Such combinations are rarely studied, but one study found that emotional demands went with more absence especially when work speed was also high (van Woerkom et al., 2016). Second, measured how? Demands can be measured objectively (number of customers, interruptions) or subjectively (ratings by the worker or by other people, such as supervisors or experts). Objective and subjective measures are related but not the same: in Li et al. (2023), expert ratings of 97 jobs and the views of over 2,000 employees correlated .53 for emotional demands, .63 for physical demands and .46 for autonomy. So measure both. The same goes for resources: perceived support (what you believe is available) versus received support (what you actually get), with mixed evidence on which matters more.

Third, which resource, and how much? Resources are scarce, so "the more the better" is not enough. The resource substitution hypothesis says one resource matters most for people who lack others: autonomy may compensate for less support from a manager. There may also be a tipping point: too much support when you don't need it, or so much autonomy that it becomes ambiguity, can turn into a burden. And the resource must match the demand (the triple-match principle): emotional support for emotional demands, cognitive aids for cognitive demands. A vignette study showed that availability is not enough: the resource must also be relevant and the employee willing to use it (van den Tooren & de Jonge, 2010).

So the practical message is: name the demand, then supply the matching resource at the right level, and check that people actually receive and use it. That is exactly what job designers will need as technology changes work, which is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ More social support always protects employees, whatever the demand.✓ Resources work best when they match the demand (emotional support for emotional demands) and at the right level: unwanted support or too much autonomy can become a burden.

STEP8
Demands, resources and the challenges of future jobs

Technology will change the demands, and the resources are a gamble

❓ This step answers: What do the two theories predict for jobs shaped by automation and AI?

We now know what each theory says and what is still open. The second half of the article uses both to look ahead. The World Economic Forum expects automation to replace routine tasks and to make digital skills, data analysis, critical thinking, creativity, emotional intelligence and lifelong learning more important. What happens to demands and resources?

For demands, the author (drawing on Demerouti, 2022) predicts three changes. Their nature shifts: technology takes over handling small objects and information, leaving people the cognitively heavy parts, while physical demands may drop. The change itself is demanding: new routines, training and asking for technical support raise workload and job insecurity. And there are by-products: new tasks that clash with old work design, technology that intrudes into private life (work–family conflict), more surveillance and more administration. For resources the picture is mixed: technology can increase or decrease autonomy, skill use, feedback and social contact depending on how it is introduced. Two resources are most likely to grow: opportunities for development and learning, and flexibility in time and place. But flexibility led by the employer (overtime, imposed hours) is a demand, while flexibility chosen by the employee is a resource.

The two theories give two scenarios. JD-R says: provide adequate, matching resources for the new demands, such as transparency and autonomy when people work with robots and AI, and ask whether social resources will survive. COR gives a "bleak picture": people with few resources, such as low-skilled employees, precarious jobholders and people in flexible employment, are most likely to lose. Hence the author's strong point that responsibility should not be pushed onto employees alone. Organizations, authorities and policymakers must design jobs that satisfy basic human needs. The evidence is mixed too: from 1975 to 2010, skill variety, task identity, task significance and autonomy all rose (Wegman et al., 2016), while robotization in car manufacturing (1975 to 1990) brought 40% more production, 20% fewer accidents and 45% more executives, but also a 45% smaller workforce (Bréchemier et al., 2016).

So technology is neither good nor bad for well-being in itself: it depends on how jobs are redesigned around it. For you as an HR professional, that is the core message. The last content step turns this into concrete advice for researchers and organizations.

⚠ The trap✗ Flexible working times are always a job resource.✓ Employee-led flexibility (choosing your own start and end time) is a resource; employer-led flexibility (time restrictions, overtime) acts as a demand.

STEP9
Future research (Table 2) and implications for job design (Table 3)

Prevention is better than cure

❓ This step answers: What should researchers study next, and what should organizations do?

We have the predictions. The article closes by turning them into two lists: what researchers should study (Table 2) and what organizations should do (Table 3). This is where the course goal "theory- and evidence-based practical implications" comes in.

For research, the main gaps are: how specific combinations of demands and resources work together (and the triple-match principle); whether resources can substitute for each other; how much and how fast demands and resources must change to affect well-being; whether AI acts as a resource or a demand depending on its design and use; spillover between work, family and personal life (remote work adds family demands but also flexibility); the role of organizational context, such as hierarchical versus flat structures; and, for COR, turning abstract ideas such as resource caravans into measurable variables.

For job design, Table 3 gives eight implications. Human-centered design starts from employees' needs, abilities and limits. Prospective job design anticipates future demands and resource needs before problems arise: "prevention is better than cure". Organizations can enhance resources (AI-personalized training and feedback, which build self-efficacy, a key personal resource in COR; more agile, less bureaucratic structures), mitigate demands (automate repetitive and physical tasks; make monitoring transparent and give employees a say; improve the timing and predictability of work), promote resource fit (social support for emotional roles, decision support for cognitive ones) and encourage job crafting, for example by letting workers choose the AI tools that fit them. Job crafting interventions have been found to stimulate crafting (Demerouti et al., 2019).

The conclusion brings it together: research shows that resources become most valuable under challenging conditions (Hobfoll & Freedy, 2017), and JD-R adds that demands and resources interact. Future job design should manage demands and provide matching resources. The next reading, Ten Years Later, shows how JD-R itself has grown to include levels, people and life outside work.

⚠ The trap✗ Prospective job design means redesigning a job after burnout has appeared.✓ Prospective job design anticipates future demands and resource needs and designs jobs before problems arise: prevention rather than cure.

STEP10
Conclusion and main take-aways

The article in one minute

❓ This step answers: What should you remember from the whole article?

We have walked through the whole article: why compare the theories, COR's principles, what the theories share and where they differ, how they see spirals, what is still unclear about demands and resources, and what both predict for future jobs.

COR is a broad, person-centred stress theory: people protect and build valued resources, losses weigh more than gains, and spirals depend on the resources you already have. JD-R is a specific, job-centred theory: demands and resources start two processes, they interact, and behaviours drive the loops. They share a lot, but JD-R is not a subset of COR. Drawing on Demerouti (2022), the author predicts that future jobs will bring new, more cognitive and change-related demands, and that resources must match them, with special protection for those who have few resources.

The buffering power of a job resource should be stronger for people who lack other (for example personal) resources; resources can compensate for each other, so the effect of one resource is smaller when others are present.

STEP2
What question does the review ask?

What have ten years of research added?

❓ This step answers: What does the review want to find out, and why now?

We know who wrote it. A review needs a clear question, otherwise it becomes a long list. So what do these authors want to know?

The overall question is: what do we now know about the causes and consequences of burnout and work engagement, and how has JD-R theory developed over the past decade? The popularity of their first review shows that many researchers are trying to understand burnout and engagement, and that JD-R offers useful tools for this. Ten years of research is a good moment to take stock.

The review also checks its own homework. In 2014 the authors asked for three things: studies of daily ups and downs in burnout and engagement, research on whether job crafting is the "missing link" from well-being back to demands and resources, and more research with observable outcomes. In the "Back to the Future" section they report what happened: they found 191 new diary and experience-sampling studies since 2014; a review concludes that burnout predicts fewer job crafting attempts over time, and some studies show that engaged employees craft more; but studies using observer ratings instead of self-reports are still scarce.

So the review is like a ten-year school report for a theory: what it learned, where it grew, where it still struggles. To follow the report, we first need sharp definitions of the two outcomes everything revolves around. That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ The review concludes that its 2014 research agenda was fully completed.✓ Daily studies boomed (191 new ones) and job crafting got support, but the authors found only scarce examples of observer studies and still call for less subjective measures.
📝 Exam-style questionIn 2014 the authors called job crafting the "missing link" in one causal path. Which path did they mean?

Model answer: The reversed causal path from burnout and work engagement to future job demands and job resources: engaged employees craft their jobs and so create more resources, while burned-out employees craft less.

STEP3
Key concepts · Learning goal 2

Burnout and work engagement: what they are, and what they are not

❓ This step answers: How are burnout and work engagement defined, and how do they relate to similar concepts?

The whole theory predicts two outcomes, so the review starts by defining them properly. This is more than vocabulary: how you define burnout decides how you measure it, and how you measure it decides what studies find.

Burnout was named by Freudenberger (1974), who saw volunteers lose their motivation. Maslach and Jackson (1981) defined it as three things: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization (a cold, detached attitude towards the people you serve) and lack of personal accomplishment. In the 1990s, depersonalization was replaced by cynicism, a distant attitude towards work in general, not necessarily towards other people, which makes the concept usable beyond care work. At least 13 definitions were published between 1974 and 2019, and the WHO recognizes burnout as an "occupational phenomenon" in ICD-11, not as a medical condition. Two problems remain: burnout questionnaires were not built to diagnose, and burnout overlaps strongly with depression (meta-analytic r = .80). Around 2015, about 10% of workers in European countries and 17% in non-European countries had burnout.

Work engagement was introduced by Kahn (1990) as bringing your whole self into your work role. Two views compete. Maslach and Leiter see engagement as the direct opposite of burnout (energy, involvement, efficacy), measured by the reverse of burnout scores. Schaufeli et al. (2002) define it as a separate positive state of vigor (energy and persistence), dedication (involvement, enthusiasm, significance) and absorption (being happily engrossed, time flies). In Europe, 71% of workers felt full of energy or dedicated in 2015. Engagement is not the same as workaholism, though absorption overlaps with working excessively and compulsively, and it is not job satisfaction: satisfaction is calm, contented (hedonic) well-being, engagement is energized (eudemonic) well-being.

The review uses Schaufeli's definition and notes that whether the two states can co-occur or are mutually exclusive is still debated; a meta-analysis found no significant cross-lagged links between them over time (Maricuțoiu et al., 2017). Now that we know what they are, the next step asks what causes them.

⚠ The trap✗ Since ICD-11, burnout is a medical diagnosis, like depression.✓ The WHO lists burnout as an "occupational phenomenon", explicitly not a medical condition. And because burnout and depression correlate about .80, telling them apart is hard.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat is the difference between depersonalization and cynicism, the component that replaced it in the 1990s?

Model answer: Depersonalization is a negative, detached response towards the recipients of one's service or care. Cynicism is a distant attitude towards work in general, not necessarily towards other people. (Because it does not depend on serving people, cynicism makes the concept usable in all kinds of jobs.)

STEP4
Antecedents · Learning goals 1 and 2

Demands drive burnout, resources drive engagement, personality colours both

❓ This step answers: What causes burnout and what causes work engagement, according to the meta-analyses?
📊 Predict like a meta-analyst: in the game below, guess which predictor came out strongest before you see the result.

We know what burnout and engagement are. The next question is the one employers care about most: what causes them? The review answers with meta-analyses, which combine many studies, so these findings are the most robust in the reading.

For burnout, job demands are the main situational cause. Role ambiguity, role conflict, workload and work pressure led to exhaustion and depersonalization among human service workers (Lee & Ashforth, 1996), and later in all kinds of jobs (Alarcon, 2011). Resources were less strongly linked, mostly to the cynicism part. Interestingly, the longitudinal meta-analysis of Aronsson et al. (2017) found the strongest evidence for two resources: low job control and low support went with more emotional exhaustion, and reduced personal accomplishment was linked only to low rewards. People matter too: neurotic employees focus on the negative and burn out more, while extraverted, agreeable and conscientious employees burn out less (Swider & Zimmerman, 2010), and emotional stability was the strongest predictor of exhaustion (Alarcon et al., 2009).

For engagement, resources dominate. Autonomy, social support, feedback and task significance predicted engagement much more strongly than demands did (Christian et al., 2011; Halbesleben, 2010). Lesener et al. (2020), with 55 longitudinal studies, sorted resources into group, leader and organizational levels: all three mattered, but organizational resources had the strongest effect over time, because they reflect how work is organized and designed. Mazzetti et al. (2021), with 94 studies, found developmental resources (career perspective) strongest and coworker support weakest, and personal resources such as resilience, self-efficacy and optimism even stronger than job and social resources.

So the two processes of JD-R show up neatly in the evidence: demands mostly feed burnout, resources mostly feed engagement, and personality shapes both. Next we ask: what happens after someone burns out or becomes engaged?

⚠ The trap✗ In the meta-analyses, coworker support was the strongest resource for work engagement.✓ In Mazzetti et al. (2021), developmental resources were strongest and coworker support was the weakest predictor of engagement; personal resources were stronger than job and social resources.
📝 Exam-style questionLesener et al. (2020) found that organizational-level resources had the strongest effect on work engagement over time. How did they explain this?

Model answer: Organizational resources reflect how work is organized, designed and managed, so they are more directly connected to the employee than group-level or leader-level resources.

STEP5
Consequences · Learning goal 2 and course goal "what drives performance"

Burnout harms health and work; engagement spreads energy

❓ This step answers: What are the consequences of burnout and of work engagement?

We know what causes the two states. But why should an organization care? Because, the review shows, burnout and engagement lead to very different outcomes, for people and for the business.

Burnout harms health, and not briefly. Social workers high on burnout reported sleep problems, headaches and infections three years later (Kim et al., 2011); burnout also predicts diabetes and the common cold. The most alarming finding: in more than 7,000 workers followed for ten years, burnout contributed to mortality among employees under 45 (Ahola et al., 2010). Burnout also harms work. Each dimension has its own closest outcome: exhaustion predicts absenteeism, depersonalization predicts turnover, and personal accomplishment predicts job performance (Swider & Zimmerman, 2010). The link with performance was even stronger when others, not the employee, rated performance. In 6,000 Swedish employees, exhaustion predicted long-term sick leave (90 days or more) over 44 months (Peterson et al., 2011). In healthcare, burnout goes with more medical errors and lower patient satisfaction.

Engagement has the opposite profile. Engaged people are more open and creative: weekly engagement went with weekly creativity, especially for people who love to learn (Bakker et al., 2020b). Engagement crosses into home life: partners were happier on days the employee was more engaged (Rodríguez-Muñoz et al., 2014), and in Japanese families, parents' engagement went with their own happiness, and happier parents' children had fewer problems (Shimazu et al., 2020). At work, engagement relates to task performance (Neuber et al., 2022), to helping colleagues (extra-role behaviour) and, most strongly, to commitment and job satisfaction (Mazzetti et al., 2021).

So this is why JD-R matters to managers: burnout costs health, attendance, staff and quality; engagement pays back in performance, creativity and well-being beyond work. Now we can look at the theory itself, in its updated, nine-proposition form.

⚠ The trap✗ Emotional exhaustion is the closest predictor of employee turnover.✓ In Swider and Zimmerman (2010), exhaustion was the most proximal predictor of absenteeism, depersonalization of turnover, and personal accomplishment of job performance.
📝 Exam-style questionMost studies on burnout and performance use self-reported performance. What did Swider and Zimmerman (2010) find when other ratings of performance were used?

Model answer: The relationship between burnout and job performance was actually stronger when other ratings (instead of self-reports) of performance were used, so the link is not just common method bias.

Same theory, now in nine propositions

❓ This step answers: How does this review state the theory, and how does it differ from the seven propositions in the FAQ?

The rules of JD-R appeared in reading 1 as seven propositions. Here, largely the same authors list nine. Before you panic: it is the same theory, just cut into finer pieces. Knowing why helps you on the exam.

The review first places JD-R in history. It is a "unifying job design theory" that synthesizes several older models: Herzberg's two-factor theory, job characteristics theory (five fixed job characteristics), the job demands–control model (demands are most stressful when control is low), the effort–reward imbalance model and COR theory. Older models claimed a fixed set of job characteristics; JD-R is flexible. Propositions 1 to 6 match the FAQ: two categories (demands and resources), two processes, a multiplicative effect (buffer and boost), personal resources reciprocal with job resources, personal resources as moderators, and job crafting.

The difference is at the end. The FAQ has one proposition on self-undermining. Here the loops get their own propositions: 7, engagement can start a gain cycle of proactive behaviour, resources and optimal demands; 8, demands and strain lead to self-undermining; 9, strain can start a loss cycle. The review also sharpens a distinction: a gain cycle may become a gain spiral, in which the loops get stronger and the levels get higher and higher, the same criteria the FAQ used. Evidence is strong for the two processes: in 203 samples (N = 186,440), demands such as risks and hazards related to burnout and then to errors, and safety climate related to engagement and then to fewer accidents (Nahrgang et al., 2011). Xanthopoulou et al. (2009a) supported proposition 4: self-efficacious, optimistic employees reported more autonomy, feedback and growth opportunities over time.

So don't memorize "seven" or "nine" blindly. Remember the logic: ingredients, two processes, interactions, personal resources, crafting, and two loops. If an exam question gives a number, check which source it refers to. Next: the four innovations of the decade.

⚠ The trap✗ The boost hypothesis says job resources amplify the effect of job demands on burnout.✓ The boost hypothesis says job demands amplify the impact of job resources on work engagement (and work motivation). The buffer hypothesis is about resources weakening demands' impact on strain.
📝 Exam-style questionAccording to the review, when does a gain cycle become a gain spiral?

Model answer: When the positive relationships (loops) between work engagement, proactive behaviour and resources become increasingly stronger and the levels of these variables become increasingly higher over time.

STEP7
Innovations 1 and 2 · Learning goal 4

Days within people, people within teams, teams within organizations

❓ This step answers: What is the person × situation approach, and what is the multilevel version of JD-R theory? (Learning goal 4)

JD-R was born as a model of one employee at one moment. The first two innovations of the decade place that employee in time and in context. Learning goal 4 asks exactly about this.

The person × situation approach starts from a simple fact: personality is stable, but workload and support change from day to day. So working days are "nested" in people. The daily process is the familiar one: daily demands lead to exhaustion and self-undermining, daily resources lead to engagement and job crafting. What is new is that stable personality moderates these daily effects (Bakker, 2015). Neurotic people are expected to suffer most on days with high workload and complex tasks; extraverts may flourish most on days with many social resources. General well-being works the same way: people with chronic burnout react more strongly to a bad day, while generally engaged people get more out of a good day.

The multilevel approach zooms out: employees are nested in teams, teams in organizations. Top management sets the HR strategy and climate; through HR practices, organizations select and develop leaders; leaders shape their team's demands and resources; and that "trickles down" to each employee's well-being and performance. Evidence: when employees perceived a psychosocial safety climate, a sign that management cares about their psychological health, they had a better mix of demands and resources and more engagement (Dollard & Bakker, 2010). Transformational leaders created fewer demands and more resources (Fernet et al., 2015), empowering leaders stimulated followers' job crafting (Wang et al., 2023), and leaders can be a key resource themselves. Teams also share perceptions of their demands and resources: team job resources raised objective research output (publications, presentations, patents) through team work engagement; relationship conflict weakened that link, task conflict strengthened the link from engagement to performance (Costa et al., 2015). And people craft more when their team members craft (Tims et al., 2013).

So, for an intervention, the multilevel model tells you where you can start: the organization's climate and HR practices, the leader, the team or the individual. The next reading, Team Resource Mobilization, builds exactly on the team level. First, the other two innovations.

⚠ The trap✗ In the person × situation approach, personality directly causes daily exhaustion.✓ Personality is a stable, between-person factor that moderates (changes the strength of) the daily effects of demands and resources on well-being and behaviour.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the multilevel version of JD-R theory and give one example of a trickle-down effect.

Model answer: Employees are nested in teams and teams in organizations. Top management determines the HR strategy and organizational climate; HR practices select and develop leaders; leaders influence their team's demands and resources, which affect employee well-being and performance. Example: psychosocial safety climate led to a better combination of job demands and resources and more work engagement (Dollard & Bakker, 2010), or transformational leadership led to fewer demands and more resources (Fernet et al., 2015).

STEP8
Innovations 3 and 4 · Learning goal 5

Three ways to act, and a life outside work

❓ This step answers: Which proactive behaviours does JD-R now include, and how do work and home affect each other?

The first two innovations put the employee in time and in context. The last two give the employee more ways to act, and a life outside work. Both connect to the loss and gain spirals of learning goal 5, and both lead to the two book chapters at the end of this theme.

JD-R now includes three proactive work behaviours, self-initiated actions to change the situation or yourself. Job crafting got two new insights. First, simply reducing demands (avoiding annoying customers, cutting workload) costs more energy than it gives; optimizing demands (simplifying processes, working more efficiently) is linked to more engagement (Demerouti & Peeters, 2018) and performance (Demerouti et al., 2018a). Second, people can learn job crafting in training (Oprea et al., 2019). Proactive vitality management changes the self instead of the situation: looking for energy and inspiration, such as a walk in nature or a museum visit; people were more creative in weeks when they did this (Op den Kamp et al., 2018a). Playful work design makes the work itself more fun or more competitive, without changing the job: an office worker imagining being Charlie Chaplin while doing emails, or a security guard guessing which route a customer will take. Designing fun satisfied the need for relatedness, designing competition the need for competence, and both the need for autonomy (Scharp et al., 2022a).

The work–home resources model (ten Brummelhuis & Bakker, 2012) links work and private life. Demands and resources at work influence home life through volatile personal resources such as time, mood and energy, and home influences work the same way. Key resources (personality, skills) and macro resources (culture, welfare) change how strong these effects are. Example: helping colleagues gave employees a sense of accomplishment but also tired them out, especially when the help was not returned; the tiredness led to withdrawal at home, the accomplishment to better family functioning (Aw et al., 2021). In another study, when employees had positive child-related events and shared them at home the evening before, job demands related positively to their task performance the next day (Du et al., 2020).

So employees are not passive receivers of their job: they can reshape it (crafting), refuel themselves (vitality) or make it playful (playful design), and their home life is part of the story. The last content step covers the mistakes researchers make, what organizations can do and what is still unknown.

⚠ The trap✗ Reducing job demands, for example avoiding difficult customers, is the most effective form of job crafting.✓ Proactively reducing demands costs more energy than it generates. Optimizing demands (simplifying, working more efficiently) is linked to engagement and performance.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat is the difference between job crafting and proactive vitality management?

Model answer: Job crafting aims at changing the situation (one's job demands and resources). Proactive vitality management aims at changing the self: improving one's own physical and psychological resources (energy, motivation, inspiration) to function optimally at work.

STEP9
Common problems (Table 2), practical implications and future research

How researchers misuse JD-R, and how organizations can use it well

❓ This step answers: What mistakes do researchers make with JD-R, what can organizations do, and what is still unknown?

We have seen how the theory grew. The review ends practically: what goes wrong when people use JD-R, what organizations can do with it, and what we still need to learn.

Common problems (Table 2). Researchers sometimes mix up the categories. A low score on a resource is not a demand ("low support does not mean conflict"), and a low demand is not a resource ("lack of conflict is not support"), the same rule you met in reading 1. Personal resources are positive beliefs about yourself, not behaviours, so "proactive coping" is not a personal resource, and it is not task autonomy either. And demands and resources can both have positive and negative outcomes: hindrance demands are detrimental, challenge demands can be both, and resources may even be "too much of a good thing". What matters is testing the two independent processes, health impairment and motivation, with good indicators of each.

Practical implications. The review gives five routes, four at organizational level and one individual: HR practices (open communication, work–life balance, learning); a psychosocial safety climate; monitoring and redesigning jobs, through participation or team-level job crafting; training leaders to offer the right challenges, remove hindrances such as bureaucracy, and enable resource exchange (even by redesigning the office with a coffee corner); and individual interventions, ideally tailored to each person's level of burnout or engagement. Future research should study interventions, especially organizational and team-level ones, since most interventions so far targeted individuals; team-level processes such as emotional contagion; and demands and resources from other life domains, as COVID-19 showed. Demerouti and Bakker (2023) propose that resources from one domain can buffer demands from another: workload may be buffered by autonomy at work, but also by autonomy at home.

So the review ends where your course goals point: use theory and evidence to give practical advice, at the right level. The review's own conclusion: high demands with low resources lead to burnout, high challenge demands with resources lead to engagement, and both organizations and employees can act on this.

⚠ The trap✗ Most JD-R interventions so far have targeted teams and whole organizations.✓ Most interventions focused on individual employees. The authors call for more structural interventions at the organizational or team level, and for combining both levels.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher measures "proactive coping" and calls it a personal resource in her JD-R study. What is wrong with this, according to Table 2?

Model answer: Personal resources are aspects of the self (positive beliefs or self-evaluations), whereas coping is a behaviour: an action, not a characteristic of the person or the job. Proactive coping is also not the same as task autonomy, a job resource.

STEP2
What question does the article ask?

Having resources is not the same as using them

❓ This step answers: What problem does the article address, and what are its research questions?

We know who wrote it. Now the puzzle, and it is one every manager recognizes: why does a team with more people, more expertise and more diversity not necessarily perform better than a team with less?

Research shows exactly that. Teams with more resources do not necessarily perform better, whether the resource is manpower, expertise or diversity. The authors conclude that many team problems are "less about resource access and more about resource mobilization". They define resource mobilization as using and sourcing resources in the team's internal and external environment. Think of a well-stocked kitchen: having ingredients does not make a meal; someone has to use them, coordinate the cooks, and fetch what is missing.

Here is the critique. Both JD-R (a micro-level theory about employees) and the resource-based view of the firm (a macro-level theory: firms win with resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable) treat resources as "inherently useful", as if their mere presence were enough. Both rarely put teams at the centre. So the authors look somewhere new: agile teams, where mobilizing resources is visible, varied and important. They treat them as "extreme cases", as in multiple case study research. Two research questions follow: how do agile work practices help teams mobilize resources (RQ1), and when does this work better or worse (RQ2)?

The outcome they care about is sustained team effectiveness, defined by two criteria: the team meets its goals across many performance episodes (the performance criterion), and not at the cost of members' physical and psychological health (the well-being criterion). Next we need to know what agile work practices are.

⚠ The trap✗ The article argues that teams mainly fail because they lack resources.✓ The core claim is that at team level the critical issue is often mobilization, not access: teams with more resources do not necessarily perform better.

STEP3
Key concepts and definitions

Agile work practices: a vehicle for resources, not a resource

❓ This step answers: What are agile work practices, and why are they not simply a job resource?

The question was how teams mobilize resources. The answer the authors study is agile work practices (AWPs), so we need to know exactly what they are before looking at the evidence.

AWPs are "planning routines that facilitate iterative approaches to taskwork and teamwork" (Junker et al., 2023). Agile taskwork means working in short cycles called sprints: instead of planning a whole marketing campaign in advance, a team builds a quick prototype, tests it cheaply, and adds and adapts parts each sprint. Agile teamwork happens in two meetings: brief daily stand-up meetings, where members update each other on progress and ask for help, and longer retrospective meetings at the end of a sprint, where they reflect and identify improvements. Teams may also develop products together with end-users ("co-created solutions"). Agile practices did not start in software: similar ideas came from total quality management and lean manufacturing; Scrum is a widely used framework.

Now the key conceptual move. Are AWPs a resource? Not in JD-R terms, because a meta-analysis shows they have both positive and negative effects on employees (Koch et al., 2023), so they are not universally beneficial. Not in RBV terms, because so many firms use them that they are no longer rare or inimitable. Instead, the authors call AWPs a "vehicle" that helps teams use, coordinate and leverage resources that are already embedded in the team or outside it. A stand-up helps mobilize knowledge already present in the team; working with end-users helps mobilize resources from outside.

So AWPs are like the logistics system of a warehouse: they are not the goods, but they get the goods to where they are needed. Next: how the authors searched and selected the studies, because a review is only as good as its method.

⚠ The trap✗ Agile work practices are a job resource, so JD-R predicts they always raise engagement.✓ The authors argue AWPs are not a resource in themselves (they have positive and negative effects), but a vehicle that helps teams mobilize existing resources.
📝 Exam-style questionGive one reason why, according to the authors, agile work practices cannot be seen as a firm resource in the sense of the resource-based view.

Model answer: AWPs have been widely adopted across industries, so they are no longer rare or inimitable, which the RBV requires for a resource to give competitive advantage. (Only when part of an integrated, hard-to-imitate HR system might they count as firm resources.)

Research method · the systematic review

Only studies that really measured the team

❓ This step answers: How did the authors select and analyse the studies, and why were they so strict?
🔎 Be the reviewer: in the game below, decide which studies would pass the authors' three criteria.

A review stands or falls with which studies it includes. These authors were unusually strict, and the reason teaches you an important idea about team research.

Most earlier agile research measured individual employees' perceptions of agile practices and linked them to team outcomes. That invites the atomistic fallacy: drawing conclusions about teams from data about individuals. One person's view of "our stand-ups" does not tell you what the team does. Valid claims about teams need intersubjectivity: agreement among several team members, relationships as the unit of analysis (social networks), or, in qualitative work, triangulation across data sources. The existing meta-analysis (Koch et al., 2023) called its own findings "preliminary" because study quality was low; for example, 24% of studies used single-item measures. When Junker et al. screened it, they found that only 2 of its 53 effect sizes were based on team-level data.

So the authors used three inclusion criteria: a study had to (1) examine agile team effectiveness (well-being or performance), (2) describe mechanisms or boundary conditions of AWPs, and (3) use data that establish intersubjectivity. They started from Koch et al.'s studies and their references (14 extra studies), then searched a database: 179 articles, 57 read in full, 17 excluded. The final set was 40 studies, 60% qualitative (20 multiple case studies, 4 ethnographies, on average six teams and 40 interviews per study) and 16 quantitative team-level studies. They then inductively coded each study for mechanisms ("AWPs work via…") and moderators ("strengthened or weakened by…"), shown in their Table 2.

So the evidence is of good quality for team-level claims, but mostly qualitative, and about half from software development. Keep that in mind: the model that follows is a set of propositions to be tested, not a proven law. Next: what the studies say about mobilizing resources inside the team.

⚠ The trap✗ A survey of individual employees' perceptions of their team's stand-ups is enough to draw conclusions about team effectiveness.✓ That risks the atomistic fallacy. Team-level conclusions need intersubjective data: agreement among multiple team members, network data, or triangulated qualitative data.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy did the authors not simply rely on the existing meta-analysis on agile project management?

Model answer: Most of those studies correlated individual employees' perceptions of agile practices with team outcomes, which cannot establish intersubjectivity and risks the atomistic fallacy; only 2 of 53 effect sizes were based on team-level data.

STEP5
Findings · RQ1 · mobilizing team-internal resources

Time, knowledge, manpower and psychological resources

❓ This step answers: How do agile teams mobilize the resources they already have?

We know how the studies were chosen. Now research question 1: through which mechanisms do agile practices help? The authors sorted the mechanisms into resources inside the team and resources outside it. Inside, they found four kinds.

Time. In an ethnography, agile team members dynamically prioritized their activities and, by building this into daily meetings, avoided time conflicts between competing deadlines (Kremser & Blagoev, 2021). In a 48-hour hackathon, agile practices helped teams produce more useful solutions (Ghosh & Wu, 2021), and objectively measured physiological stress near a deadline was lower in an agile team than in a non-agile one (Tuomivaara et al., 2017). But some members see daily stand-ups as a "waste of time". Knowledge. Agile learning routines help teams share internal knowledge (Annosi et al.) and cross knowledge boundaries (Majchrzak et al., 2012). The catch: agile teams can become too inward-focused, which limits innovation, their "Achilles heel".

Manpower (human resources). Agile teams involve all members in estimating the effort and time tasks need, where non-agile teams leave this to managers (Hoda & Murugesan, 2016); they allocate work to fit members' capabilities, with high autonomy and empowerment. Proactive use of one's strengths explained part of the link between agile practices and team performance (Junker et al., 2022). Psychological resources. Autonomy and empowerment partly explain why agile team members feel vigorous, dedicated and absorbed, in other words engaged. Several qualitative studies describe psychological safety (feeling safe to speak up and experiment), and agile practices correlated positively with it (Peeters et al., 2022). Agile working reduced emotional exhaustion, especially for people with strong planning and communication skills (Venkatesh et al., 2020). But it can also backfire through peer pressure, coercive control and competitive communication.

So agile practices are not magic: the same routine can mobilize resources or create pressure, depending on how a team enacts it. That is why research question 2, about when it works, matters. But first, the resources outside the team.

⚠ The trap✗ The review shows that agile work practices always protect members' well-being.✓ Effects are mixed: agile practices can build engagement and psychological safety and reduce exhaustion, but can also create peer pressure, coercive control and competitive communication.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat do the authors mean when they call internal knowledge mobilization the "Achilles heel" of agile teams?

Model answer: Agile social practices help teams share internal knowledge, but they can make teams too inward-focused, which limits their capacity for innovation because external knowledge is not mobilized.

STEP6
Findings · RQ1 · mobilizing team-external resources

The weak spot is at the team's borders

❓ This step answers: Why do teams also need resources from outside, and how do they get them?

Inside the team, agile practices can mobilize a lot. But the review shows that this is "not sufficient in all contexts". The authors find the real weak spot somewhere else.

Several studies place the "Achilles heel" of agile teams at their boundaries: poor collaboration with customers, mismatched views of what customers want, lack of support from top management, or competing project priorities. The answer is boundary spanning: activities through which teams source resources from outside parties, such as customers, internal clients and other teams. It works like a sports team that trains well together but also needs scouts, sponsors and good relations with the league.

Studies frame boundary spanning in two ways. Reactive: fixing a problem, such as inviting clients to product demonstrations when they are not involved enough (Hoda et al., 2011). Too much involvement is also a problem: extensive customer involvement caused interruptions (Wiesche, 2021). Proactive: agile practices may nurture an entrepreneurial mindset, so that members anticipate and seek opportunities with outside parties. Employee intrapreneurship led to larger performance gains in agile teams than in non-agile teams (Junker et al., 2022).

So, reactive or proactive, mobilizing outside resources can amplify the benefits of agile practices. This becomes the second half of the model: team-external resource mobilization predicts effectiveness on top of internal mobilization. Next, research question 2: when do agile practices help more, and when less?

⚠ The trap✗ The more a customer is involved in an agile team's work, the better.✓ Too little customer involvement hampers progress, but over-involvement causes interruptions. Teams need effective boundary management.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat is the difference between reactive and proactive boundary spanning? Give an example of each.

Model answer: Reactive boundary spanning responds to an obstacle, e.g. inviting clients to product demonstrations because customer involvement is inadequate. Proactive boundary spanning anticipates and seeks opportunities with external parties, e.g. intrapreneurial members spotting new opportunities with partners.

STEP7
Findings · RQ2 · boundary conditions · JD-R link

Challenges make agile pay off, hindrances block it

❓ This step answers: When are agile practices more, and when less, effective?

We know how agile teams mobilize resources. Research question 2 asks when this works. To sort the moderators they found, the authors use a distinction from stress research that JD-R has also adopted: the challenge–hindrance distinction.

Challenge demands are difficult but motivating, and they increase the need for structured ways of mobilizing resources. The studies suggest agile practices are most useful in complex, uncertain and volatile settings. Agile teams can even "thrive on uncertainty", which people usually experience as a hindrance (Dönmez & Grote, 2018): daily meetings clarify priorities under task uncertainty, and sprints let teams re-prioritize when requirements keep changing. Agile practices contributed to team engagement mainly when work complexity was high (Junker et al., 2021). One study adds an HR twist: agile practices only reduced the harm of changing requirements on project quality when members were rewarded for collective, not individual, performance (Maruping et al., 2009).

Hindrance demands create unnecessary obstacles and weaken the benefits of agile practices. They come from three places. Inside the team: destructive, competitive or excessive communication. From management: agile teams in project portfolios with high operational control had lower teamwork quality (Bechtel et al., 2022); conflicting requests weakened the benefits for engagement (Junker et al., 2021); clashing expectations with non-agile teams made full empowerment hard. From outside: clients who were sceptical about agile or withheld information, or who were involved too often.

In our words, this resembles JD-R's buffer-and-boost logic, moved up to the team: challenge demands make mobilization more valuable, hindrance demands get in its way. A limitation: most studies only sampled agile teams, without non-agile comparison teams, so we cannot yet say in which contexts agile works best. Next: the general model the authors build from all this.

⚠ The trap✗ Uncertainty is always a hindrance demand for teams.✓ Whether a stressor is a challenge or hindrance depends on appraisal. The review describes agile teams that thrive on uncertainty, because their routines let them take control of it.
📝 Exam-style questionMaruping et al. (2009) found a three-way interaction between agile practices, requirement changes and reward allocation. What does it show?

Model answer: Agile practices only helped to reduce the negative effects of volatile requirements on project quality when team members were rewarded for collective rather than individual performance.

STEP8
Model of sustained team effectiveness · propositions · Learning goal 4

Mobilize inside and outside, and watch the demands

❓ This step answers: What is the model of sustained team effectiveness, and what does it add to JD-R theory?
🎚️ Play with the model: move the two sliders and watch how mobilization and effectiveness respond.

From 40 studies on agile teams, the authors now generalize to all teams. This is the theory-building part, and it answers why the team level needs its own version of resource thinking.

The model has three parts. Proposition 1a: sustained team effectiveness is uniquely predicted by mobilizing team-internal resources (time, knowledge, manpower, psychological resources). Proposition 1b: it is also uniquely predicted by mobilizing team-external resources, over and above internal mobilization; external resources can even compensate for missing internal ones or strengthen them. Previous research called a team's ability to use resources a sign of a hidden "collective intelligence" factor (Mayo & Woolley, 2021). The authors offer an alternative: teams can develop this ability through visible activities, such as sprints (awareness of time), stand-ups (coordinating manpower and knowledge) and retrospectives (psychological resources).

Then the demands. Challenge demands (a) motivate teams to mobilize resources, for example through learning and high effort, and (b) increase the level of resources needed, because harder tasks need more time, knowledge, manpower and psychological resources. Hindrance demands (a) undermine mobilization, for example because conflict reduces knowledge exchange, causes power struggles and distracts attention, and (b) also increase the resources needed. Shared hindrances that hit the whole team are expected to be worse than conflicts between a few members. (In the article these propositions are numbered 3 and 4, although section 6 also calls them P2 and P3.) The bigger message for JD-R: resource-based theories started from a "static" view in which having resources is enough, and even job crafting is framed as something individuals do on their own, while colleagues cannot craft their jobs in isolation. Team-based approaches are not yet explicitly part of JD-R.

So the model moves JD-R from "do you have resources?" to "does your team mobilize them, inside and outside, given its demands?". That is the team piece of learning goal 4. The last content step turns it into advice for leaders and HR.

⚠ The trap✗ The model says challenge demands only help team effectiveness.✓ Challenge demands motivate resource mobilization, but they also increase the level of resources a team needs to stay effective. Hindrance demands undermine mobilization and also raise the resources needed.
📝 Exam-style questionHow do Junker et al. explain differences between teams in the ability to use their resources, and how does this differ from the collective intelligence view?

Model answer: Instead of a latent collective intelligence factor, they argue teams can develop the ability to mobilize resources through overt activities such as agile work practices (sprints, stand-ups, retrospectives).

STEP9
Implications for team leaders and HR managers · future research

Recognize, regulate, empower, guard the borders

❓ This step answers: What can team leaders and HR managers do to help teams mobilize their resources?

The model is general, but the authors also want it to be usable. So they end with advice, mainly for team leaders, coaches and HR managers, with one rule up front: the best solutions emerge "in co-creation with all team members".

First, recognize team demands, for example in retrospectives or with surveys over time. This requires psychological safety, because nobody admits to workload or role conflict in an unsafe team; leaders build it by acknowledging their own struggles and asking questions with open curiosity. Second, regulate demands: some can be reappraised, like presenting uncertainty as a driver of innovation; others must be fixed at their source, for example by clarifying vague client requests, or by setting up team charters that define roles and relations with other teams. Third, facilitate proactivity, for instance with strengths-based job crafting interventions, while avoiding the "proactivity paradox": valuing only initiatives that benefit the leader personally, which undermines innovation.

Fourth, manage the boundaries: shield the team from unnecessary interruptions and connect it with other teams and customers; a meta-analysis suggests boundary management by leaders is more effective than by team members. Fifth, tailor HR practices: select people with proactive personalities, the ability to prioritize conflicting requests and boundary-spanning experience, and reward collective rather than individual performance. Sixth, treat agile practices as work design: give teams autonomy in how they use them (how often to hold stand-ups), offer stimulating tasks, and create meaningful moments of contact. Leaders can also simply add resources: enough time, people and knowledge. The research agenda (Table 3) asks, for example, how long a sprint should be and who should do the boundary spanning.

So the reading closes the loop with your course goals: from a theory problem (JD-R is too static and individual) to evidence (40 studies) to a model and practical HR advice. Next, the two chapters show what individual employees can do themselves.

⚠ The trap✗ The authors recommend individual performance bonuses to make agile team members work harder.✓ The reviewed studies point to the superiority of collective (team) rewards for agile team effectiveness, and suggest also rewarding collaboration across team boundaries.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat is the "proactivity paradox" that leaders of agile teams should avoid?

Model answer: A situation in which leaders only evaluate favourably those initiatives that benefit them personally. It lowers the value of proactivity because it favours predictable outcomes and undermines team innovation.

STEP10
Conclusion and main take-aways

The article in one minute

❓ This step answers: What should you remember from the whole article?

We have walked through the whole article: the access-versus-mobilization puzzle, agile work practices, the strict review method, internal and external resources, challenge and hindrance demands, the model and the advice.

At team level, the problem is often not having resources but mobilizing them. Agile work practices are a vehicle for this: they help teams use time, knowledge, manpower and psychological resources, and boundary spanning brings in resources from outside. Challenge demands strengthen these benefits, hindrance demands weaken them. Generalized, this becomes a model in which internal and external resource mobilization each predict sustained team effectiveness: performing across episodes without harming members' well-being.

"At the team-level, many difficulties are less about resource access and more about resource mobilization in terms of using resources embedded in the team-internal or team-external environment."

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why do the authors say JD-R and the resource-based view underestimate resource mobilization?

Both treat resources as inherently useful, assuming that their presence or level is enough for good outcomes. Neither puts teams at the centre or explains how resources are used and coordinated by multiple actors.

Time (sprints, daily prioritizing), knowledge (stand-ups, learning routines), manpower (collaborative effort estimation and work allocation), psychological resources (autonomy, empowerment, psychological safety, retrospectives).

Challenge demands (a) motivate team resource mobilization and (b) increase the level of resources needed. Hindrance demands (a) undermine team resource mobilization and (b) increase the level of resources needed for sustained team effectiveness.
