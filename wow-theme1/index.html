Meeting 1 · Job demands and resources · prepared for Sezen

Why do some people burn out at work, while others thrive?

Two colleagues can have the same job, and one ends up exhausted while the other comes alive. This theme gives you the most widely used theory to explain why, and to know what leaders, teams and employees themselves can do about it.

The story of this theme · 8 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Every job has two kinds of ingredients. Some take energy: workload, time pressure, difficult clients. These are job demands. Others give energy and motivation: support from colleagues, feedback, autonomy, learning. These are job resources. When demands keep outweighing resources, people slowly drain until they burn out. When resources are rich, people become engaged, even when the work is hard. And it snowballs: drained people make choices that make their work worse, while engaged people shape their job so it gets better.

Each reading looks at one side of that idea. The two JD-R articles explain the theory itself and what is new. Demerouti (2025) compares it with its "parent", conservation of resources (COR) theory. Junker and colleagues move up a level, from the individual to the team. The two chapters show what employees can do themselves: job crafting and playful work design.

Why a psychologist learns this: work fills a large part of adult life. As a work psychologist, HR professional or coach you will be asked why a team is exhausted or disengaged, and what to change. JD-R theory is the go-to framework for that diagnosis, and it works for students and their studies too.

Tutorial group 1 · preparation

Your own job demands and resources

The tutorial starts with you: your study or side job. Are you facing a heavy workload? What resources can you draw on? Rate each one below and see where JD-R theory would place you.

✎Rate your own situation (1 = low, 5 = high)
⚠️ TrapA demand is not automatically bad. Challenging demands (a complex task, responsibility) can boost engagement when resources are present. Hindering demands (red tape, role conflict) mostly drain.
💬 Tutorial questionWhich of your resources would most effectively buffer your biggest demand, and why?

A good answer names a specific demand, picks a resource that matches it (for example, social support for emotional demands, or autonomy over timing for time pressure), and uses the buffer idea: resources reduce the impact of demands on strain because they give the motivation to deal with them.
