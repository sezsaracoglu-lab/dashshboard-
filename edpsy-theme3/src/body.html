Self-regulation and learning

Schunk, D. H., & Zimmerman, B. J. (2012). Self-regulation and learning. Chapter 3 in I. B. Weiner, W. M. Reynolds & G. E. Miller (Eds.), Handbook of psychology: Educational psychology (2nd ed., pp. 45–68). Wiley.

STEP1
What self-regulated learning is, and what this chapter does

The learner steers: goals, checking, adjusting, and the will to do it

❓ This step answers: How is self-regulated learning defined, which features do all theories share, and how is the chapter built?
Authors
Dale H. Schunk and Barry J. Zimmerman, two of the central researchers of self-regulated learning; many of the studies in this chapter are their own
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 3 of the Handbook of Psychology, volume Educational Psychology (2nd ed., 2012), pp. 45–68
Built for
Overview: five theories of self-regulation, the research on self-regulatory processes, two interventions, and future directions
Main message
Self-regulation can be explained from several theories, it can be studied, and it can be taught

Self-regulation, or self-regulated learning, is learning that results from a student's own thoughts and behaviours that are systematically aimed at reaching her learning goals (Zimmerman, 2000). It consists of goal-directed activities that students start, change and keep up themselves (Zimmerman, 2008). The chapter's examples: paying attention to instruction, processing information, rehearsing and relating new learning to what you already know, believing that you are capable of learning, and building productive social relationships and work environments (Zimmerman & Schunk, 2004). Notice that the list mixes thinking, motivation and the social world. Self-regulation is more than a study technique.

The theories differ in many ways, but they share three features (Zimmerman, 2001). First, students are self-regulated to the extent that they are active in their own learning in three ways: behaviourally, metacognitively (thinking about and steering their own thinking) and motivationally. Second, self-regulation is a cyclical process made of feedback loops (Lord, Diefendorff, Schmidt & Hall, 2010): people set goals, monitor their progress, and respond to that self-monitoring and to outside feedback, for example by working harder or by changing their strategy. Third, the theories pay attention to why people choose to regulate themselves. Effective self-regulation requires not only knowing what to do and how, but also regulating your motivation and your emotional involvement (Pintrich, 2000; Schunk & Zimmerman, 2008).

The plan. The chapter first explains five theories of self-regulation: operant, information processing, developmental, social constructivist and social cognitive. The authors chose these five because each has a solid theoretical and empirical base. Then it reviews research that identified self-regulatory processes and studied how they work during learning. Then it describes two interventions in detail that were designed to improve students' self-regulation. It ends with future research directions. Research supports the point that self-regulatory processes influence learners' achievement-related thoughts, behaviours and emotions (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2008; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011).

Where the topic comes from. Research on academic self-regulation was prompted by studies of people's behavioural self-control in areas such as personal management and finishing tasks (Karoly & Kanfer, 1982). Researchers then asked whether the same processes also improve academic learning, motivation and achievement (Zimmerman, 2001). Self-regulation fits a central idea of cognitive theories of learning: students are active seekers and processors of information who contribute to their own learning goals and exercise control over reaching them. Read the chapter as a guided tour, not as one argument. You will meet ideas from Theme 2 again (self-efficacy, attributions, goals, mindsets): here they become tools the learner uses on herself. Next: the map of the five theories.

⚠ The trap✗ A self-regulated learner learns alone and never needs help from others.✓ Self-regulation does not mean social independence. It means increasing internalization and self-direction; seeking help from teachers, peers or parents is itself one of the self-regulatory processes students use.
📝 Exam-style questionName the three features that theories of self-regulation share, and give an example of each.

Model answer: (1) Learners are behaviourally, metacognitively and motivationally active in their own learning, for example checking whether they understand a text. (2) Self-regulation is cyclical, made of feedback loops: learners set a goal, monitor progress and respond, for example by working harder or switching strategy. (3) The theories address why people choose to self-regulate: besides knowing what and how, learners must regulate their motivation and emotions, for example keeping going after a disappointing grade.

The map · five theories of self-regulation

Five lenses on the same learner

❓ This step answers: Which five theories explain self-regulation, and what does each one look at?
🗺️ Sort the situations: six learners, five lenses. For each situation, tap the lens that explains it best.

Some words first. A theory is an organized explanation that research can test. Operant behaviour, in Skinner's theory, is voluntary behaviour shaped by its consequences. A cognitive theory looks at what happens in the mind: attention, memory, thinking. Metacognition is knowledge about, and control over, your own thinking. A cultural tool is something a culture gives you to think with, such as language or symbols. In the picture: five lenses, each with the question it asks. Six learners appear one by one. You tap the lens that explains the situation best; only then does the lens show its name.

The chapter explains self-regulation from five theoretical perspectives: operant, information processing, developmental, social constructivist and social cognitive. Operant theory comes first, for two reasons: many of the earliest studies of self-regulation were done by researchers in this tradition, and its principles set the context for the other four theories, which all stress cognitive features. So the line runs from a theory that looks only at behaviour to theories that look at the mind, at development, at what learners construct, and at the interplay of person, behaviour and environment.

The chapter's Table 3.1 summarizes the key features (the table is below the picture). Operant: behaviours (self-monitoring, self-instruction, self-reinforcement). Information processing: cognitive processes (attention, coding, storage, retrieval, metacognition) and learning strategies. Developmental: improvements in cognitive processes with development, and private speech. Social constructivist: the construction of theories of self-identity, tasks and strategies; cultural tools such as languages and symbols; the zone of proximal development. Social cognitive: reciprocal interactions among personal, behavioural and social/environmental factors, and a cyclical process of forethought, performance control and self-reflection.

The five overlap. Private speech, for example, appears twice: in developmental theory (Luria's stages) and in the social constructivist theory of Vygotsky, who gave language a central role. Metacognition appears in information processing theory and returns in developmental theory, which studies how it grows with age. Goals appear everywhere. So do not learn the five as five separate boxes. Learn what each one puts in the foreground, because that is what an exam question about a situation will test.

Each lens also suggests a different way of helping a student. Operant: arrange cues, records and rewards. Information processing: teach strategies and comprehension monitoring. Developmental: fit the demands to what the child can control at that age. Social constructivist: support the student in the zone where she can succeed with help, and attend to the theories she builds about herself. Social cognitive: use models, goals and feedback, and teach the cycle of before, during and after. The next steps take the five theories one by one, starting with operant theory.

📝 Exam-style questionA teacher has a pupil talk himself through each step of a sum out loud. Which two theories in the chapter give private speech a central place, and how do they differ?

Model answer: Developmental theory and social constructivist theory. Developmental theory (for example Luria) describes stages in which speech gradually comes to start, direct and stop a child's actions, and studies why children fail to use helpful verbalizations (production, mediational and continued-use deficiencies). Vygotsky's social constructivist theory sees language, a cultural tool, as central, and private speech as what develops thought: children internalize word meanings from social interaction and use them to direct their own behaviour, and overt speech gradually goes inward.

STEP3
Theory 1 · operant theory

Cue it, record it, say it, reward it

❓ This step answers: How does operant theory explain self-regulation, what are its three key processes, and what is its limitation?
🎯 Build Brad's study plan (the chapter's own example), then pick the right way to self-monitor in four new cases.

Some words first. Operant behaviour is voluntary behaviour. A discriminative stimulus is a cue in the situation to which people may respond: it signals that a behaviour can now be followed by a consequence. A reinforcer is a consequence that makes a behaviour more likely in future; a punishment makes it less likely. A contingency is a rule that links a behaviour to a consequence. In the picture: on the left, Brad's study plan with three empty slots; on the right, a monitoring notebook. You fill in the plan, then choose how four other students should keep track of their own behaviour.

Operant theory comes from Skinner (1953). Voluntary behaviour occurs in the presence of discriminative stimuli, and whether it becomes more or less likely depends on its consequences. If a teacher praises a correct answer and the student keeps volunteering answers, the praise is a reinforcer; if a teacher criticizes a wrong answer and the student volunteers less, the criticism is a punishment. Self-regulated behaviour, in this view, means choosing among courses of action, typically by putting off (deferring) an immediate reinforcer in favour of a different, usually greater, one later (Mace, Belfiore & Shea, 1989). The chapter's example: Brad studies too little and is easily distracted. With his school counsellor he sets a fixed time and place for studying (6 to 9 p.m. in his room, with two 10-minute breaks), agrees not to use his phone or other devices then, and gives himself a point for each successful night; 10 points earn him a night off. So one decides which behaviours to regulate, sets up cues for them, evaluates performance against a standard and gives reinforcement (Mace, Belfiore & Hutchinson, 2001). The three key processes are self-monitoring, self-instruction and self-reinforcement.

Self-monitoring is deliberately paying attention to some aspect of your own behaviour, often with a record of how often or how strongly it happens (Mace et al., 2001). You cannot regulate what you are not aware of. Behaviour can be judged on quality, rate, quantity and originality, in areas as different as motor skills (how fast you run 100 metres), art (how original your drawings are) and social behaviour (how much you interact at social events). Students often have to be taught how to self-monitor (Belfiore & Hornyak, 1998; Lan, 1998). The methods: narrations (written accounts of behaviour and its context), frequency counts (how often a behaviour occurs in a period), duration measures (how long it occurs), time-sampling (a period is split into intervals and you record whether the behaviour occurred in each), behaviour ratings (always, sometimes, never) and behavioural traces and archival records (lasting records such as the number of worksheets completed) (Mace et al., 1989). Without a record, memory becomes selective, and self-recording often brings surprises, such as discovering that most study time goes to non-academic tasks. Two criteria matter (Bandura, 1986): regularity (observing continually, for example daily, not now and then) and proximity (recording close in time to the behaviour, not reconstructing it at the end of the day). Students can also self-monitor their attention, which research shows improves attention and achievement (Harris, Graham, MacArthur, Reid & Mason, 2011). Self-monitoring places the responsibility for assessment on the person who does it, and the recorded responses are themselves consequences that affect future behaviour. Self-recordings are immediate responses that bridge the gap between a behaviour and its distant consequences, such as praise and good grades (Mace & West, 1986). Self-monitoring can by itself improve behaviour (Belfiore & Hornyak, 1998); in a review of interventions for children with attention deficits and hyperactivity, self-monitoring, alone or with self-reinforcement, was often part of effective interventions (Reid, Trout & Schartz, 2005).

Self-instruction means setting up discriminative stimuli that set the occasion for self-regulatory responses that lead to reinforcement (Mace et al., 1989). One type arranges the environment: a student who needs to review notes tomorrow writes herself a reminder before bed. Another type consists of statements that guide behaviour; such statements have been used to teach academic, social and motor skills. Strategy instruction is an effective way to improve comprehension and achievement beliefs among remedial readers: Schunk and Rice (1987) had them say a six-step strategy aloud before reading each passage (the strategy's own wording): (1) What do I have to do? (2) Read the questions. (3) Read the passage to find out what it is mostly about. (4) Think about what the details have in common. (5) Think about what would make a good title. (6) Reread the story if I don't know the answer to a question. Saying the statements keeps students focused on the task, which may especially help learners with attention deficits. Kosiewicz, Hallahan, Lloyd and Graves (1982) improved the handwriting of a student with learning disabilities with five self-instruction steps (say the word aloud, say the first syllable, name each of its letters three times, repeat each letter while writing it, repeat for each next syllable). In the Self-Regulated Strategy Development programme (step 9), teachers modelled statements such as "What is my goal?" and "What is my next step?" (Reid & Lienemann, 2006).

Self-reinforcement is giving yourself a reinforcer after performing a response, which makes that response more likely in future (Mace et al., 1989). Reinforcement contingencies improve academic performance (Bandura, 1986), but it is unclear whether self-reinforcement is more effective than reinforcement from outside, such as from a teacher. Studies often have problems that make it hard to isolate its effect (Brigham, 1982): in classrooms, students usually work when the teacher tells them to, and they may stay on task because of the teacher's control rather than the reinforcement. Self-reinforcement is hypothesized to be an effective part of self-regulated behaviour (O'Leary & Dubey, 1979), but the reinforcement may matter more than who gives it. It may help keep a behaviour going over time, while explicitly provided reinforcement may matter more while the skills are still being learned. In the Reid and Lienemann (2006) project, students also said self-reinforcing statements, such as "I like that part!".

The critique. Behavioural methods promote useful behaviours, but by focusing only on behaviour, operant theory ignores the cognitive and motivational aspects of self-regulation. It defines motivation in behavioural terms, as an increased rate or duration of behaviour, and neglects beliefs and emotions. Behavioural methods work in the short term for increasing on-task behaviour, but self-regulation matters more over the long term, such as writing a dissertation or earning a college degree. For goals and actions beyond the immediate, a more elaborate perspective is needed.

So operant theory gives a teacher practical tools: help students set up cues, keep honest records, talk themselves through a task and reward themselves. The tools remain in every later theory; self-monitoring, for example, returns in social cognitive theory as self-observation. What operant theory cannot explain is what goes on in the student's head while she does all this. That is where the next theory starts.

⚠ The trap✗ Research shows that rewards students give themselves work better than rewards from the teacher.✓ It is unclear whether self-reinforcement is more effective than external reinforcement; the reinforcement may be more important than who gives it, and external reinforcement may matter more while skills are being learned.
📝 Exam-style questionA student records once a week, from memory, how much he studied. Which two criteria for self-monitoring does he break, and why do they matter?

Model answer: Regularity and proximity (Bandura, 1986). Regularity means observing behaviour continually, such as a daily record, rather than intermittently; proximity means recording close in time to the behaviour rather than long afterwards. Non-regular and delayed recording depends on memory, which is selective, so it often gives misleading results.

Theory 2 · information processing theory

The mind checks its own work

❓ This step answers: How does information processing theory describe self-regulation, and what are the TOTE model and Winne and Hadwin's model?
🔁 Run Jenny's loop, then Kai's assignment: say what the mind does next, then place each moment in a phase.

Some words first. Encoding means putting information into memory. Long-term memory is the large, lasting store; working memory (short-term memory) is the small store of what you are consciously handling right now. A standard is the level you compare your work with. A discrepancy is a gap between where you are and the standard. A schema or script is an organized pattern in memory for doing something. In the picture: at the top, a loop of four boxes; you tell Jenny's mind what to do next. Below, four stepping stones with plain questions; you place Kai's moments on them, and each stone then shows its official name.

Information processing theories see learning as encoding information in long-term memory. Learners activate the relevant parts of long-term memory and relate new knowledge to existing information in working memory; organized, meaningful information is easier to integrate and more likely to be remembered (Matlin, 2009). From this perspective, self-regulation is roughly the same as metacognitive awareness: individuals monitor, direct and regulate their actions toward goals (Gitomer & Glaser, 1987; S. Paris & Paris, 2001). It covers three kinds of knowledge: about the task (what you must learn, and when and how), self-knowledge (your capabilities, interests and attitudes) and knowledge of strategies for completing the task (Hadwin, Järvelä & Miller, 2011; Winne, 2011). It also includes procedural knowledge that regulates learning: monitoring your level of learning, deciding when to take a different approach, and judging whether you are ready for a test. These are control processes under the learner's direction.

The basic unit of self-regulation may be a problem-solving production system: the problem is to reach the goal, and monitoring checks whether you are making progress. The system compares the present situation with a standard and tries to reduce the gap (Anderson, 1996, 2000). An early version was the TOTE model (Test-Operate-Test-Exit; Miller, Galanter & Pribram, 1960). The first test compares the present situation with a standard; if they match, nothing more is needed. If they do not match, control switches to operate, which changes behaviour to resolve the gap. The new situation is compared with the standard in a second test; when they match, you exit; if not, more changes and comparisons follow. The chapter's example: Jenny reads her history text and stops now and then to summarize. She recalls what she read and compares it with her internal standard for an adequate summary (which may consist of rules built up through experience, such as "be accurate" and "include all topics"). If it matches, she reads on. If not, she locates the problem (her understanding of the second paragraph) and applies a correction strategy (she rereads that paragraph).

Winne and Hadwin (1998, 2008; Winne, 2001, 2011) built an information processing model of self-regulated learning, which Greene and Azevedo (2007) consider very relevant for education. It has three necessary phases (definition of the task, goals and plans, studying tactics) and one optional phase (adaptations). Phase one is about getting clear what the task actually is, using information about its conditions. There are two main sources: task conditions, information about the task that learners interpret from the external environment (such as the teacher's directions), and cognitive conditions, retrieved from long-term memory (such as how they did on earlier work, and motivational variables such as perceived competence and attributions). In the second phase, learners decide on a goal and a plan to reach it, including relevant learning strategies. Applying those strategies is the third phase. In the fourth phase they change their plan if their own evaluation says it is not working; when the first plan succeeds, this phase is skipped, which is why it is optional.

Within each phase, information processing works on existing information and creates products (new information). The processes form the acronym SMART: searching (scanning memory to retrieve information), monitoring (comparing retrieved information with desired information to form new knowledge), assembling (creating new links between new and stored information), rehearsing (repeating new information or using strategies to keep it from being lost) and translating (creating a new format, such as words, from another, such as mental images; Winne, 2001). Working on a task uses a schema or script with five slots, the acronym COPES: conditions (resources such as beliefs, motivation and prior knowledge), operations (methods and strategies), products (new information, such as new goals and adapted strategies), evaluations (do the products meet the standards, or is more work needed?) and standards (qualities the products should have, such as accuracy and comprehensiveness). Figuratively, these are the elements a student "copes with" to learn. Products are judged against the standards, and those evaluations (on target, too high) become new conditions for the student's next learning activities. The model matters for education because it was developed and used with real learning content and because it includes motivational variables alongside cognitive ones, an advance over information processing models that emphasize only cognitive components; much research supports the importance of motivational variables during self-regulated learning (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2008). Other models exist (for example Carver & Scheier, 1998, and models of self-regulation with technology), but they agree in their emphasis on learning strategies.

So information processing theory puts a small inspector inside the learner: compare, find the gap, fix it, compare again. That inspector needs tools, and the tools are learning strategies. The second tab shows which ones the chapter discusses, and how a learner checks whether she has understood.

Some words first. A learning strategy is a plan for learning something well. Rote learning means learning by repetition, without linking to meaning. A mnemonic is a memory aid; an acronym is a word made from first letters. Verbatim means word for word. Comprehension means understanding. In the picture: a toolbox with four drawers, each with a plain description. Students use a study method one by one; you tap the drawer it belongs in, and the drawer shows its official name.

Learning strategies are mental plans for doing a task successfully (Pressley et al., 1990; Weinstein & Mayer, 1986). They include selecting and organizing information, rehearsing material, relating new material to information in memory and making material more meaningful. They also include techniques to create and maintain a positive emotional climate, for example ways of handling test anxiety, building self-efficacy, seeing why learning is worthwhile and forming positive attitudes (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986). Strategies are an integral part of self-regulated learning because they give learners better control over their information processing (Winne, 2011).

Rehearsal includes repeating information, underlining and summarizing. Repeating information aloud, in a whisper or silently is effective for tasks that require rote memorization, such as Tim learning the 50 state capitals, the lines of a song or poem, or translations of foreign words. But rote repetition does not link information to what you know and does not organize it, so long-term memory does not store it in a meaningful way and retrieval after some time can be difficult. For complex learning, rehearsal must be more than repeating. Underlining (highlighting) improves learning if used judiciously (Snowman, 1986): when too much is underlined, it loses its effectiveness, because less important material is marked together with the important ideas; what you underline should be the points most relevant to your learning goals. Summarizing means putting the main ideas into your own words; it too loses effectiveness if it includes too much, and limiting the length of a summary forces students to identify the main ideas.

Elaboration expands information by adding something that makes learning more meaningful: imagery (a mental picture, often more meaningful than words), mnemonics (relating information to what you know, such as the acronym HOMES for the five Great Lakes, or the sentence "Every Good Boy Does Fine" for the notes E, G, B, D, F), questioning (stopping now and then to ask yourself questions, including higher-order ones such as how this relates to the previous section, or how it could be applied in a school), and note taking (meaningful paraphrases of the most important ideas, integrated with other information in personal ways). Notes should not be word for word: copying text counts as rehearsal; it may help recall, but it adds no meaning, so it is not elaboration. Organization includes outlining (setting up headings, or finding topic sentences and the points related to each; simply telling students to outline does not help if they do not understand the procedure) and mapping (identifying important ideas and how they relate, with main ideas at the top of a hierarchy and supporting points, examples and sub-concepts below). Research shows that mapping improves knowledge retention (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006).

Comprehension monitoring (or metacognition) is essential for effective self-regulated learning (Dinsmore, Alexander & Loughlin, 2008). It helps learners judge whether they are processing the material properly, whether they understand it, whether their strategy works or a better one is needed, and why strategy use will improve learning (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986). The monitoring processes are self-questioning, rereading, checking consistencies and paraphrasing (Baker & Brown, 1984). When a text does not provide questions, students must generate their own; teachers can teach them to stop now and then and ask who, what, when, where, why and how. In a hypermedia learning environment with middle and high school students, monitoring activities, especially self-questioning, significantly enhanced students' understanding of complex science topics (Greene & Azevedo, 2009). Rereading often goes together with self-questioning: not being able to answer is a cue to reread. Checking consistencies means asking whether parts of the material contradict each other and whether conclusions follow; a sense of inconsistency is also a cue to reread. Paraphrasing checks your level of understanding: if you can paraphrase, rereading is unnecessary (Paris & Oka, 1986).

So the toolbox has four drawers: rehearsal for holding on to information, elaboration for adding meaning, organization for building structure, and comprehension monitoring for checking whether it worked. The last drawer is the one that makes a strategy self-regulated: the learner decides for herself when to reach for another tool. Reading 2 of this theme is entirely about that drawer in reading. The next step asks how all of this grows as children develop.

⚠ The trap✗ Copying the important sentences of the textbook into your notes is elaboration.✓ Copying counts as rehearsal: it may help recall but adds no meaning. Elaborative notes put the most important ideas in your own words and connect them with other information.

Theories 3 and 4 · developmental and social constructivist theory

Growing into self-regulation, and building theories about yourself

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop in children, what role does private speech play, and why do children sometimes not use a strategy that would help them?
🌱 Grow a child: place four milestones on the timeline, predict a memory span, then diagnose three children who do not use their self-talk well.

Some words first. Maturation is growth that unfolds with age. Compliance means doing what someone asks. Internalization means taking something from outside (a rule, someone's guidance) and making it part of your own thinking. Private speech is talking to yourself to steer what you do. Automaticity means that a process runs by itself, without effort. A digit span is how many numbers you can repeat after hearing them once. In the picture: a timeline from birth to age four, with two phases filled in and four empty; you place the other four. Below, three children who are not helped by their own self-talk; you diagnose each.

Developmental theorists see self-regulation as progressive cognitive changes that let learners exert greater control over their thoughts, feelings and actions (Paris, Byrnes & Paris, 2001). Self-regulation includes beginning and ending actions, changing the frequency and intensity of what you say and do, delaying action on a goal, and acting in socially approved ways (Kopp, 1982). Researchers in this tradition have explored developmental changes in self-regulatory processes and the key role of private speech.

Kopp's (1982) framework links behaviours to developmental phases. From birth to about 3 months (neurophysiological modulation), control is limited to states of arousal and early, rudimentary behaviours such as reaching; maturation, parent routines such as feeding, and interactions matter most. From 3 to 9 months (sensorimotor modulation), ongoing behaviours change in response to events. Around 9 to 12 months, the first voluntary control shows up as compliance with caregivers' requests, which depends on the infant's receptivity and the quality of the mother-child relationship. Impulse control appears in the second year (12 to 18 months): awareness of social demands, and starting, keeping up and stopping acts and communications; intentionality and goal-directed action become visible. The second year is critical for the shift from external to internal control (Kochanska, Tjebkes & Forman, 1998): parental discipline expands, and the child's compliance becomes linked with internalizing rules. The self-control phase emerges in the third year (24 to 36 months), with more reactivity to adult commands and more communication through the growth of language. Children enter a period of self-regulation in the fourth year (36 months and older): they adopt rules that guide their behaviour, internalize others' guidance, show cognitive mediation of behaviour, and adapt behaviour to changes in the environment.

Information processing has gained priority in the study of development (Samuelson & Smith, 2000). Attention, encoding, retrieval and metacognition improve with development, as does processing speed (Kail & Ferrer, 2007). Sustained attention and attending to what is relevant are difficult for young children, as is switching attention quickly; better control of attention contributes to better working memory (Swanson, 2008). So teachers should forewarn students of the attentional demands of a task, use outlines and study guides as advance organizers, and use prompts, questions and feedback to keep students focused (Meece, 2002). In a digit-span task, an average 5-year-old repeats four digits accurately; by age 12 this rises to six or seven (Meece, 2002). Most basic cognitive processes are in place by early childhood; after that, development is mainly learning to use them better: finer discriminations, automaticity, selective attention and control over attention. When decoding becomes automatic, more processing can go to comprehension; poor readers spend so much effort decoding that comprehension suffers. Rehearsal appears early and improves with age; organization and elaboration also improve and can be taught; older children use better retrieval strategies, such as searching memory exhaustively instead of giving up (Flavell, 1999). Children change strategies slowly, but adopt new ones when these consistently give more accurate solutions (Siegler & Svetina, 2006). Metacognitive understanding expands greatly between ages 5 and 10 (Flavell, 1999), with steady improvements in metacognitive knowledge and in self-monitoring and regulation of strategy use (Alexander, Carr & Schwanenflugel, 1995). Children learn which strategies suit which tasks, and with development they are more likely to believe that using a strategy improves performance. Self-monitoring is aided by self-recording, such as diaries and checklists with the essential aspects of the task (Zimmerman, Bonner & Kovach, 1996); for reading comprehension, a checklist can contain steps such as reading the passage, finding the main characters and deciding on the main action. Older children also know more ways to find information in memory, for example by thinking of situations in which it could be useful.

Private speech is speech that steers your own behaviour rather than communicating with others (Fuson, 1979); developmental theory links it strongly to self-regulation (Berk, 1986). Part of the history comes from Pavlov (1927), who distinguished a first (perceptual) from a second (linguistic) signal system. He believed the differences in conditioning between humans and animals came from the human capacity for language and thought: people interpret stimuli in light of experience. He did not study the second system himself, but later research supported his view that human conditioning is complex and that language plays a mediating role. Luria focused on the child's move from the first to the second signal system. He (1961) described three stages in the verbal control of motor behaviour. First, the speech of others directs the child's behaviour (ages 1½ to 2½). Second, the child's own overt verbalizations can start motor behaviours but do not necessarily stop them (ages 3 to 4). Third, the child's private speech can start, direct and stop motor behaviours (ages 4½ to 5½). Luria believed private speech worked through neurophysiological mechanisms. The mediating role of language is also central in Vygotsky's theory (second tab).

Why do children not use private speech when it would help? A production deficiency is a failure to produce task-relevant verbalizations (rules, strategies, information to remember) when they could improve performance. A mediational deficiency occurs when the verbalizations are produced but do not affect later behaviour (Fuson, 1979). Young children produce verbalizations that do not steer performance; later they can, but may not produce them at the right times; with development they learn to verbalize when it helps. This model fits simple verbal self-regulation (such as rote rehearsal) better than complex verbalizations, where both deficiencies may coexist. Once trained to verbalize, children often stop when they are no longer required to (Schunk, 1982): a continued-use deficiency. It has three causes, each with a remedy. (1) An inadequate understanding of the strategy, after too little instruction and practice: give repeated instruction and practice with spaced review. (2) Linking the strategy to the training context: train with multiple tasks, and give explicit training on modifying the strategy when small changes are troublesome. (3) Not understanding that it helps (believing effort or time matter more; Fabricius & Hagen, 1984): give strategy value information, telling students the strategy will help them, that it benefited other students, and giving feedback that links strategy use to progress (Baker & Brown, 1984; Schunk & Rice, 1987). Such information enhances performance, continued use and transfer to other tasks (Lodico et al., 1983; Paris, Newman & McVey, 1982).

So development sets the stage: what a child can control, how well she can attend, how much she can hold in mind, and whether her own words can steer her. For teachers this means fitting demands to the child's level, and teaching not just a strategy but also why it works, on several tasks, with spaced practice. Social constructivist views of self-regulation are grounded in theories of cognitive development and rest on core assumptions (Paris & Byrnes, 1989; Paris et al., 2001). People are intrinsically motivated to learn: from birth they actively explore, understand and control their environment; their understanding goes beyond the literal information they receive, because they impose meaning on what they perceive based on prior experience. Mental representations change with development: infants represent the world in actions and images; later, learners use the tools of their culture, such as languages and symbols. There are progressive refinements in understanding: reconciling what you know with what you meet never ends, driven by internal reorganization and reflection, physical experience, social guidance and new information. Development places limits on learning: readiness includes maturation and prior experience, and learning goes best when information fits the learner's readiness. And reflection and reconstruction stimulate learning: teaching can produce learning, but the primary motivation comes from within, a need to re-examine your own knowledge and behaviour. Learners construct theories about what they are able to do and why.

So self-regulation, here, is the process of acquiring beliefs about and forming theories of your abilities and competencies, of the structure and difficulty of tasks, and of how to regulate effort and strategy use to reach goals (Paris & Byrnes, 1989; Paris et al., 2001). These theories are constrained by development and change with development and experience. Children's earliest attributions are undifferentiated, but with development a distinct conception of ability emerges (Nicholls, 1978); children then realize that performance may not match ability and that other factors, such as effort and help from others, matter. Children's theories about the causes of their results follow this developmental progression. They also construct theories about the use and value of strategies: they are taught methods for different tasks and build their own versions of what works best for them, including what the strategy is for, which tasks it fits, how it improves performance and how much effort it takes (Borkowski, Johnston & Reid, 1987). Most strategies are task specific, but some general ones, such as goal setting and evaluating progress, apply to many tasks. Learners often construct erroneous theories from incomplete information. In subtraction, children may come to believe that in each column you subtract the smaller number from the larger, whether it is on top or not. This buggy algorithm (Brown & Burton, 1978) produces answers and can create a false sense of self-efficacy: the child believes he can do far more than he actually can.

Dweck and colleagues showed that people develop mindsets, theories about their own capacity to learn (Dweck, 2006). With a fixed mindset, you believe what you can learn is limited and that no effort or help can overcome that limit; with a growth mindset, you believe you can keep improving. Learners with growth mindsets tend to engage more productively and persevere when they meet obstacles. Learners' theories about themselves come partly from direct instruction by teachers, peers and parents, but largely from their own reflections on their performances, effects in the environment and responses from others, built with cultural tools and in social contexts in the zone of proximal development. What learners are building, in the end, is an identity as a student, and that identity is shaped by other people, including stereotypes about gender, culture and ethnic background (Graham & Williams, 2009). Paris et al. (2001) argued that identity development and self-regulated learning cannot be separated, because achievement behaviours show who students believe they are or want to become; strategies cannot be taught independently of students' goals, roles and identities. When children are successful, they construct theories of competence, tasks and themselves that support learning; when they are unsuccessful, they may construct inappropriate goals and strategies. So self-regulation depends heavily on how children see themselves and achievement tasks (Dweck & Master, 2008).

Vygotsky emphasized the role of language in self-regulation (Tudge & Scrimsher, 2003). He believed private speech helps develop thought by organizing behaviour: children use it to understand situations and overcome difficulties, in connection with their social interactions. As their language develops, the words others say take on meaning beyond their sounds and grammar, and children internalize these meanings and use them to direct their behaviour (Vygotsky, 1962). He hypothesized a curvilinear pattern: overt verbalization (thinking aloud) increases until about ages 6 to 7, then declines and becomes mainly covert (internal) by ages 8 to 10, although overt verbalization can occur at any age when people meet problems. Research paints a mixed picture: between about ages 4 or 5 and 8 children talk to themselves less, yet a growing proportion of that self-talk serves self-regulation (Fuson, 1979). In many studies the amount is small and many children do not verbalize at all, so the pattern seems more complex than Vygotsky hypothesized. His second concept is the zone of proximal development: the amount of learning possible for a student under the proper instructional conditions. A task belongs in the zone when the student needs some help to do it but cannot yet do it alone. While teachers or peers support the learner (scaffolding), learners increasingly operate independently as they internalize their learning, and eventually the zone shifts to reflect new, higher-order learning.

So in this view self-regulation means internalizing skills and beliefs so that learners can act in new learning situations on their own, and it is bound up with who the learner thinks she is. For a teacher, that means two things: give help in the zone and fade it, and pay attention to the theories students build about themselves, about tasks and about strategies, because a wrong theory (a buggy algorithm, a fixed mindset) steers behaviour as surely as a right one. The next step turns to the theory that runs through this whole theme: social cognitive theory.

Social cognitive principles have been applied extensively to self-regulation (Bandura, 1997, 2001; Pintrich, 2004; Zimmerman, 2000; Zimmerman & Schunk, 2004). In this view self-regulation involves learner choices. Zimmerman's (1998) framework names six areas one can self-regulate: motives, methods, time, outcomes, the physical environment and the social environment. The more of these areas a learner can make choices in, the more room there is for self-regulation. If everything about a task is fixed in advance, students can still learn, but the control lies outside them, with teachers, parents or computers.

The framework is Bandura's (1986): human functioning involves reciprocal interactions among personal, behavioural and social/environmental factors (Figure 3.1). Self-efficacy shows the reciprocity. Students' self-efficacy influences their choice of tasks, persistence, effort and achievement (Schunk & Pajares, 2009); in turn, their behaviour modifies their self-efficacy, for example when progress on a term paper tells them they are capable. Self-efficacy and the environment also interact: many students with learning disabilities hold low self-efficacy (Licht & Kistner, 1986), and people may react to them on the basis of attributes associated with them rather than what they actually do; teachers may judge them less capable and expect less, even where they perform adequately, and teacher feedback can affect self-efficacy. Persuasive statements such as "I know that you can do this" can raise it. Behaviour and environment influence each other too: when a teacher asks students to look at a visual, they attend to it without much conscious deliberation; when students give incorrect answers, the teacher may reteach some points instead of moving on.

The classical social cognitive view describes three key processes: self-observation, self-judgment and self-reaction (Bandura, 1986; Kanfer & Gaelick, 1986). They interact, and the environment can help them develop. Self-observation is substantially similar to self-monitoring (step 3). Self-judgment is comparing present performance with your goal. Believing you are making progress enhances self-efficacy and sustains motivation. Students who find a task easy may conclude their goal was too low and set it higher next time; knowing that similar others succeeded can promote self-efficacy; and students who judge their progress as too slow keep their courage if they still feel capable and expect another strategy to work better. Self-reactions to goal progress have motivational effects (Bandura, 1986). Judging progress as acceptable and anticipating satisfaction keeps students motivated. Negative evaluations need not lower motivation if students believe they are capable of improving, for example by working harder; but motivation will not increase if they believe they lack the ability to succeed or improve. Evaluations are not tied to the level of performance itself: some students are content with a B, others want only an A. Assuming people believe they can improve, higher goals lead to greater effort and persistence than lower goals (Locke & Latham, 2002).

So the social cognitive learner is never a closed system: her beliefs steer her behaviour, her behaviour changes her setting, and the setting talks back to her beliefs. Self-regulation lives in the spaces where she has a choice, and it runs on judging her own progress against her goals. The second tab shows how these processes are ordered in time.

The interaction of personal, behavioural and environmental factors is cyclical, because these factors change during learning and must be monitored (Bandura, 1986, 1997; Zimmerman, 2000, 2011). Monitoring leads to changes in strategies, thoughts, feelings and behaviours. Zimmerman's (2000) three-phase model captures this cycle, and it expands the classical view, which covered only what happens during and after a task, by adding what happens before it. The forethought phase comes before performance and sets the stage for action. The performance (volitional) control phase involves processes during learning that affect attention and action. During the self-reflection phase, after performance and during pauses, people respond evaluatively to their efforts. Similar phases appear in other models: Lord et al. (2010) name four goal-related processes in a feedback loop (goal establishment, planning, action, and evaluation and feedback); Pintrich (2000) names forethought/planning/activation, monitoring, control, and reaction/reflection.

Various processes come into play in each phase (Zimmerman, 2011). In forethought, learners set goals, plan strategically and hold a sense of self-efficacy for learning and reaching their goals. Performance control involves implementing learning strategies that affect motivation and learning, and monitoring and recording your performance. In self-reflection, learners judge how far they have come toward the goal, explain their results, choose to keep or change their strategy, and set new goals where needed. Then the cycle starts again: the reflection on one round becomes the forethought of the next.

Research supports the three phases and the processes in them. DiBenedetto and Zimmerman (2010) studied high school students who were high, average or low achievers in science: compared with average and low achievers, the high achievers used more self-regulatory processes in each of the three phases, spent more time studying science and achieved more. Teaching all three phases also has desirable effects (Cleary, Zimmerman & Keating, 2006). College students who were novice basketball players all received instruction on shooting free throws, and then self-regulation instruction for one, two or three phases before practising. The one-phase group (forethought) learned to set goals; the two-phase group (forethought and performance control) set goals and recorded their performance; the three-phase group also made attributions and adjusted their strategies after missed throws. The three-phase group showed the most adaptive attributions and strategy corrections; the two- and three-phase groups shot most accurately.

So the cycle turns a single study session into a loop that improves itself: what you notice afterwards changes what you plan next time. For teachers, the message from the free-throw study is that teaching only goal setting is not enough; students also need to monitor during the task and reflect afterwards. Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) proposed that self-regulation develops first from social sources and then shifts to self-sources, in a series of levels. Beginners pick up strategies fastest from other people: being taught, watching models, getting a structured task and being encouraged (Zimmerman, 2000).

At the observation level, learners can work out the main features of a strategy by watching models, but most also need practice to fully master it; accuracy improves if models give guidance, feedback and social reinforcement during practice. In participant (mastery) modeling (Bandura, 1986), models repeat aspects of the strategy and guide the learner's attempts. Learners reach the emulation level when their performance approximates the general form of the model's: they are not copying, but imitating general patterns or styles, for example asking the same type of question as the model, not repeating the model's words. For these first two levels the source of learning is mainly social. At the self-control level, learners can use the strategy independently on transfer tasks; their use is internalized but still shaped by representational standards of the modeled performance (mental images and verbal meanings) and by self-reinforcement. At the self-regulation level, learners systematically adapt strategies to changing personal and situational conditions: they decide when to use a strategy, adjust it to the situation and are motivated by goals and self-efficacy, with little or no guidance from models.

Reciprocal interactions run through all the levels. Early on, teachers who see problems offer corrections, learners at the emulation level ask for help, and learners' performances affect their self-efficacy. More advanced learners practise mentally and overtly and seek out teachers, coaches and tutors to refine their skills. Social influences do not disappear: learners at the self-control and self-regulation levels use social sources less often but still rely on them (Zimmerman, 2000). Self-regulation does not mean social independence; it means increasing internalization and self-direction of your learning. It is possible to learn on your own, but self-teaching does not fully use the benefits of the social environment, and failing to use it may limit skill acquisition unless learners have good self-regulatory skills.

So the four levels give a teacher a plan: show the strategy (observation), let students try it with feedback (emulation), let them use it alone (self-control), and let them adapt it to new situations (self-regulation). Goals are integral to self-regulation, and goal setting is a key part of the forethought phase (Zimmerman, 2011). Researchers have studied how the properties of goals affect learning, motivation and self-regulation (Locke & Latham, 2002).

Near goals. Bandura and Schunk (1981) tested whether proximal goals improve achievement more than distant goals. Children received subtraction instruction and practised solving problems on their own over several sessions. One group aimed to finish one set of materials per session (a proximal goal); others a distant goal of completing all sets by the last session; a third group was advised to work productively (a general goal). Proximal goals produced the best self-regulated practice and the highest self-efficacy and achievement in subtraction; the distant goal did no better than the general goal. Hard goals. In a long-division programme, Schunk (1983a) gave children either difficult but attainable goals or easier goals for the number of problems per session; within each, some were told directly by an adult that they could reach it ("You can do this") and others heard that similar children had completed that many. Difficult goals enhanced motivation during practice and achievement; the direct attainment information promoted self-efficacy.

Process goals and feedback. Schunk and Swartz (1993) taught children to write different types of paragraphs: an adult modeled a writing strategy and children then practised it. One group's goal was to learn how to use the strategy (a process or learning goal); another group's goal was simply to write paragraphs (a product or performance goal); a third group was asked to do their best. Half of the process goal children periodically received progress feedback that linked strategy use to improved performance. The process goal plus feedback condition was the most effective, and the process goal alone brought some benefits. These children outperformed the product and general goal children on self-efficacy, writing achievement, self-evaluated progress and self-regulated strategy use. The gains held after 6 weeks, and children applied the strategies to types of paragraphs they had not been taught.

Shifting goals. Zimmerman and Kitsantas (1996, 1997) studied dart throwing with ninth- and tenth-grade girls. Process-goal girls were told to focus on the steps of throwing; product-goal girls on their scores; some girls also self-monitored by writing down after each throw either the steps they did correctly or the outcome. In the first study, process-goal girls reached higher self-efficacy and performance than product-goal girls, and self-recording also improved these outcomes. The second study replicated this and added a shifting-goal condition: girls first aimed at the steps, and once the steps ran automatically they switched to aiming for high scores. The shifting goal led to the highest self-efficacy and performance.

So the research on goals suggests four lessons for teachers (our summary): make goals near, make them challenging but attainable, start with learning the process and link it to progress with feedback, and switch to outcome goals once the process runs automatically. The last rule connects to the four levels of tab ③ in step 6: the shift from following a strategy to adapting it is the move to the self-regulation level. Some words first. Self-evaluation is judging your own capability or progress. Progress feedback is information that shows you are getting better. Time on task is the time actually spent working on the task. External monitoring means someone else records your progress. In the picture: two panels, one per line of research. While working toward a goal, learners need to believe they are getting closer. During self-reflection they can evaluate progress when tasks have clear criteria, but on many tasks progress is hard to see, especially when standards are unclear or progress is slow. Feedback indicating progress can substantiate self-efficacy and motivation, and as learners become more skilful they get better at evaluating their own progress.

Schunk (1996) studied goals and self-evaluation during fraction instruction. One group's goal was to learn how to solve the problems, the other's simply to solve them; in each group, half the students evaluated their problem-solving capabilities after each session. Three of the four groups did better on self-efficacy, skill and motivation: both learning-goal groups and the performance-goal group that evaluated itself. Only the performance-goal group without self-evaluation lagged behind. In a second study, all students evaluated their progress once; the learning goal then led to higher motivation and achievement than the performance goal. The conclusion: frequent self-evaluation raised outcomes whichever goal students had, while infrequent self-evaluation promoted self-regulated learning and self-efficacy only with learning goals. Self-evaluation may fit better with learning goals than with performance goals. Schunk and Ertmer (1999) found the same pattern with college students learning computer skills: when there were few chances for self-evaluation, students with a learning goal had higher self-efficacy, self-evaluated progress, and self-regulatory competence and strategy use; self-evaluation itself raised self-efficacy; and with frequent self-evaluation, both goals worked about equally well.

The effects of self-monitoring have been studied extensively (Mace et al., 1989; Zimmerman et al., 1996). In an early study (Sagotsky, Patterson & Lepper, 1978), fifth- and sixth-graders either monitored their work during mathematics periodically (recording whether they were working on the right materials), set daily performance goals, or did both. Self-monitoring significantly increased time on task and mathematics achievement; goal setting had minimal effects. The authors thought the children might first have needed to learn how to set goals that are hard but reachable. Schunk (1983b) compared children who recorded their own progress at the end of each session, children whose progress was recorded by an adult, and children without monitoring. Self-monitoring and external monitoring enhanced self-efficacy and achievement equally well, and better than no monitoring. The effect did not come from doing more work, because the groups completed the same amount: the key was the monitoring of progress, not who did it.

So making progress visible matters, in two ways: frequent self-evaluation lifts outcomes, and monitoring helps whether the student or an adult does the recording. Our application for teachers: you can start by recording progress for students and hand the recording over as they become able. The next step turns to learning from others: models and self-talk.

Who to watch, and what to say to yourself

❓ This step answers: How do models affect students' self-efficacy and self-regulation, and why can a model who struggles be better than a perfect one?

Some words first. A mastery model performs competently from start to finish. A coping model first struggles and makes errors, then improves and gains confidence. A peer is someone of your own age or level. Self-modeling means learning from watching yourself, for example on video. A path analysis tests a chain of influences (A affects B, B affects C). Modeling studies show how information conveyed by others can be internalized and used self-regulatively to produce more learning. Models do more than teach a skill: they show observers that following the same steps can lead to success, and students who believe they know how to perform a skill feel efficacious and motivated (Schunk & Pajares, 2009). Observing models is also an important way to acquire self-evaluative standards: children who observe modeled standards are more likely to adopt them, and similarity to the model increases adoption.

Zimmerman and Ringle (1981) had children watch an adult model try unsuccessfully to solve a wire puzzle for a long or a short time, while saying confident or pessimistic things. Children who watched a pessimistic model persist for a long time lowered their self-efficacy judgments. Brown and Inouye (1978) found similar results with college students solving anagrams: after being told they performed better than, or the same as, a model, they watched the model fail. Students told they were more competent than the model had higher self-efficacy and persistence than those told they were equally competent. Schunk (1981) gave children either adult modeling or written instruction on division, followed by guided and self-directed practice. Both raised self-efficacy, persistence and achievement, but modeling led to higher achievement and a more accurate match between self-efficacy and actual performance. A path analysis showed that modeling raised self-efficacy and achievement, that self-efficacy directly affected persistence and achievement, and that persistence raised achievement.

Perceived similarity in competence was studied by comparing coping models with mastery models. Coping models show how effort and positive thoughts can overcome obstacles, and observers learn these motivational beliefs and self-regulatory actions along with the skill. In the early stages of learning, many students may see themselves as more similar to coping models. Schunk and Hanson (1985) had children observe peer mastery models, peer coping models, an adult mastery model or no model solving subtraction problems. Peer mastery and peer coping models raised self-efficacy and achievement more than the adult and no model; the adult-model children outperformed the no-model children. Schunk, Hanson and Cox (1987) used fractions, a task at which the children had not been successful before, and found that peer coping models raised self-efficacy and achievement more than peer mastery models. Coping models may be more effective when students have little familiarity with a task or have had learning difficulties. Multiple peer models (coping or mastery) did as well as one coping model and better than one mastery model: when there are several models, a learner is likely to find at least one who resembles her.

Schunk and Hanson (1989) studied self-modeling: children were videotaped solving mathematics problems and then watched their tapes before practising on their own. They showed higher self-efficacy, motivation and self-regulated strategy use than children who were taped but did not watch, and than children who were not taped. Zimmerman and Kitsantas (2002) tested the levels of step 6: college students learned writing revision skills (combining short kernel sentences into non-repetitive sentences). At the observation level they watched a mastery model, a coping model (who made errors, corrected them and gradually improved) or no model; at the emulation level some received encouragement and feedback while revising. Coping model > mastery model > no model for writing skill and self-efficacy, and encouragement and feedback promoted skill and self-efficacy in every condition. The dart study (Zimmerman & Kitsantas, 1997; step 7) tested the move from self-control to self-regulation, and defined each level in its own way. Observation: participants received an explanation and a modeled demonstration of the task. Emulation: they practised throwing darts. Self-control: throwing consistently with the prescribed strategic steps (some had a process goal of performing the steps properly, others an outcome goal of a given score). Self-regulation: changing from following a specific strategy to adapting it on one's own, which the shifting-goal condition was designed to encourage, because learners had to adapt their strategy to reach the score. Internalization begins at observation and emulation, increases at self-control and becomes established at self-regulation.

So the best model for a beginner is often not the most brilliant one: it is a peer who struggles visibly, recovers and succeeds, or several peers, so that every student finds someone similar. And watching yourself improve on video can work too. The second tab looks at a different kind of help that the learner gives herself: talking her way through the task.

Saying your strategy out loud can steer your learning while you work, in the performance control phase. This is the research side of private speech (step 5) and of self-instruction (step 3).

Schunk (1982) gave children with low mathematics achievement modeled instruction in long division, followed by self-directed practice. The adult models said strategy descriptors (such as "multiply" and "check") at the right moments. Self-regulation can be taught: a writing programme and a reflection programme

❓ This step answers: What is Self-Regulated Strategy Development, how was it used to teach story writing, and what did it achieve?

Some words first. An intervention is a programme designed to change something, tested in research. ADHD is attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder. A criterion is the level a learner must reach. Maintenance means the gains last after the programme ends; generalization means they spread to other settings. In the picture: five stepping stones with plain names. You place five classroom moments on the right stone; each stone then shows the programme's own name.

Self-regulation does not simply grow with age, and children do not soak it up passively from their surroundings; it takes deliberate, systematic teaching. The chapter describes two programmes, chosen because they include several of the processes discussed, involve different participants (children and adults) and address two content areas (writing and mathematics). Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD; Harris, Graham & Mason, 2006) teaches students strategies for successfully completing academic tasks, with instruction and practice on self-regulation strategies such as goal setting, self-monitoring and managing effort. It uses teacher modeling, collaborative peer practice and independent practice in which scaffolding is gradually faded, and includes general and specific strategies plus motivational components such as self-reinforcement. It has been applied to writing (Harris et al., 2006), reading (Mason, 2004) and mathematics (Fuchs et al., 2003), with positive effects on learning, motivation and self-regulated strategy use; adaptations are usually needed for different students, content and skills.

Reid and Lienemann (2006) used SRSD with elementary school children identified as having ADHD, for story writing. Students learned to set goals, monitor their performance, instruct themselves, and manage their effort, strategy use and other behaviours; improvements were made concrete through self-monitoring and graphing. Responsibility gradually shifted to the students, and feedback and support were individualized, with each student moving at her own pace. Sessions of 30 minutes continued until a student could independently write a story with seven parts, captured in the mnemonic WWW, What = 2, How = 2: Who are the main characters? Where does the story take place? When does it take place? What do the main characters want to do? What happens next? How does the story end? How do the main characters feel? A planning mnemonic helped too: POW, "Pick my ideas", "Organize my notes", "Write and say more".

The instruction had five stages. (1) Develop background knowledge: the teacher explained the mnemonics, the students explained them back and said why they matter, and the teacher read stories aloud while students picked out the seven parts. (2) Discuss it: students kept picking out story parts, analysed their own stories for all seven parts and charted the number of parts and words. (3) Model it: the teacher demonstrated POW and the seven-part strategy by generating a story with student input on a planning sheet, saying self-statements for problem definition ("What is my goal?"), planning ("What is my next step?"), self-evaluation ("Does that make sense?"), self-reinforcement ("I like that part!") and coping ("I'm almost done!"); after discussing why such statements matter, students created their own. (4) Support it: teacher and students wrote a story together with the mnemonics and graphic organizers, checked that all seven elements were included, and talked about how the strategies helped them write better. (5) Independent performance: students wrote stories on their own from a prompt and graphed their story parts and words.

Students gained significantly in the number of story parts and the number of words in their stories, and maintenance tests 3 and 6 weeks after the last stage showed that the gains were maintained. This and other applications show that SRSD benefits strategy knowledge and use, performance and motivation, and supports maintenance and generalization beyond the original setting. Nobody has measured SRSD students against Zimmerman's four levels. Still, the programme descriptions show that observation, emulation and self-control are built in, and the results hint that many students may have reached the self-regulation level.

So SRSD is the chapter's theories turned into a lesson plan: self-monitoring and self-reinforcement from operant theory, strategies and monitoring from information processing, faded scaffolding as in Vygotsky's zone, and models, goals and the social-to-self levels from social cognitive theory. Using Zimmerman's three-phase model, Zimmerman, Moylan, Hudesman, White and Flugman (2011) developed a semester-long intervention to improve the self-regulatory processes and achievement of at-risk undergraduate students in mathematics. About 90% came from diverse minority groups and attended an urban public technological college; more than half were born outside the United States and spoke another language than English at home; the college's graduation rate for an associate degree was only 21% after 6 years. Despite these challenges, many students reported overly confident self-efficacy, which can hinder adaptive responses to academic feedback (Schunk & Pajares, 2009). The accuracy, or calibration, of self-efficacy beliefs is a particular problem among students who struggle academically (Pajares & Kranzler, 1995).

With a randomized controlled design, the researchers tried to improve students' self-reflection on academic feedback through three components: instructor modeling of error correction; guided self-reflection with quiz correction forms; and an incentive: extra quiz points for completing the form. Students were taught to see errors not as end points of learning but as opportunities for further learning. For each quiz item, the form prompted students to compare their self-efficacy and self-evaluative judgments with their actual outcome, explain the strategies that did not work, adopt more effective strategies, and indicate their confidence for solving a similar problem. The hypotheses: trained students would outperform control students taught in the traditional way, and would show less bias in their self-efficacy beliefs and self-evaluations.

The self-reflection group outperformed the control group on the instructor-developed mathematics examinations. They also showed less over-estimation bias in their task-specific self-efficacy before solving problems and in their self-evaluations after solving them. The training raised the pass rate on a national gateway examination in mathematics by 25 percentage points compared with the control group. Within the trained classes, instructors noticed that students used the forms very differently; as hypothesized, high self-reflectors scored higher on periodic and final exams and over-estimated themselves less than low self-reflectors. What is shown, and what is still open

Some words first. Longitudinal research follows the same people over a long time. Transfer means using a skill outside the setting where you learned it. Hypermedia are computer environments with linked text, images and video. Distance education is education at a distance, often online. In the picture: two baskets. Each claim lands in the one you choose: shown by research reviewed in this chapter, or still an open question.

Self-regulation and human development. Developmental researchers have shown how memory and metacognition change with age, and self-regulation researchers have shown that children can learn and use self-regulation strategies; the two literatures need a closer connection. A key question: is it better to teach children self-regulation strategies or to help them construct strategies themselves? One way to find out is to compare direct teaching (a teacher explains and demonstrates the strategies, students practise and get feedback) with constructivist teaching (student groups develop strategies for studying given material), with peers as teachers in the direct condition to control for the type of model. That still leaves the home: parents and caregivers vary widely in how much they use and teach self-regulatory skills, so longitudinal research is needed on how parents stress, encourage and reward self-regulation as children develop.

Self-regulation and the curriculum. Teaching students to set goals, organize their schedules or rehearse may not transfer beyond the context where it is taught. Teaching self-regulation inside a school subject shows students how to use it while they actually learn, and research shows improved motivation, learning, self-regulation and achievement in reading, writing, science and mathematics, and students can be taught to adapt the processes for use outside the instructional context (Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). The authors recommend expanding this research, also because it shows students the value of self-regulation: students who learn strategies but feel they are not useful are unlikely to use them, while students who believe they can use strategies well to learn and perform better tend to have high self-efficacy for learning, which strengthens their motivation. Their example: in a middle school team, the language arts teacher teaches the strategies for a term paper (goals and timelines, choosing a topic, organizing ideas, collecting information, outlining, writing, revising), and the mathematics, science and social studies teachers show how to adapt them in their classes, which also shows how to facilitate transfer.

Self-regulation across cultures. The earliest studies were done in Western cultures. It is tempting to assume that self-regulation works the same way regardless of culture, but the authors call that assumption unwarranted: every culture has its own values, traditions and beliefs (McInerney, 2011), so people in different cultures may not give the same meanings to the elements of self-regulated learning. Chinese and Japanese cultures, for example, emphasize education, effort and high standards, and children are expected to fulfil their parents' ambitions (McInerney, 2008); these students do well in the structured situations common in their cultures but may not show high levels of self-regulated behaviour in less structured ones, and the strategies in this chapter may not be ones they are skilled in. This does not mean that students in non-Western cultures do not self-regulate: effort is valued, and self-regulation covers emotional and motivational strategies as well, of which regulating effort and persistence is critical.

Self-regulation during learning with technology. Computer-based environments offer simulations, contact at a distance and expert tutoring systems, but they usually lack the external controls of traditional settings, such as teachers keeping students on task, so many self-regulation strategies seem critical there. Research is needed on which strategies help, how to teach them and how to raise motivation to use them, and on how technology can improve self-regulation itself: Azevedo and colleagues have shown how hypermedia environments can be structured to foster planning, knowledge activation, metacognitive monitoring and self-reflection. In their review of distance education, Bernard et al. (2009) suggest that learning may improve with more and better interaction among students; since regulating the social environment is a key part of self-regulated learning, students starting distance courses may benefit from tutorials on interacting with their peers.

The conclusion. Self-regulation is now a central topic in research on how people learn. Different theoretical perspectives have been advanced, and each has important implications for research and practice. The authors expect the knowledge base to expand greatly, and more intervention studies to show how best to improve people's self-regulatory skills. In short, they conclude, research on self-regulation will deepen our understanding of achievement processes and matter for teaching and learning.

Learning to drive. At first you watch your instructor (observation), then you drive with her beside you correcting you (emulation), then you drive the usual route alone (self-control), and finally you handle a new city in the rain on your own (self-regulation). Every trip you plan the route (forethought), watch the road and your speed (performance control) and think back on the tricky junction (self-reflection). The five theories are five instructors who each notice something different about how you learn to drive.

✅ Comprehension check

1. A student uses a strategy perfectly in class but never at home. Which theories in the chapter help explain this, and what would they advise?

Developmental theory calls it a continued-use deficiency, and advises repeated, spaced practice, training on multiple tasks with explicit training in modifying the strategy, and strategy value information. Social cognitive theory places the student at the self-control level at best, not yet at the self-regulation level where strategies are adapted to new situations; it advises moving from process goals to adapting the strategy, with models and progress feedback. The chapter's future directions add that linking strategies to academic content helps transfer.

2. Why is operant theory not enough to explain self-regulation, according to the chapter?

By focusing only on behaviour, it ignores the cognitive and motivational aspects of self-regulation; it defines motivation as a higher rate or duration of behaviour and neglects beliefs and emotions. Behavioural methods work in the short term for on-task behaviour, but long-term goals such as a degree need a more elaborate perspective.

3. Why can the chapter interpret the effects of the self-reflection training of Zimmerman et al. (2011) as causal?

Because it was a true experiment with random assignment of at-risk students to classes. The self-reflection training led to higher exam scores, less over-estimation bias and a 25-percentage-point higher pass rate on a national gateway mathematics exam, with effects that were statistically large or near large.
