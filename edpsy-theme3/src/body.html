Theme 3 · Self-regulation and metacognition · Prepared for Sezen

How does a learner take charge of her own learning, and how can a teacher help?

Two students are taught the same strategy. One makes it her own: she plans, checks how it goes and adjusts. The other drops it as soon as nobody asks for it. This theme explains the difference, and what teachers can do about it.

The story of this theme · 6 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Theme 1 was about how to study (learning strategies). Theme 2 was about why a student starts and keeps going (motivation). Theme 3 puts the two together. A self-regulated learner steers both: she chooses and checks her own strategies, and she manages her own motivation and feelings while she learns. Metacognition, thinking about your own thinking, is the part that watches and steers.

Three readings, one line. (1) Self-regulation and learning is a handbook chapter that explains self-regulation through five theories, reviews what self-regulating students do, and describes two programmes that teach it. It is the map of the whole theme. (2) Self-regulated learning in reading shows self-regulation in one subject: a reader must judge whether she understands a text before that judgment can steer her rereading. (3) Social cognitive theoretical perspective of self-regulation zooms in on the theory that runs through the whole theme.

The five questions of this theme (the course's learning goals, in its own order): How is self-regulated learning defined, and what are its key components? How do the five theoretical perspectives explain it, and what are their limitations? Which instructional practices follow from each theory? How can self-regulated learning be applied in reading? And how can educational professionals support its development in students?

Reading 1 of 3 · Text (handbook chapter) · The map of the theme

Self-regulation and learning

Schunk, D. H., & Zimmerman, B. J. (2012). Self-regulation and learning. Chapter 3 in I. B. Weiner, W. M. Reynolds & G. E. Miller (Eds.), Handbook of psychology: Educational psychology (2nd ed., pp. 45–68). Wiley.

STEP1
What self-regulated learning is, and what this chapter does

The learner steers: goals, checking, adjusting, and the will to do it

❓ This step answers: How is self-regulated learning defined, which features do all theories share, and how is the chapter built?
🧭 Watch Noor study: decide whether each move is self-regulated, and which shared feature it shows.
Authors
Dale H. Schunk and Barry J. Zimmerman, two of the central researchers of self-regulated learning; many of the studies in this chapter are their own
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 3 of the Handbook of Psychology, volume Educational Psychology (2nd ed., 2012), pp. 45–68
Built for
Overview: five theories of self-regulation, the research on self-regulatory processes, two interventions, and future directions
Main message
Self-regulation can be explained from several theories, it can be studied, and it can be taught

Think of learning to drive: at first the instructor decides everything; later you set the route, watch the road and correct yourself. Self-regulated learning is the learning version: learning that comes from your own thoughts and actions, systematically aimed at your own goals (Zimmerman, 2000). The chapter's examples mix thinking, motivation and people: attending to instruction, linking new material to what you know, believing you can learn, and building good working relations and places.

All theories agree on three things (Zimmerman, 2001). Active: the learner is involved in her behaviour, her metacognition (steering her own thinking) and her motivation. Cyclical: it runs in loops: set a goal, monitor progress, respond, for example by working harder or changing strategy. Why: the theories also ask why people choose to regulate, so managing motivation and emotions belongs to it. In plain words: you drive, you check the map, and you keep yourself going.

The chapter has four parts: five theories (operant, information processing, developmental, social constructivist, social cognitive), research on which processes students use, two programmes that teach self-regulation, and open questions. The topic grew out of research on behavioural self-control (Karoly & Kanfer, 1982). Ideas from Theme 2 (self-efficacy, attributions, goals, mindsets) return here as tools the learner uses on herself.

⚠ The trap✗ A self-regulated learner learns alone and never needs help from others.✓ Self-regulation does not mean social independence. It means increasing internalization and self-direction; seeking help from teachers, peers or parents is itself one of the self-regulatory processes students use.
📝 Exam-style questionName the three features that theories of self-regulation share, and give an example of each.

Model answer: (1) Learners are behaviourally, metacognitively and motivationally active in their own learning, for example checking whether they understand a text. (2) Self-regulation is cyclical, made of feedback loops: learners set a goal, monitor progress and respond, for example by working harder or switching strategy. (3) The theories address why people choose to self-regulate: besides knowing what and how, learners must regulate their motivation and emotions, for example keeping going after a disappointing grade.

STEP2
The map · five theories of self-regulation

Five lenses on the same learner

❓ This step answers: Which five theories explain self-regulation, and what does each one look at?
🗺️ Sort the situations: six learners, five lenses. For each situation, tap the lens that explains it best.

Some words first. A theory is an organized explanation that research can test. Operant behaviour, in Skinner's theory, is voluntary behaviour shaped by its consequences. A cognitive theory looks at what happens in the mind: attention, memory, thinking. Metacognition is knowledge about, and control over, your own thinking. A cultural tool is something a culture gives you to think with, such as language or symbols. In the picture: five lenses, each with the question it asks. Six learners appear one by one. You tap the lens that explains the situation best; only then does the lens show its name.

Five photographers can shoot the same student from five angles. Each photo is true, but each shows something else. The chapter explains self-regulation through five theories: operant, information processing, developmental, social constructivist and social cognitive. Operant theory comes first, because much early research came from it and because the other four, which all stress the mind, were built on its context.

Table 3.1 in one line each. Operant: behaviours you can arrange (self-monitoring, self-instruction, self-reinforcement). Information processing: what the mind does (attention, coding, storage, retrieval, metacognition) and learning strategies. Developmental: what grows with age, including private speech. Social constructivist: theories learners build about themselves, tasks and strategies; cultural tools such as languages and symbols; the zone of proximal development. Social cognitive: person, behaviour and environment influencing each other, in a cycle of forethought, performance control and self-reflection.

The five are not separate boxes. Private speech appears in developmental theory (Luria) and in Vygotsky's social constructivist theory. Metacognition appears in information processing and returns in developmental theory. Our tip: learn what each theory puts in the foreground, because an exam question about a situation tests exactly that.

Each lens suggests a different kind of help (our summary). Operant: arrange cues, records and rewards. Information processing: teach strategies and comprehension monitoring. Developmental: fit demands to what the child can control. Social constructivist: help in the zone and watch the theories she builds about herself. Social cognitive: models, goals, feedback and the cycle. The next steps take the theories one by one.

📝 Exam-style questionA teacher has a pupil talk himself through each step of a sum out loud. Which two theories in the chapter give private speech a central place, and how do they differ?

Model answer: Developmental theory and social constructivist theory. Developmental theory (for example Luria) describes stages in which speech gradually comes to start, direct and stop a child's actions, and studies why children fail to use helpful verbalizations (production, mediational and continued-use deficiencies). Vygotsky's social constructivist theory sees language, a cultural tool, as central, and private speech as what develops thought: children internalize word meanings from social interaction and use them to direct their own behaviour, and overt speech gradually goes inward.

STEP3
Theory 1 · operant theory

Cue it, record it, say it, reward it

❓ This step answers: How does operant theory explain self-regulation, what are its three key processes, and what is its limitation?
🎯 Build Brad's study plan (the chapter's own example), then pick the right way to self-monitor in four new cases.

Some words first. Operant behaviour is voluntary behaviour. A discriminative stimulus is a cue in the situation to which people may respond: it signals that a behaviour can now be followed by a consequence. A reinforcer is a consequence that makes a behaviour more likely in future; a punishment makes it less likely. A contingency is a rule that links a behaviour to a consequence. In the picture: on the left, Brad's study plan with three empty slots; on the right, a monitoring notebook. You fill in the plan, then choose how four other students should keep track of their own behaviour.

Think of training a puppy: cues before, a treat after. Operant theory (Skinner, 1953) applies that logic to yourself. Behaviour happens in the presence of cues (discriminative stimuli) and depends on its consequences: praise that makes a pupil answer more often is a reinforcer; criticism that makes her answer less is a punishment. Self-regulation means choosing among actions, usually by giving up an immediate reward for a later, usually greater one (Mace, Belfiore & Shea, 1989).

The chapter's Brad studies too little. With his counsellor he sets a fixed time and place (6 to 9 p.m. in his room, two short breaks), puts his phone away, and earns a point per successful night; 10 points buy a night off. The routine in four moves (Mace et al., 2001): choose the behaviour, set up cues, compare performance with a standard, reinforce. Three key processes follow: self-monitoring, self-instruction and self-reinforcement.

Self-monitoring is deliberately watching one aspect of your own behaviour, usually with a record. Six methods: narrations, frequency counts, duration measures, time-sampling, behaviour ratings, and lasting records such as worksheets completed. Two criteria (Bandura, 1986): regularity (record continually) and proximity (record right away). In plain words: a step counter works because it counts every day, straight away. Self-monitoring alone can already improve behaviour (Belfiore & Hornyak, 1998).

Self-instruction means setting up cues that trigger the right action: a note to yourself before bed, or statements you say while working. Schunk and Rice (1987): weak readers said a six-step strategy aloud before reading each passage ("What do I have to do?" … "Reread if I don't know the answer"). Strategy instruction like this improved comprehension and achievement beliefs. In plain words: talking yourself through the steps keeps you on task.

Self-reinforcement is rewarding yourself after a response, so the response becomes more likely. But it is unclear whether rewarding yourself works better than being rewarded by a teacher: in class, students may stay on task because of the teacher's control, so the effect is hard to isolate (Brigham, 1982). The reward may matter more than who gives it. Explicit reinforcement may matter more while skills are still being learned.

Behavioural methods help in the short term, for example more time on task. But by looking only at behaviour, operant theory ignores the cognitive and motivational side: beliefs and emotions. Long-term goals, such as finishing a degree, need a richer view. In plain words: the puppy logic explains what you do, not what you think while you do it.

Operant theory gives teachers practical tools: cues, honest records, self-talk and self-rewards. Self-monitoring returns later as self-observation in social cognitive theory. What the theory cannot explain is what goes on in the student's head, and that is where the next theory starts.

⚠ The trap✗ Research shows that rewards students give themselves work better than rewards from the teacher.✓ It is unclear whether self-reinforcement is more effective than external reinforcement; the reinforcement may be more important than who gives it, and external reinforcement may matter more while skills are being learned.
📝 Exam-style questionA student records once a week, from memory, how much he studied. Which two criteria for self-monitoring does he break, and why do they matter?

Model answer: Regularity and proximity (Bandura, 1986). Regularity means observing behaviour continually, such as a daily record, rather than intermittently; proximity means recording close in time to the behaviour rather than long afterwards. Non-regular and delayed recording depends on memory, which is selective, so it often gives misleading results.

STEP4
Theory 2 · information processing theory

The mind checks its own work

❓ This step answers: How does information processing theory describe self-regulation, and what are the TOTE model and Winne and Hadwin's model?
🔁 Run Jenny's loop, then Kai's assignment: say what the mind does next, then place each moment in a phase.

Some words first. Encoding means putting information into memory. Long-term memory is the large, lasting store; working memory (short-term memory) is the small store of what you are consciously handling right now. A standard is the level you compare your work with. A discrepancy is a gap between where you are and the standard. A schema or script is an organized pattern in memory for doing something. In the picture: at the top, a loop of four boxes; you tell Jenny's mind what to do next. Below, four stepping stones with plain questions; you place Kai's moments on them, and each stone then shows its official name.

Think of a thermostat: it compares the room with the setting and heats until they match. Information processing theory sees self-regulation as roughly metacognition: monitoring, directing and regulating your own thinking toward a goal. It uses three kinds of knowledge: about the task, about yourself and about strategies, plus the know-how to check your learning and decide when to change approach or whether you are ready for a test.

The TOTE model (Miller, Galanter & Pribram, 1960): Test (compare now with the standard), Operate (change something if they differ), Test again, Exit when they match. The chapter's Jenny reads history and stops to summarize. Her summary falls short of her standard ("accurate, all topics"), so she finds the problem, the second paragraph, and rereads it. Then she reads on.

Winne and Hadwin (1998, 2008) describe four phases: (1) define the task, (2) set goals and plans, (3) use study tactics, and optional (4) adapt, which is skipped when the plan works. To define the task, learners use task conditions (from outside, such as the teacher's instructions) and cognitive conditions (from memory, such as earlier results and how competent they feel). In plain words: work out what is asked, plan, do it, and change course only if it is not working.

Inside each phase, the mind does five things, SMART: searching memory for what you know, monitoring (comparing what you found with what you want), assembling (linking new to old), rehearsing (repeating so it is not lost) and translating (changing the format, for example from images to words).

Every task has five slots, COPES, which are what a student "copes" with: conditions (beliefs, motivation, prior knowledge), operations (strategies), products (what comes out), evaluations (does it meet the standard?) and standards (the qualities it should have). The verdict becomes a new condition for the next round. Why it matters: the model was built with real study tasks and includes motivation.

Information processing puts a small inspector inside the learner: compare, find the gap, fix it, compare again. The inspector needs tools, and those tools are learning strategies (second tab).

⚠ The trap✗ Winne and Hadwin's model has four phases that every learner always goes through.✓ Three phases are necessary (definition of the task, goals and plans, studying tactics); the fourth, adaptations, is optional: it is not needed if the original plan is successful.
📝 Exam-style questionIn Winne and Hadwin's model, a student defines a task using two sources of information. Name and explain both, with an example.

Model answer: Task conditions: information about the task that learners interpret from the external environment, for example the teacher's directions for an assignment. Cognitive conditions: information learners retrieve from long-term memory, for example how they did on prior work and motivational variables such as perceived competence and attributions.

❓ This step answers: Which learning strategies does the chapter describe, when do they work, and how does a learner monitor her comprehension?
🧰 Fill the toolbox: nine students use a study method. Tap the drawer it belongs in, then judge two claims.

Some words first. A learning strategy is a plan for learning something well. Rote learning means learning by repetition, without linking to meaning. A mnemonic is a memory aid; an acronym is a word made from first letters. Verbatim means word for word. Comprehension means understanding. In the picture: a toolbox with four drawers, each with a plain description. Students use a study method one by one; you tap the drawer it belongs in, and the drawer shows its official name.

Learning strategies are mental plans for doing a task well (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986): selecting, organizing, rehearsing and linking information, but also handling test anxiety or building confidence. They are part of self-regulation because they give learners control over their own processing. Think of them as a toolbox with four drawers.

Rehearsal means repeating, underlining or summarizing. Repetition works for rote tasks, such as capitals or song lyrics, but it links nothing to what you know, so it is hard to retrieve later. Underlining and summarizing help only when you are selective: mark or keep only the main ideas. In plain words: highlighting the whole page is the same as highlighting nothing.

Elaboration adds meaning: mental images, mnemonics (HOMES for the five Great Lakes), asking yourself questions, and notes in your own words. Copying word for word is rehearsal, not elaboration. Organization builds structure: outlining, and mapping the main ideas and how they connect. Mapping improves retention (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006).

Comprehension monitoring checks whether it worked. Four moves: self-questioning (who, what, why, how), rereading when you cannot answer, checking consistency (do the parts fit?) and paraphrasing (if you can say it in your own words, no need to reread). Greene and Azevedo (2009): in a hypermedia science environment, monitoring, especially self-questioning, clearly improved students' understanding.

Rehearsal holds information, elaboration adds meaning, organization builds structure, and monitoring checks the result. Monitoring is what makes the toolbox self-regulated: the learner decides when to reach for another tool. Reading 2 is entirely about that drawer.

⚠ The trap✗ Copying the important sentences of the textbook into your notes is elaboration.✓ Copying counts as rehearsal: it may help recall but adds no meaning. Elaborative notes put the most important ideas in your own words and connect them with other information.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy is rote rehearsal a weak strategy for complex learning, according to the chapter, and what should rehearsal involve instead?

Model answer: Rote repetition does not link information with what the learner already knows and does not organize it, so long-term memory does not store it in a meaningful way and retrieval after some time can be difficult. For complex learning, rehearsal must involve more than repeating, for example judicious underlining of the points most relevant to the learning goals, or short summaries in your own words that force you to identify the main ideas.

STEP5
Theories 3 and 4 · developmental and social constructivist theory

Growing into self-regulation, and building theories about yourself

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop in children, what role does private speech play, and why do children sometimes not use a strategy that would help them?

How does a baby who controls almost nothing become a child who can steer herself, and why do children sometimes not use a strategy that would help them?Why this matters. Operant and information processing theory describe how self-regulation works. Developmental theory asks a different question: how does it grow? The answer tells a teacher what she can expect from a learner of a given age, and why training a strategy once is not enough.In this step you learn four things:1When control appears, from birth to about age three2What keeps growing at school age: attention, memory, thinking about your thinking3The tool that carries control inside: talking to yourself4Why children don't use helpful self-talk, and how a teacher fixes it

⭐ Self-regulation is not a switch you turn on. It grows: as children develop, their thinking changes in ways that give them more and more control over their thoughts, feelings and actions.That is how developmental theorists define it (Paris, Byrnes & Paris, 2001). What does "control" mean in practice? Kopp (1982) spells it out: starting and stopping an action, making speech or movement more or less frequent or intense, waiting before acting on a goal, and behaving in ways others approve of. Our example: a toddler sees a biscuit and grabs it; a four-year-old sees the same biscuit and waits until after dinner. Same wish, more control.So this means: what you can ask of a learner depends on how far her control has grown. Researchers in this tradition study two things: how control changes with age, and the key role of private speech, talking to yourself.→ Next: when does that control first appear?

⭐ The first voluntary control appears at 9 to 12 months, when the baby does what a caregiver asks. From there control grows in phases and moves from outside the child (a parent says "stop") to inside (she stops herself), reaching self-regulation from about 36 months.Kopp (1982) describes the phases. Read them as a ladder where each phase adds a new source of control: first the body, then reactions to the world, then a relationship, then rules, then language, then thought.0–3mNeurophysiological modulation. The baby controls only her arousal and simple acts such as reaching; maturation and parents' routines, such as feeding, shape her behaviour.3–9mSensorimotor modulation. Her behaviour changes in response to what happens around her.9–12mCompliance. The first voluntary control: doing what a caregiver asks. It depends on how receptive she is and on the quality of the mother-child relationship.12–18mImpulse control (second year). She notices what a situation demands and can start, continue and stop actions; she starts to act on purpose, towards goals. The second year is the critical shift from outside to inside control (Kochanska et al., 1998): parents discipline more, and her compliance becomes linked to internalizing rules, taking them in as her own.24–36mSelf-control (third year). She reacts more to adults' commands, and growing language lets words direct her (the "directive function of speech"); taking in adults' guidance becomes common.36m+Self-regulation (fourth year). She adopts rules that guide her, takes in even more of others' guidance, thinking starts to steer her behaviour, and she adapts when demands change.So this means: self-regulation is the end of a long path from outside control to inside control. Keep this thread, "outside → inside"; it returns with Vygotsky in the next tab and in reading 3.→ Next: by about three, the basics of control are there. What keeps growing after that?

⭐ By early childhood the basic thinking processes are in place. What grows next is how well and how quickly children use them.Developmental research now looks mostly through an information processing lens: how children take in, store and retrieve information (Samuelson & Smith, 2000). Attention, storing, retrieving and metacognition improve with age, and so does their speed (Kail & Ferrer, 2007). Young children find it hard to keep their attention on one thing, to attend to what matters and ignore the rest, and to switch quickly between activities. As they learn to control attention, their working memory (what you can hold in mind right now) improves. A simple test is the digit span: a researcher reads digits at one per second and the child repeats them. An average 5-year-old repeats four; by 12 it is six or seven (Meece, 2002). Behind this, capacity and strategies work together: as capacity grows, better strategies can be used (Matlin, 2009). Rehearsal (repeating) appears early (Flavell, 1999); organizing and linking information improve with age and can be taught; older children search their memory longer before giving up.So this means: older children don't just have more room in their heads; they use what they have better.→ Next: what happens when a skill starts to run by itself?

⭐ When a skill becomes automatic, it stops costing attention, so the child's effort is free for the harder part of the task.This is automaticity: a process starts to run by itself, without effort. Less effort is then needed in the early stages of processing: once a child decodes words automatically, her effort is free for understanding the text; poor readers spend so much effort decoding that their comprehension suffers. Our example: a new driver can't chat while changing gears; an experienced one can.So this means: young learners have limited attention to spend, so teachers warn them what to pay attention to, give outlines and study guides beforehand, and use prompts, questions and feedback to keep them focused (Meece, 2002).→ Next: the change that matters most for self-regulation, thinking about your own thinking.

⭐ Metacognition, knowing and steering your own thinking, expands greatly between ages 5 and 10.Metacognition is the "inner coach" of self-regulation, so this growth matters most (Flavell, 1999). Children learn methods for monitoring (checking how well they understand), asking themselves questions about what they read, and summarizing (Dinsmore et al., 2008). They learn which strategy fits which task, and they come to believe that using a strategy improves performance. They do switch to a new strategy, slowly, once it gives more accurate answers again and again (Siegler & Svetina, 2006). Alexander, Carr and Schwanenflugel (1995) found steady improvement with age in metacognitive knowledge and in monitoring and regulating strategy use. In plain words: the inner coach gets better year by year, gradually.So this means: a teacher can support self-monitoring from outside with self-recording, such as diaries and checklists of the task's steps (Zimmerman, Bonner & Kovach, 1996), for example: read the passage, find the main characters, decide on the main action.→ Next: how does a child's own voice become that inner coach?

⭐ Language. Children learn to steer themselves by talking to themselves: private speech.Private speech is speech that steers your own behaviour rather than communicating with someone (Fuson, 1979), and cognitive developmental theory links it strongly to the growth of self-regulation (Berk, 1986). The idea goes back to Pavlov (1927), who separated a first, perceptual signal system from a second, language-based one. He thought language and thought explain why humans are conditioned differently from animals: we don't simply react to a stimulus, we interpret it with words and experience. He never studied the second system himself; later research supported him. Luria (1961) traced how words take over a child's actions in three stages. Ages 1½ to 2½: other people's words direct the child. Ages 3 to 4: her own words can start an action but cannot reliably stop it. Ages 4½ to 5½: her private speech can start, direct and stop what she does. Luria believed this inner speech steers behaviour through neurophysiological (brain) mechanisms. Our example: a child doing a puzzle says "corners first… no, not that one", and her hand follows her words.So this means: the voice that guides the child moves from her parents to her own head. It is the same outside-to-inside thread as Kopp's phases, now with words as the tool. Vygotsky's theory builds this second signal system in; you meet it in the next tab.→ Next: if self-talk helps, why don't children always use it?

⭐ Three different problems: the child doesn't say it, says it but it doesn't steer her, or stops saying it once nobody asks her to.Production deficiency: she doesn't produce task-relevant words (a rule, a strategy, what to remember) when they would help. Mediational deficiency: she says them, but they don't change what she does next (Fuson, 1979). Young children often talk without it steering them; later their words can steer them, but they don't always talk at the right moment. This neat order fits simple self-talk, such as repeating a list; with complex self-talk both problems can occur together. Continued-use deficiency: trained children often stop talking themselves through a task once they are no longer asked to (Schunk, 1982). Our examples: one child stays silent during a memory task (production); another keeps saying "carry the one" but never carries (mediational); a third used her checklist in training and dropped it the week after (continued use).So this means: "she doesn't use the strategy" has three different causes, and each needs a different fix.→ Next: how a teacher makes a trained strategy last.

⭐ Find out why the child stopped, then fix that cause: too little practice, a strategy stuck to one task, or no belief that it helps.The chapter names three causes of a continued-use deficiency, each with its remedy. 1. She doesn't understand the strategy well enough, after too little instruction and practice → repeated instruction and practice, with spaced review sessions. 2. She ties it to the training task and doesn't see how to use it elsewhere → train on several tasks, and teach explicitly how to adapt the strategy. 3. She doesn't believe it improves her performance; she may think effort or available time matter more (Fabricius & Hagen, 1984) → give strategy value information (Baker & Brown, 1984; Schunk & Rice, 1987): tell her the strategy will help, that it helped other students, and give feedback that links her strategy use to her progress. Research shows that strategy value information improves performance, continued use and transfer to other tasks (Lodico et al., 1983; Paris, Newman & McVey, 1982).So this means: teaching a strategy is not enough; students also need to know why and when it works. Cause and remedy pairs are a classic exam question for this step.→ Last card: what you can now answer.

⭐ Developmental theory in one line: self-regulation grows from outside control to inside control, and language carries it inside.✓What is self-regulation here? Progressive changes in thinking that give more control over thoughts, feelings and actions.✓When does control appear? Kopp's phases, from arousal (0 to 3 months) to self-regulation (from 36 months); the second year is the shift from outside to inside.✓What keeps growing? Attention, working memory (digit span four at 5, six or seven at 12), automaticity, and metacognition (big growth from 5 to 10).✓What carries control inside? Private speech, in Luria's three stages.✓Why isn't helpful self-talk used? Production, mediational and continued-use deficiencies, and the three causes and remedies.Typical exam shapes: place a child's behaviour in a phase; diagnose which deficiency a case shows; name the remedy for a given cause.→ Next tab: ② Social constructivist. Vygotsky takes the same idea, words moving inside, further.

⚠ The trap✗ If a child does not use a strategy she was trained in, she has a production deficiency.✓ Stopping after training, once no longer required, is a continued-use deficiency. A production deficiency is failing to produce helpful verbalizations at all; a mediational deficiency is producing them without them steering behaviour.
❓ This step answers: What does social constructivist theory assume, which theories do learners construct, and what do Vygotsky's private speech and zone of proximal development add?

If learners build their own knowledge, what do they build about themselves, and how does help from others become their own skill?Why this matters. Developmental theory showed what grows with age. Social constructivism adds that learners are not passive: they actively build theories about their abilities, their tasks and their strategies, and those theories, right or wrong, steer how they regulate themselves.In this tab you learn four things:1The five assumptions of social constructivism2The theories learners build, and how they go wrong3Mindsets and the identity as a student4Vygotsky: private speech and the zone of proximal development

⭐ Learners build their own knowledge, driven from within, and how far they can build depends on their development.The chapter names five assumptions (Paris & Byrnes, 1989; Paris et al., 2001). 1. People are intrinsically motivated to learn: from birth they explore and try to understand, and they give meaning to what they see based on their experience. 2. Mental representations change with development: the way you hold knowledge in mind changes; infants think in actions and images, older learners use their culture's tools, such as language and symbols. 3. Understanding keeps being refined: fitting what you know with what you meet never ends, pushed by reflection, experience, others' guidance and new information. 4. Development limits learning: readiness is maturation plus prior experience, and learning works best when new information fits it. 5. Reflection and reconstruction drive learning: teaching can help, but the main motor is the learner's own need to rethink.So this means: together they say the drive is inside (1, 5), what is built changes with age (2, 4), and building never stops (3). So self-regulation must be something the learner builds, not something she is given: theories about what she can do and why.→ Next: which theories, and what if they are wrong?

⭐ Learners build theories about their abilities, about tasks, and about how to use effort and strategies, and these theories can be wrong.The theories change with development. Take attributions, the causes you give for your results. Young children's attributions do not separate ability from effort; a distinct idea of ability appears later (Nicholls, 1978), and then children realize that performance may not match ability and that effort and help also matter. Children also build their own versions of the strategies they are taught. Knowing a strategy includes its goal, the tasks it suits, how it helps and how much effort it costs (Borkowski et al., 1987). Most strategies suit specific tasks, but some, such as setting goals and checking progress, work across many (Pressley et al., 1990; Zimmerman, 2011). Because theories are built on incomplete information, they can go wrong. A buggy algorithm is such a wrong strategy (Brown & Burton, 1978): always subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in each column. Our example: 52 − 17 becomes 45 instead of 35. The method still produces answers, so the child feels capable: a false sense of self-efficacy (self-efficacy is your belief about what you are capable of): a big gap between what she believes she can do and what she can do.So this means: a wrong theory steers behaviour as surely as a right one, so a teacher needs to find out what students believe, not only what they do.→ Next: the theories learners build about themselves.

⭐ Learners build a mindset: a theory about whether their ability to learn can grow. It decides whether effort and new strategies seem worth it.Dweck's mindsets are theories about your own capacity to learn. With a fixed mindset you believe what you can learn is limited, and no effort or help can change that; with a growth mindset you believe you can keep improving. Growth-mindset learners engage more productively and persevere when they meet obstacles (Dweck, 2006). Why this matters for regulation, in our words: a learner who thinks effort is useless has no reason to plan, try a new strategy or keep going when it gets hard. These self-theories come partly from teachers, peers and parents, and largely from reflecting on your own results, your surroundings and others' reactions. They are built with the culture's tools (language, signs, symbols), in social settings, inside the zone of proximal development you meet later in this tab.→ Next: from beliefs about your ability to beliefs about who you are.

⭐ Learners build an identity as a student, and how they study expresses who they believe they are or want to become.The end goal of all this theory building is an identity as a student, which others influence, including stereotypes about gender, culture and ethnic background (Graham & Williams, 2009). Paris et al. (2001) argue that identity and self-regulated learning cannot be separated: how you study shows who you believe you are or want to become, so strategies cannot be taught apart from a student's goals, roles and identity.So this means: successful learners build theories that help them; unsuccessful ones may build unhelpful goals and strategies. Self-regulation depends heavily on how children see themselves and their tasks (Dweck & Master, 2008).→ Next: Vygotsky, and how words from others become your own.

⭐ Vygotsky expected thinking aloud to rise until about 6 or 7 and then go inward; research found a messier pattern.Vygotsky, a Russian psychologist, gave language the central role in self-regulation: private speech helps develop thought by organizing behaviour (Vygotsky, 1962). It is Pavlov's and Luria's second signal system again. Children use private speech to understand situations and get past difficulties, together with their social interactions; they take in the meaning of others' words and use them to steer themselves. He predicted a curvilinear pattern (up, then down): thinking aloud increases until ages 6 to 7, then declines and is mostly silent and inner by 8 to 10, although anyone may think aloud again when stuck. Research (Fuson, 1979): from about 4 or 5 to 8, the amount of private speech goes down, but the share of it that is self-regulating goes up. In many studies the amount is small and many children don't talk aloud at all. Our example: an adult muttering "this screw, then that one" while assembling a cupboard.So this means: the direction (words moving inside) holds; the neat curve does not.→ Next: how does help from others become your own skill?

⭐ Through the zone of proximal development: help is given just beyond what the learner can do alone; as she takes the skill in, she works more on her own and the zone moves up.The zone of proximal development is the amount of learning possible with the right instruction: the tasks a learner cannot do alone but can do with some help. Teachers or peers give scaffolding, temporary support; as the learner internalizes the skill, she works more and more on her own, and the zone moves on to harder learning. Our example: learning to ride a bike with a hand on the saddle, until the hand lets go without you noticing. Our extension: tasks she can already do alone lie below the zone; tasks she cannot do even with help lie beyond it.So this means: self-regulation is internalizing skills and beliefs so that you can handle new learning situations on your own. The outside → inside thread again, now with a helper.→ Last card: what you can now answer.

Intrinsic motivation, changing representations, endless refinement, readiness limits learning, reflection drives it.✓What theories do learners build? STEP6
Theory 5 · social cognitive theory

Person, behaviour and setting, in a cycle that becomes your own

❓ This step answers: How does social cognitive theory see self-regulation, what are reciprocal interactions, and what happens when learners judge and react to their own progress?
🔺 Follow the arrows: five classroom moments. For each, say which way the influence runs; then decide what choice and self-judgment do.

Some words first. Reciprocal means going both ways. Self-efficacy (Theme 2) is the belief that you can learn or perform something at a given level. A standard is the level you hold your work against. In the picture: a triangle of person (your thoughts and beliefs), behaviour (what you do) and environment (the people and setting around you), with arrows both ways along each side. For each moment you pick the direction of the influence and the arrow lights up. On the right, the six areas in which a learner can make choices.

In social cognitive theory, self-regulation lives where learners have choices. Zimmerman (1998) names six areas: motives, methods, time, outcomes, physical environment and social environment. If a teacher fixes all six, students can still learn, but the control lies outside them. In plain words: no choice, no self-regulation.

Bandura's (1986) reciprocal interactions (Figure 3.1): person, behaviour and environment influence each other. Self-efficacy, your belief that you can learn or perform at a given level, shows it. Person ↔ behaviour: self-efficacy steers effort, and progress raises self-efficacy. Person ↔ environment: low expectations of a pupil with a learning disability can lower her self-efficacy; "I know you can do this" can raise it. Behaviour ↔ environment: pupils look where the teacher points; wrong answers make the teacher reteach.

Three classic processes (Bandura, 1986): self-observation (much like self-monitoring), self-judgment (comparing your performance with your goal) and self-reaction (how you respond). Seeing progress raises self-efficacy and motivation. Even a poor result need not lower motivation, if you believe you can improve. Higher goals bring more effort, as long as you believe you can reach them (Locke & Latham, 2002).

The social cognitive learner is never a closed system: beliefs steer behaviour, behaviour changes the setting, and the setting talks back to beliefs. The next tab orders these processes in time.

⚠ The trap✗ A negative self-evaluation always lowers a student's motivation.✓ Negative evaluations need not decrease motivation if students believe they are capable of improving, for example with more effort or another strategy. Motivation does not increase if they believe they lack the ability to succeed.
📝 Exam-style questionGive an example from the chapter of a reciprocal influence for each of the three pairs of factors: person and behaviour, person and environment, behaviour and environment.

Model answer: Personal → behaviour: self-efficacy influences task choice, effort, persistence and achievement. Behaviour → personal: noticing progress on a term paper raises self-efficacy. Environment → personal: teachers who expect less of students with learning disabilities give feedback that can lower their self-efficacy (and persuasive statements can raise it). Behaviour → environment: incorrect answers lead the teacher to reteach. Environment → behaviour: students look at a visual the teacher points to.

❓ This step answers: What happens before, during and after learning in Zimmerman's three-phase model, and what did research find?
🔁 Turn the wheel: six moments from one study evening. Tap the phase each belongs to, then predict a basketball experiment.

Some words first. Forethought means thinking ahead. Volitional means having to do with the will, keeping yourself at a task. An attribution is the cause you give for a result. A free throw is an unopposed shot in basketball. In the picture: a wheel with three sectors and plain names (before, during, after). Six moments appear; you tap the sector where each belongs, and the sector shows its official name and processes. Then you predict the result of an experiment.

Think of a sports match: preparation, the match, the review that shapes the next preparation. Zimmerman's (2000) three-phase cycle adds "before" to the classic "during and after": forethought (before), performance (volitional) control (during) and self-reflection (after the task and during pauses). Other models name similar phases (Pintrich, 2000; Lord et al., 2010).

Forethought: set goals, plan strategies, believe you can (self-efficacy). Performance control: use the strategies, monitor and record how it goes. Self-reflection: judge your progress, explain the result, keep or change the strategy, set new goals. Then the cycle restarts: one round's reflection becomes the next round's forethought.

DiBenedetto and Zimmerman (2010): high-achieving science students used more self-regulation in all three phases. Cleary, Zimmerman and Keating (2006): novice basketball players learned one phase (set goals), two (also self-record) or three (also make attributions, the cause you give for a miss, and adjust strategy). Found: three phases gave the most adaptive attributions and corrections; two and three phases shot most accurately. In plain words: goal setting alone is not enough.

The cycle turns a study session into a loop that improves itself. For teachers, the free-throw study says: teach students to monitor during the task and reflect afterwards, not only to set goals. Reading 3 works this cycle out in depth.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-efficacy only plays a role after the task, when students judge how they did.✓ In Zimmerman's model, a sense of self-efficacy for learning is part of the forethought phase, before the task, together with goal setting and strategic planning.
📝 Exam-style questionIn Cleary, Zimmerman and Keating (2006), which group made the most adaptive attributions, and which groups shot most accurately? What does this suggest for teaching?

Model answer: The three-phase group (goal setting, self-recording, and attributions and strategy adjustments after misses) made the most adaptive attributions and strategy corrections; the two-phase and three-phase groups shot most accurately. It suggests that teaching self-regulation across all phases, not only goal setting in forethought, has desirable effects on motivation and performance.

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop from social sources to self-sources, according to Schunk and Zimmerman's four levels?
🪜 Climb the stairs: four moments in Mira's learning of a revision strategy. Tap the step she is on, then judge two claims.

Some words first. A model is a person whose behaviour you observe and learn from. To emulate is to imitate the general form of something, not every detail. A transfer task is a new task on which you use a skill you learned elsewhere. To internalize is to make something your own. In the picture: four stairs with plain descriptions, and a band that shows where the source of the skill lies: mainly in others on the first two stairs, mainly in yourself on the last two. You place Mira's moments on the stairs.

Nobody learns to cook alone from scratch: you watch, copy, cook alone with the recipe, then improvise. Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) proposed that self-regulation develops first from social sources and then from self-sources, in four levels.

Observation: watching models shows the main features of a strategy. Emulation: practising the general pattern with guidance and feedback, imitating the style rather than copying. Self-control: using the strategy alone, but still following the model's way. Self-regulation: adapting the strategy to new situations, driven by goals and self-efficacy. Levels one and two are social; three and four are internal.

Even advanced learners keep using teachers, coaches and peers to refine their skills (Zimmerman, 2000). Self-regulation does not mean social independence; it means taking more and more control of your own learning. Learning alone is possible, but it misses what the social environment offers.

The levels give a teaching plan: show it, let students try it with feedback, let them do it alone, then let them adapt it. Steps 8 and 9 show experiments and a programme built on this.

⚠ The trap✗ At the emulation level, the learner copies the model's actions and words exactly.✓ Emulation means the performance approximates the general form of the model's: learners imitate general patterns or styles (the type of question), not the model's exact words.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat distinguishes the self-control level from the self-regulation level in Schunk and Zimmerman's progression?

Model answer: At the self-control level, learners use a strategy independently on transfer tasks, but their use still follows representational standards of the modeled performance and self-reinforcement. At the self-regulation level, learners systematically adapt the strategy to changes in personal and situational conditions: they choose when to use it and adjust it themselves, motivated by goals and self-efficacy, with little or no guidance from models.

The research · what self-regulating students do, and what helps

Fourteen processes, the right goals, and keeping track

❓ This step answers: Which self-regulated learning processes do students report, who uses which, and how can they be measured?
📋 Name the process: five students describe what they do; you name the category. Then predict who uses which.

Some words first. A structured interview asks every participant the same questions in the same order. A category is a group of similar answers. Gifted students are students identified as highly able. A questionnaire measures with written statements that people rate. In the picture: fourteen tiles, the categories of Table 3.2. Each lights up when one of your answers uses it. Below the scene you find the whole table as text.

Research first asked which processes students use. Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons (1986) interviewed students about eight situations, such as writing a paper or preparing a test. Answers fell into fourteen categories (Table 3.2), including self-evaluating; organizing and transforming; goal setting and planning; seeking information; keeping records and monitoring; environmental structuring; self-consequating; rehearsing and memorizing; seeking help from peers, teachers or adults; and reviewing tests, notes or texts.

Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons (1990), grades 5, 8 and 11. Found: compared with younger students, older students reviewed notes more and textbooks less, asked teachers more and parents less, and kept more records, organized more and planned more. Girls reported more record keeping, environmental structuring and goal setting than boys. Gifted students organized more, rewarded themselves more and sought more peer help. In plain words: self-regulation grows with age and differs between groups.

Pintrich and De Groot (1990) used the MSLQ, a questionnaire with two parts: motivational beliefs (self-efficacy, intrinsic value, test anxiety) and learning strategies (cognitive strategy use, self-regulation). An item might ask whether you put important ideas into your own words. Keeping positive beliefs about learning is itself a self-regulation strategy (Zimmerman, 2011).

Self-regulation is a repertoire, not one skill, and students differ in which parts they use. Three of the fourteen categories are about asking others for help. Next: goals, studied in a series of experiments.

❓ This step answers: Which properties of goals improve self-regulated learning, according to the experiments in the chapter?
🎯 Run the goal lab: four experiments. Before each result appears, predict which group came out on top.

Some words first. A proximal goal is near in time (finish this set today); a distant goal is far away (finish everything by the end). A process goal (learning goal) is about learning how to do something; a product goal (performance goal) is about the result, such as a score. A condition is one of the groups in an experiment. In the picture: four small lab benches, one per experiment. You predict which condition did best; then the bars show the order.

Goal setting is the core of forethought. But which goals work best? Researchers varied the properties of goals (Locke & Latham, 2002): how near they are, how hard, and whether they aim at learning a process or at a result.

Bandura and Schunk (1981): children practising subtraction aimed at one set per session (near goal), all sets by the end (distant goal), or simply working hard (general goal). Found: near goals gave the best practice, self-efficacy and achievement; the distant goal was no better than the general one. Schunk (1983a): difficult but reachable goals raised motivation and achievement; being told "you can do this" raised self-efficacy. In plain words: close and challenging beats far away and vague.

Schunk and Swartz (1993): children learning to write paragraphs had a process goal (learn the strategy), a product goal (write paragraphs) or a general goal (do your best); half the process-goal children also got progress feedback. Found: process goal plus feedback was most effective on self-efficacy, writing, self-evaluated progress and strategy use; gains held after 6 weeks and transferred to new paragraph types. In plain words: aim at learning the method and see your progress.

Zimmerman and Kitsantas (1996, 1997): girls learning darts focused on the throwing steps (process goal) or on their score (product goal). Process goals and self-recording raised self-efficacy and performance. A shifting goal, first the steps and then the score once the steps were automatic, worked best of all. In plain words: learn the technique first, then aim for results.

Four lessons (our summary): make goals near; make them hard but reachable; start with learning the process, with progress feedback; switch to outcome goals once the process is automatic. That switch is the move from self-control to self-regulation.

⚠ The trap✗ Any goal helps: even a distant goal is better than being told to work productively.✓ In Bandura and Schunk (1981), the distant goal resulted in no benefits compared with the general goal; only the proximal goal improved self-regulated practice, self-efficacy and achievement.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat was the shifting-goal condition in Zimmerman and Kitsantas (1997), and what did it produce?

Model answer: Girls learning to throw darts first pursued a process goal (performing the steps properly); when the steps ran automatically, they switched to a product goal: high scores. The shifting goal led to the highest self-efficacy and performance, higher than a process goal or a product goal alone.

❓ This step answers: What do self-evaluation, progress feedback and self-monitoring add to self-regulated learning?
📈 Predict the dashboard: four findings about keeping track of your progress.

Some words first. Self-evaluation is judging your own capability or progress. Progress feedback is information that shows you are getting better. Time on task is the time actually spent working on the task. External monitoring means someone else records your progress. In the picture: two panels, one per line of research. You predict the outcome; then the bars show which group did best.

Working toward a goal, learners need to see they are getting closer, but progress is often hard to see. Progress feedback supports self-efficacy and motivation, and skilled learners get better at judging their own progress.

Schunk (1996): students learning fractions had a learning goal (learn how) or a performance goal (just solve); half in each group evaluated their own skills after each session. Found: both learning-goal groups and the self-evaluating performance group reached higher self-efficacy, skill and motivation than the performance group without self-evaluation. With infrequent self-evaluation, only learning goals helped. Schunk and Ertmer (1999) found the same with college students. In plain words: evaluate often, and if you rarely do, aim at learning.

Sagotsky, Patterson and Lepper (1978): pupils who monitored their maths work increased time on task and achievement; daily goal setting had little effect, perhaps because they had not learned to set realistic goals. Schunk (1983b): pupils who recorded their own progress and pupils whose progress an adult recorded improved equally, and more than pupils without recording, with the same amount of work. In plain words: what helps is making progress visible, not who writes it down.

Make progress visible: frequent self-evaluation lifts results, and recording helps whoever does it. Our tip for teachers: record progress for young learners first, then hand the recording over. Next: learning from others.

⚠ The trap✗ Schunk (1983b) showed that students must monitor their own progress; an adult doing it for them does not help.✓ Self-monitoring and external monitoring by an adult enhanced self-efficacy and achievement equally well, and better than no monitoring. The key was monitoring of progress, not who performed it.
📝 Exam-style questionIn Schunk (1996), which condition did worse than the other three in the first study, and what does the overall pattern suggest about self-evaluation and goals?

Model answer: The performance goal without self-evaluation produced lower self-efficacy, skill and motivation than both learning-goal groups and the performance-goal group that evaluated itself. Frequent self-evaluation raised outcomes regardless of goal type, whereas infrequent self-evaluation promoted self-regulated learning and self-efficacy only with learning goals; self-evaluation may fit better with learning goals.

STEP8
The research · learning from others, and talking yourself through it

Who to watch, and what to say to yourself

❓ This step answers: How do models affect students' self-efficacy and self-regulation, and why can a model who struggles be better than a perfect one?
👥 Choose the model: eight studies on watching others. Predict each result, and watch the gallery of models fill in.

Some words first. A mastery model performs competently from start to finish. A coping model first struggles and makes errors, then improves and gains confidence. A peer is someone of your own age or level. Self-modeling means learning from watching yourself, for example on video. A path analysis tests a chain of influences (A affects B, B affects C). In the picture: a gallery of models. You predict each study's result; the matching model card then shows what was found.

Watching someone succeed teaches you the steps and also that you can do it. Models raise self-efficacy and pass on standards, especially when the observer feels similar to the model (Schunk & Pajares, 2009).

Zimmerman and Ringle (1981): children who watched a pessimistic model struggle long with a puzzle lowered their own self-efficacy. Brown and Inouye (1978): students told they were more competent than a failing model kept higher self-efficacy and persisted longer. Schunk (1981): modeling and written instruction both raised self-efficacy and achievement, but modeling raised achievement more and made self-efficacy more accurate. In plain words: what a model shows rubs off on you.

A coping model struggles and recovers; a mastery model is flawless. Schunk and Hanson (1985), subtraction: peer coping and peer mastery models worked equally well, both better than an adult model, which beat no model. Schunk, Hanson and Cox (1987), fractions, a task the children had failed before: peer coping models beat peer mastery models, and several peer models did better than one mastery model. In plain words: when a task is new or hard, a struggling peer helps most.

Schunk and Hanson (1989): children who watched videos of themselves solving problems gained self-efficacy, motivation and strategy use (self-modeling). Zimmerman and Kitsantas (2002): watching a model was the observation level, practice with or without feedback the emulation level. Coping model beat mastery model beat no model, and feedback helped. The dart study (1997) covered the next step: from self-control (doing the steps) to self-regulation (adapting them to hit a score).

The best model for a beginner is often not the most brilliant one, but a peer who struggles, recovers and succeeds, or several peers, so every student finds someone like herself. Next tab: the help a learner gives herself by talking.

⚠ The trap✗ A model who performs perfectly is always the best model, because students see exactly how it should be done.✓ For fractions, a task the children had failed at before, peer coping models raised self-efficacy and achievement more than peer mastery models; in writing revision, coping models also beat mastery models. Coping models may work best when students are unfamiliar with a task or have had difficulties.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain why multiple peer models can work better than a single mastery model, according to Schunk, Hanson and Cox (1987).

Model answer: Several peer models, coping or mastery, did as well as one coping model and better than one mastery model. With several models, learners are apt to perceive themselves as similar to at least one of them, and perceived similarity increases the adoption of the model's standards and raises self-efficacy.

❓ This step answers: How do students' verbalizations of strategies affect their learning and self-efficacy?
🗣️ Tune the voice: two experiments on talking aloud while learning. Predict the winners, then decide how to fade the talking.

Some words first. To verbalize is to put into words, here: saying the steps aloud. A descriptor is a short word for a step, such as "multiply" or "check". A verbal mediator is a word that steers what you do. Overt means out loud; covert means silently, in your head. To fade is to reduce step by step. In the picture: speech bubbles that go from loud to whisper to thought. You predict which way of talking worked best.

Saying your strategy aloud can steer you while you work. This is the research side of private speech (step 5) and self-instruction (step 3).

Schunk (1982): low-achieving children learning long division said the model's strategy words, made up their own, did both, or stayed silent. Found: own words gave the most self-directed practice and the highest achievement; both together gave the highest self-efficacy. Schunk and Cox (1986): children with learning disabilities said the subtraction steps aloud throughout, stopped halfway, or never did. Found: talking aloud throughout gave the highest self-efficacy and achievement. The authors' tentative explanation: those who stopped may have dropped the words that steered them.

Self-talk works best in the learner's own words, and students may need to fade it into silent self-talk rather than stop suddenly. The whisper stage in the picture and the link to Vygotsky's curve are ours.

⚠ The trap✗ Once children have learned a strategy by saying it aloud, the teacher should simply tell them to stop talking.✓ In Schunk and Cox (1986), children who kept verbalizing did best; those asked to stop halfway may no longer have used the words that steered them. The authors suggest students may need to be taught to fade talking aloud to silent self-talk.
📝 Exam-style questionIn Schunk (1982), which kind of verbalization produced the highest achievement, and which produced the highest self-efficacy?

Model answer: Self-constructed verbalizations (children making up their own statements, which typically included the strategies) produced the highest self-directed practice and mathematical achievement. Children who verbalized both the model's strategy descriptors and their own self-constructions judged their self-efficacy highest.

STEP9
Teaching self-regulation · two programmes

Self-regulation can be taught: a writing programme and a reflection programme

❓ This step answers: What is Self-Regulated Strategy Development, how was it used to teach story writing, and what did it achieve?
✍️ Run the writing course: five classroom moments. Put each on the right stage of the programme, then check two details.

Some words first. An intervention is a programme designed to change something, tested in research. ADHD is attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder. A criterion is the level a learner must reach. Maintenance means the gains last after the programme ends; generalization means they spread to other settings. In the picture: five stepping stones with plain names. You place five classroom moments on the right stone; each stone then shows the programme's own name.

Self-regulation does not simply grow with age; it has to be taught. Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD; Harris, Graham & Mason, 2006) teaches a task strategy together with self-regulation (goals, self-monitoring, managing effort), through teacher modeling, practice with peers and independent practice, with support that fades. It has been applied to writing, reading and mathematics, with positive effects on learning, motivation and strategy use.

Reid and Lienemann (2006) used SRSD for story writing with children with ADHD. Stories had to contain seven parts: WWW, What = 2, How = 2 (who, where, when; what they want, what happens next; how it ends, how they feel). Planning followed POW: Pick my ideas, Organize my notes, Write and say more.

(1) Develop background knowledge: learn the mnemonics and find the parts in stories. (2) Discuss it: check your own stories and chart your parts. (3) Model it: the teacher writes a story aloud with self-statements ("What is my goal?", "I like that part!"), and students make their own. (4) Support it: write together with organizers. (5) Independent performance: write alone and graph your progress.

Stories grew significantly in parts and words, and the gains held 3 and 6 weeks later. SRSD in general improves strategy use, performance and motivation, and supports maintenance and transfer. Nobody measured Zimmerman's four levels, but the stages build in observation, emulation and self-control, and the results hint that many students may have reached self-regulation.

Our summary: SRSD is the five theories turned into a lesson plan: self-monitoring and self-reward (operant), strategies (information processing), fading support (Vygotsky), and models and social-to-self levels (social cognitive).

⚠ The trap✗ SRSD studies showed that students passed through all four of Zimmerman's levels of self-regulatory skill.✓ SRSD studies have not evaluated development according to the levels; the descriptions show that observation, emulation and self-control are addressed, and the results suggest many students may have reached the self-regulation level.
📝 Exam-style questionName the five stages of the SRSD writing instruction in Reid and Lienemann (2006), and explain how responsibility shifts across them.

Model answer: Develop background knowledge, Discuss it, Model it, Support it, Independent performance. The teacher first explains and models (the mnemonics, the self-statements), then teacher and students write together with scaffolds such as graphic organizers, and finally students write independently and graph their own progress. Responsibility shifts gradually from teacher to student as scaffolding is faded.

❓ This step answers: How did Zimmerman and colleagues (2011) train at-risk college students to reflect on their mathematics errors, and what were the results?
🧾 Fill in the reflection form, then predict what the experiment found.

Some words first. At-risk students are students with a high chance of failing or dropping out. Calibration (Theme 2) is the match between how well you think you will do and how well you actually do. Over-estimation bias means you think you will do better than you do. A randomized controlled design assigns participants by chance to the programme or to a control group, so that differences can be attributed to the programme. A gateway examination is an exam students must pass to move on. In the picture: the reflection form the students filled in after each quiz, and the results, which you predict.

Zimmerman, Moylan, Hudesman, White and Flugman (2011) worked with at-risk undergraduates in mathematics at an urban technological college, where only 21% finished an associate degree within 6 years. Many were over-confident: their self-efficacy was poorly calibrated, which can get in the way of learning from feedback.

A semester-long randomized controlled study, built on the reflection phase. Instructors modeled how to correct errors. After every quiz, students filled in a correction form: compare your confidence with your result, explain which strategy failed, choose a better one, and rate your confidence for a similar problem. Completing the form earned extra points. The message: errors are chances to learn, not end points.

The trained group outperformed the control group on maths exams, over-estimated itself less before and after solving problems, and passed a national gateway maths exam 25 percentage points more often. Within the trained classes, students who reflected more scored higher. Because students were randomly assigned, the effects can be read as causal, and they were large or near large. In plain words: learning from each quiz turned over-confidence into better results.

This programme strengthens the self-reflection phase: holding confidence against results and planning a better strategy turns feedback into the forethought of the next round.

⚠ The trap✗ The at-risk students' problem was low self-efficacy, so the programme tried to raise their confidence.✓ Many of them reported overly confident self-efficacy. The programme aimed at better calibration: comparing confidence with actual outcomes, which reduced over-estimation bias before and after solving problems.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy can the results of Zimmerman et al. (2011) be interpreted causally, and what were the two main outcomes for achievement and for self-efficacy?

Model answer: It was a true experiment with random assignment of at-risk students to classes, so differences can be attributed to the self-regulation training. Achievement: the trained students outperformed control students on instructor-developed exams and their pass rate on a national gateway mathematics exam rose by 25 percentage points. Self-efficacy: they showed less over-estimation bias in self-efficacy before solving problems and in self-evaluations afterwards.

❓ This step answers: Which questions does the chapter leave open, and what does the whole chapter add up to?
🔬 Sort the claims: shown by the research in this chapter, or still an open question?

Some words first. Longitudinal research follows the same people over a long time. Transfer means using a skill outside the setting where you learned it. Hypermedia are computer environments with linked text, images and video. Distance education is education at a distance, often online. In the picture: two baskets. Each claim lands in the one you choose: shown by research reviewed in this chapter, or still an open question.

Two literatures need to meet: development and self-regulation. Key question: is it better to teach children strategies, or help them construct their own? The authors propose comparing direct teaching with group construction, using peers as teachers in the direct condition so the type of model is held equal. Parents also differ greatly in how much they teach self-regulation, so long-term studies of the home are needed.

Study skills taught separately may not transfer. Taught inside a subject, self-regulation improved motivation, learning and achievement in reading, writing, science and mathematics (Zimmerman & Schunk, 2011). It also shows students the value of strategies, and students who see no value will not use them. The authors' example: one teacher teaches term-paper strategies and the maths, science and history teachers show how to adapt them.

The authors call it unwarranted to assume self-regulation works the same in every culture: cultures differ in values and beliefs (McInerney, 2011). Students from cultures that stress effort and structure, such as China and Japan (McInerney, 2008), may thrive in structured settings but show less self-regulated behaviour in open ones. That does not mean they do not self-regulate: regulating effort and persistence is part of it too.

Computer and distance learning lack the outside controls of a classroom, so self-regulation becomes critical there. Research should find which strategies help and how technology can support planning, monitoring and reflection (Azevedo). Bernard et al. (2009): more and better interaction between students may help learning; the authors suggest tutorials on interacting with peers before a distance course starts.

Self-regulation is now a central topic in learning research. Each of the theories has important implications for research and practice, and the authors expect more intervention studies to show how best to teach it. In plain words: we know self-regulation matters and can be taught; the next questions are how, for whom and where.

💭 Think of it this way

Learning to drive. At first you watch your instructor (observation), then you drive with her beside you correcting you (emulation), then you drive the usual route alone (self-control), and finally you handle a new city in the rain on your own (self-regulation). Every trip you plan the route (forethought), watch the road and your speed (performance control) and think back on the tricky junction (self-reflection). The five theories are five instructors who each notice something different about how you learn to drive.

✅ Comprehension check

1. A student uses a strategy perfectly in class but never at home. Which theories in the chapter help explain this, and what would they advise?

Developmental theory calls it a continued-use deficiency, and advises repeated, spaced practice, training on multiple tasks with explicit training in modifying the strategy, and strategy value information. Social cognitive theory places the student at the self-control level at best, not yet at the self-regulation level where strategies are adapted to new situations; it advises moving from process goals to adapting the strategy, with models and progress feedback. The chapter's future directions add that linking strategies to academic content helps transfer.

2. Why is operant theory not enough to explain self-regulation, according to the chapter?

By focusing only on behaviour, it ignores the cognitive and motivational aspects of self-regulation; it defines motivation as a higher rate or duration of behaviour and neglects beliefs and emotions. Behavioural methods work in the short term for on-task behaviour, but long-term goals such as a degree need a more elaborate perspective.

3. Why can the chapter interpret the effects of the self-reflection training of Zimmerman et al. (2011) as causal?

Because it was a true experiment with random assignment of at-risk students to classes. The self-reflection training led to higher exam scores, less over-estimation bias and a 25-percentage-point higher pass rate on a national gateway mathematics exam, with effects that were statistically large or near large.
