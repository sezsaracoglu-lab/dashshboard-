Social cognitive theoretical perspective of self-regulation

STEP1
Social cognitive theory, agency and self-efficacy

People are shaped by their world, and they shape it back

❓ This step answers: How does social cognitive theory explain human functioning, and why do agency and self-efficacy sit at the heart of its view of self-regulation?
🔺 Follow Sofia's first statistics month: predict each next link, and watch the triangle build.
Authors
Ellen L. Usher and Dale H. Schunk
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 2 of the Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed., 2017), pp. 19–35
Built on
The theorizing of Albert Bandura and Barry Zimmerman
Main message
Self-regulation is learned, it runs in a cycle before, during and after a task, and belief in one's own capability (self-efficacy) drives it

Self-regulation is systematically organizing your thoughts, feelings and actions to reach your goals. Think of a sailor: wind and waves push the boat, her course changes what she meets next, and her skill and confidence decide how she sets the sails. Social cognitive theory says human functioning comes from three interacting influences: personal, behavioural and environmental (Bandura, 1986, 2001). They are co-determinants: each shapes the others, in both directions.

The theory sets itself apart from three older views. Behaviour is not only a product of outside rewards (behaviourism), not only driven by hidden inner drives (Freud), and not only free choice (many humanists). In plain words: you are neither a puppet of your surroundings nor completely free of them.

Human agency is the ability to steer events in your life on purpose and to choose your reactions (Bandura, 2016). It works through four capacities: generating new thoughts (choosing helpful thoughts), forethought (planning, goals, imagining the future), self-reaction (comparing with standards and adjusting) and reflective self-consciousness (examining your own actions, thoughts and feelings).

At the heart lies the self-system: how people perceive, judge and compare themselves. The most influential self-judgment is self-efficacy, your belief that you can reach what you aim for. It is a critical determinant of self-regulation: confident learners set hard goals, monitor and correct, and persist; doubters give up easily. For Bandura (2016) it is the bedrock of aspiration, motivation and achievement.

The chapter covers environments and agency, the three subfunctions, the cycle (Figure 2.1), how self-regulation develops, six components, and finally research directions and practice.

⚠ The trap✗ Social cognitive theory says behaviour is mainly caused by the environment, through models and reinforcement.✓ It says personal, behavioural and environmental factors are co-determinants whose influence runs in both directions. It explicitly rejects the behaviourist view that behaviour is simply a product of external influence and reinforcement.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the social cognitive view of human functioning and how it differs from behaviourist and psychodynamic views.

Model answer: Social cognitive theory holds that human functioning results from the reciprocal, interacting influence of personal factors (biological, affective, cognitive), environmental factors and behaviour; all three are co-determinants. Behaviourist theories saw behaviour as a product of external influences and reinforcement; psychodynamic theories saw it as driven by hidden internal drives. Social cognitive theory adds human agency: people can intentionally shape their circumstances and their reactions, through capacities such as forethought and self-reaction.

STEP2
Environments that are imposed, selected and created

You cannot pick your school, but you can pick a lot inside it

❓ This step answers: How much of their environment can students shape, and why do many not use that power?
🏫 Follow Sofia's week: sort each moment into the kind of environment it is, then decide why some students hand control to others.

Some words first. Imposed means put on you, without your choice. Agentic means coming from your own agency, your own intentional action. To relegate a task is to hand it down to someone or something else. Aversive means unpleasant, something you want to avoid. To internalize a standard is to make it your own. In the picture: three rooms of Sofia's environment, from the one she cannot choose to the one she builds herself. Each moment of her week goes into one room.

Environments can be imposed, selected or created (Bandura, 1986). School is imposed. Within it, students select classes, friends and activities, and this selection may strongly shape whether they thrive or withdraw. They can also create their own learning environment with physical, social or technological resources, which takes a lot of agentic control. Our example: a city you did not choose, the neighbourhood you pick, the home you furnish.

Having agency does not mean using it. Many people find too much self-control unpleasant and leave tasks to external controls, such as deadlines and reminders (Bandura, 1986, 2016). Unless students believe their own thinking and doing can improve their situation, they may not adopt high standards as their own. Self-regulation costs energy and cognitive, motivational and emotional resources.

The environment is not just something that happens to a learner: she can choose within it and build parts of it, if she believes her actions make a difference. Next: what self-regulation is made of inside the person.

⚠ The trap✗ Because students can shape their environment, they will do so whenever it helps them.✓ The potential for agency does not mean it is used: many find too much self-control aversive and leave tasks to external controls, and unless students believe their own actions can improve their circumstances, they may not internalize high standards.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the three kinds of environment in social cognitive theory, with a school example of each.

Model answer: An imposed environment is put on the person, for example school itself, which students must attend. A selected environment is chosen within it, for example the classes, friendship groups and activities a student picks, which can strongly shape whether she thrives or withdraws. A created environment is built by the student with physical, social or technological resources, for example a study group or a self-designed study space; it requires a high degree of agentic control.

STEP3
Self-observation, self-evaluation and self-reaction

Notice it, judge it, respond to it

❓ This step answers: What are the three subfunctions of self-regulation in social cognitive theory, and why is noticing your own behaviour not enough to change it?
🔍 Watch Omar train for a race: decide which subfunction each moment shows, then explain why awareness alone did not change him.

Some words first. A subfunction is one working part of a larger process. A standard is the level you measure yourself against. Tacit means unspoken but understood. A mediating factor is something in between that decides whether one thing leads to another. Contingent means depending on a condition ("only if I finish"). In the picture: three gears that turn together: an eye (observe), a scale (evaluate) and a hand (react). Each moment lights up one gear.

Think of a smart scale: it shows your weight (you notice), compares it with your target (you judge), but nothing changes until you act (you react). Social cognitive theory names three subfunctions (Bandura, 1986): self-observation, self-evaluation and self-reaction.

Noticing your own thoughts and behaviour gives the information needed for change. It is coloured by your mood, memory and self-beliefs, and the more complex the task, the more attention and working memory it costs. But noticing is not enough: whether it leads to change depends on timing, on whether change feels controllable and valued, and on motivation. The chapter's teenager knows he games for hours, but feels no control and does not value exercise.

You judge yourself against standards: external ones, taught directly or picked up from models (a girl who sees her mother expect perfection), and your own. Consistent, explicit standards are internalized most easily; conflicting standards leave a person unsure how to act. People are not weathervanes: they can choose their standards, and personal bests become new ones. Self-set or internalized standards weigh most; without them there is little self-direction.

You respond to your own performance. One way is a tangible self-reward, such as a break or a treat after finishing, which helps most when outside pressure is absent (the author, the runner). Another is self-satisfaction: effort and persistence go up when satisfaction depends on meeting your own standards (Bandura, 1986).

The three work as a chain: notice, judge, respond. When a link is missing, for example no sense of control or no valued standard, noticing alone does not reliably lead to change. The next step shows that all three work in every phase of the cycle.

⚠ The trap✗ Making students aware of their behaviour, for example by having them track it, is enough for them to regulate it.✓ Self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation. Whether it leads to change depends on its timing, on whether change feels controllable and valued, and on motivation, like the teenager who knows he sits too much but feels no control and does not value exercise.
📝 Exam-style questionA student sees in her planner that she studied far less than planned this week, but nothing changes. Use the three subfunctions to explain why.

Model answer: She has self-observation: she noticed the gap. But self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation. Change also needs self-evaluation against a standard she has internalized and values (if the planned study time is not her own valued standard, the gap means little), and a self-reaction, such as a self-set reward contingent on meeting the plan or self-satisfaction tied to her standard. She may also not feel that change is under her control. Without these links, awareness does not lead to change.

STEP4
Zimmerman's cyclical model · Figure 2.1

Before, during and after: forethought, performance, reflection

❓ This step answers: What happens in each phase of Zimmerman's cycle, and how do the three subfunctions fit into it?
🔄 Rebuild Figure 2.1: follow Emma through a biology exam and place each of her moves in the right phase.

Some words first. A phase is a stage in a sequence. Task analysis is working out what a task requires. An outcome expectation is a belief about what an action will lead to. An implicit theory of ability is a person's unspoken belief about whether ability is fixed or can grow. A causal attribution is what someone sees as the cause of a result. In the picture: Figure 2.1 as a wheel of three phases around the three subfunctions; each move fills its phase.

Figure 2.1 (p. 22) shows Zimmerman's cycle. Forethought: task analysis (planning, goal setting, visualization) and motivation and beliefs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, implicit theory of ability, stereotype awareness). Performance: monitoring and strategy use. Reflection: assessment and self-reaction. The three subfunctions sit in the centre. Think of a sports match: preparation, the match, the review that shapes the next preparation.

Before acting, skilled learners analyse the task (plan, set goals, visualize) and motivate themselves. Self-efficacy is key: unless people believe they can do a task, they are unlikely to try it. There are kinds of efficacy: the chapter's teenager judges whether she can understand a long 19th-century novel (reading self-efficacy) and whether she can make time to read it (self-efficacy for self-regulation). Goals (Locke & Latham, 2013), interest and outcome expectations also steer effort.

During the task, learners monitor their thinking, feelings, effort and the task's demands, and use strategies: note taking, help-seeking, self-talk. Athletes review recordings of their performance; marine biologists keep careful notes. Children's egocentric speech during play also serves to regulate them (Vygotsky).

Afterwards, learners look for causes (attributions). Attributing a mediocre biology grade to an internal, changeable cause, such as "my study approach", lets them plan differently. They review their strategies, reward or sanction themselves, and react emotionally: frustration makes one student try harder and another give up. Reflection can also revise self-efficacy (Schunk & Usher, 2011).

The three subfunctions work in every phase. Regulation covers thoughts and emotions too, and sometimes not acting, which can take as much effort as acting. A cycle can take minutes, days or years (Zimmerman, 2011).

The cycle turns the subfunctions into a timeline: think ahead and believe you can, watch yourself while doing it, then explain, react and set new goals. Next: where these skills come from.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-observation belongs to the performance phase, self-evaluation to reflection, and self-reaction to the end of the cycle.✓ In Figure 2.1 the three subfunctions sit in the centre: they are involved in each of the three phases, before, during and after performance.
📝 Exam-style questionA student gets a mediocre grade and decides her flashcards were the wrong strategy for this course. In which phase is she, and which processes from Figure 2.1 is she using?

Model answer: She is in the self-reflection phase. She makes a causal attribution to an internal, changeable factor (her study approach) and carries out a strategic review of the strategies she used. Because the cause is changeable, she can plan alternatives, which feeds the forethought phase of the next cycle (for example revised goals and a new plan).

STEP5
Development of self-regulatory competence · social-self progression

From watching a model to running it yourself

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop according to social cognitive theory, and what role does self-efficacy play at each level?
🪜 Climb the four levels: Lucas learns to write an essay. Place each moment on its level, from outside help to his own control.

Some words first. A model here is a person whose behaviour others watch and learn from. Vicariously means through someone else's experience. To emulate is to imitate. Social persuasion is encouragement from others that you can do it. In the picture: a staircase of four levels; the bottom two are lit by outside (social) help, the top two by the learner's own control.

Self-regulation skills are mostly learned. Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) describe four levels: observation, emulation, self-control and self-regulation. In the first two, the source of influence is social; in the last two, it is internal: the social-self progression (Schunk, 1999). Our example: watch a chef, cook along with the video, cook the recipe alone, then adapt it to what is in your fridge.

Observation: a teacher or peer models the skill and the forethought, performance and reflection behind it, and also shows her own confidence, which passes self-efficacy on vicariously. Emulation: students practise the modelled behaviour while the teacher gives feedback and encouragement, so their self-efficacy grows.

Self-control: learners perform on their own and start to build their own efficacy beliefs, but still follow the model's pattern. Self-regulation: they systematically adapt the skill to new personal and situational conditions, start strategies themselves, evaluate their performance, and are driven by their own efficacy beliefs (Zimmerman, 2000).

Schunk and Zimmerman did not claim this is the only way to develop self-regulation. It is a social cognitive account, and it stresses agency and self-efficacy throughout.

Self-regulation starts outside the learner, in a model and a coach, and moves inside: watch, copy, do it alone, adapt it. Next: six components that research shows feed into self-regulation.

⚠ The trap✗ At the self-control level, the learner adapts the strategy flexibly to new situations.✓ At self-control the learner performs on her own but still uses the model's representational patterns. Systematic adaptation to changing personal and environmental conditions marks the fourth level, self-regulation.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the social-self progression and explain why it has that name.

Model answer: Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) proposed four levels of self-regulatory competence: observation (learning from a model's demonstration, including the model's efficacy), emulation (practising the modelled behaviour with feedback and encouragement), self-control (performing independently but still following the model's patterns) and self-regulation (adapting performance to changing conditions, motivated by one's own efficacy beliefs). It is called social-self progression because the source of influence is social in the first two levels and becomes internalized, within the self, in the last two.

STEP6
Research evidence on the components of self-regulated learning

Six components that work together

❓ This step answers: Which six components of self-regulation does the research describe, and what does each one add?
🧩 Predict the findings: for each study, predict the result. Its component lights up on the board.

Some words first. Executive functions are mental control skills: holding information in mind (working memory), stopping an impulse (inhibitory control) and switching between tasks flexibly. Cognitive load is how heavily a task taxes working memory. Self-handicapping is creating an obstacle in advance so that a failure can be blamed on it. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. A coping model shows her own struggle and effort, not effortless success. Stereotype threat is the worry of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group. In the picture: a board of six components; each finding you predict lights up its component.

Bandura and Zimmerman name six components of self-regulation: cognition, behaviour, motivation, emotion, social and environment. They act together: each can be the goal of an effort or a subgoal of another. In plain words: self-regulation is not just willpower; your thinking, doing, wanting, feeling, people and surroundings all take part and affect each other.

Self-regulation needs working and long-term memory, and schemas that lighten the load. It depends on three executive functions: working memory, inhibitory control and task switching (Hofmann et al., 2012). A chaotic environment or a heavy task leaves fewer resources for monitoring; self-efficacy and prior knowledge cushion this. Monitoring matters most while a skill is new; once you are skilled, heavy monitoring might impede performance (Mace et al., 2001). So athletes avoid over-thinking, and artists value incubation (pausing monitoring).

Visible self-regulation includes organizing your space, taking notes, recording progress, limiting distractions and rewarding yourself. Klassen et al. (2008): students with low self-efficacy for self-regulation procrastinated more. Self-handicapping means creating an obstacle in advance so an expected failure can be blamed on an outside cause rather than yourself (Urdan & Midgley, 2001). A meta-analysis of over 25,000 students linked it to poorer performance (Schwinger et al., 2014).

Self-efficacy decides whether people persist or give up; outcome expectations, value and interest matter too. Mastery goals go with better strategies (Schunk & Ertmer, 2000). Performance goals (beating others) make challenges discouraging and are often fuelled by believing ability is fixed (Dweck, 2006); avoidance goals breed defensive strategies that bring about what students fear. Self-set rewards motivate more than praise or money from others (Bandura, 1986); leaning on outside regulators lowers motivation.

Emotions affect every phase, and managing them goes with better learning (Pekrun, 1992). Fear can block a start, or spur action; challenge can be a catalyst. Pride with a sense of responsibility leads to renewed effort; shame or anxiety, especially when causes feel uncontrollable, can lead to avoidance and withdrawal (Pekrun et al., 2002).

Regulation is socially mediated. Coping models, who talk about their own hard-won progress, can persuade onlookers that effort will work for them too (Pajares, 2006); a similar peer can also be motivating. Knowing when and whom to ask for help is a skill. Parents and teachers co-regulate, and the learner later uses that guidance alone. Announcing goals creates accountability, and people choose settings for their social pull, such as a library or a gym.

Living situation, school climate and stressors all play a role. People often lean on external structures such as deadlines; with heavy outside demands, students need their own regulation less and may come to depend on outside control, though direct instruction can build self-observation and self-evaluation. Schmader et al. (2008): under stereotype threat, managing the threat leaves less cognitive capacity for the task; strong self-efficacy can buffer. Good self-regulators tailor their environment with timers and planners.

The six form a web: low self-efficacy (motivation) shows up as procrastination (behaviour); a threat (environment) costs cognitive capacity through emotion; a model (social) can rebuild belief. These links are our illustration of the chapter's point that the components interact.

⚠ The trap✗ The more closely a learner monitors herself, the better she performs, at every stage of skill.✓ The need for self-monitoring may be highest while a skill is being developed; once proficient, heightened self-monitoring might even impede performance (Mace et al., 2001), which is why athletes are coached not to over-think and writers value incubation.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain, with the chapter's evidence, how environmental stressors such as stereotype threat can undermine self-regulated learning through other components.

Model answer: In social cognitive theory the components interact. Where social messages are perceived as threatening, students must spend effort managing the threat (emotion regulation), which leaves less cognitive capacity for regulating their performance on the task (Schmader et al., 2008): emotion regulation comes at the expense of cognitive regulation. Whether students withstand such adversity depends partly on personal characteristics: a strong sense of self-efficacy can act as a buffer, while others give up or become paralyzed.

STEP7
Future research, implications for practice, and the chapter in one table

Build belief, hand over the goals, and know when to stop

❓ This step answers: What questions remain open, and how can teachers and parents support students' self-regulation according to social cognitive theory?
🩺 Run the clinic: six students, six symptoms. Choose the move the chapter recommends for each.

Some words first. Understudied means not yet researched much. A matched control is a comparison group similar in every respect except the treatment. A discrepancy creator is someone who sets new goals beyond the current state, creating a new gap to close. Scaffolding is temporary support that is removed as the learner becomes able. In the picture: a clinic. Each student shows a symptom; you choose the treatment, and the chapter's toolbox fills.

Understudied contexts: most research used university students or middle-class high schools; vocational, early-childhood, distance and high-poverty settings need study. High-technology environments: many schools gave every student a device, but few studies with matched controls have tested the effects on self-regulation (Zheng et al., 2016).

Feedback is powerful (Hattie & Timperley, 2007), but how do its forms affect the cycle? Could activity trackers or meditation assistants help learners? Standards and environments: people are discrepancy creators, who set new goals once old ones are reached (Bandura, 2001). Why do some aim high and others settle? (Our link: compare reading 2's discrepancy-reduction model, where studying closes a gap.)

Supporting motivation is one area where small interventions are likely to bring sizeable benefits. Teachers can build self-efficacy through mastery experiences, typically the strongest source (Usher & Pajares, 2008), through models who overcame difficulty, and through feedback that highlights capability. They can also help students see their emotions adaptively.

Self-set goals and incentives motivate more than imposed ones, and internalized plans last longer. Executive functions may be supported through role play, giving children a say in their learning, and supporting their social and emotional growth (Blair & Diamond, 2008). Scaffolding with suitable examples reduces cognitive load (Choi et al., 2014).

Effort is good, but knowing when to give up a goal or strategy is part of self-regulation; even growth-mindset messages can be misused (Dweck, 2015). Teachers can help students frame self-evaluation adaptively, for example by turning anger at oneself toward the task, and focus on what they can control.

William James saw voluntary attention as the brief effort that launches new habits, and estimated that habit makes up 99.99% of daily life. Schools are ideal places to train that attention and to show students many possible ideals.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why is self-observation alone not reliably related to self-regulation?

Because whether it leads to change depends on mediating factors: its timing (before, during or after), whether change feels controllable and valued, and motivation for the activity. The gaming teenager is aware of his sedentary life but does not feel in control or value exercise. Change also needs self-evaluation against a valued standard and a self-reaction.

2. Why do the authors say self-set goals and incentives deserve a place in the classroom?

Social cognitive theory suggests self-set goals and incentives are more powerful motivators than externally imposed ones (self-incentives have been shown to be more effective than external rewards such as praise or money; Bandura, 1986), and reliance on external regulators goes with lower motivation. Helping students set their own goals and incentives acquaints them with their agency, and internalized plans have more lasting effects.

3. Give two ways an emotion can work in opposite directions in the cycle.

Frustration after a hard task leads one student to renewed determination and another to withdrawal of effort. Fear of failure can block the start of effort, yet fear can also motivate action, and challenge can become a catalyst. Pride with personal responsibility leads to renewed effort; shame, anxiety or depression, especially when causes seem uncontrollable, can lead to avoidance and withdrawal.
