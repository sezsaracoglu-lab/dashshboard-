Social cognitive theoretical perspective of self-regulation

Usher, E. L., & Schunk, D. H. (2017). Social cognitive theoretical perspective of self-regulation. Chapter 2 in D. H. Schunk & J. A. Greene (Eds.), Handbook of self-regulation of learning and performance (2nd ed., pp. 19–35). Taylor & Francis.

STEP1
Social cognitive theory, agency and self-efficacy

People are shaped by their world, and they shape it back

❓ This step answers: How does social cognitive theory explain human functioning, and why do agency and self-efficacy sit at the heart of its view of self-regulation?
Authors
Ellen L. Usher and Dale H. Schunk
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 2 of the Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed., 2017), pp. 19–35
Built on
The theorizing of Albert Bandura and Barry Zimmerman
Main message
Self-regulation is learned, it runs in a cycle before, during and after a task, and belief in one's own capability (self-efficacy) drives it

The chapter opens with William James, who told schoolteachers in the late 1800s that a person's capacity to act or not to act, among countless options, decides human destiny, and with Bandura (2016), who said much the same: people help shape the events and the course of their own lives. Today, systematically organizing one's thoughts, feelings and actions to reach one's goals is called self-regulation. In a busy, information-rich world, whether we grow in a healthy direction depends on weighing options, committing, noticing where we go astray and steering ourselves back.

According to social cognitive theory, human functioning results from the interacting influence of three kinds of factors: personal (biological, affective, cognitive), environmental and behavioural (Bandura, 1986, 2001). This sets it apart from three older views: behaviour is not just a product of outside influence and reinforcement, as behaviourist theories claimed (Skinner); not just driven by hidden inner impulses, as psychodynamic theories argued (Freud); and not just the result of free choice, as many humanists claimed. The three factors are co-determinants, and their causal influence runs both ways. In school, too, a student's self-regulation is guided by inner and outer circumstances together.

The ability to steer the course of events in one's life on purpose, and to choose one's reaction to them, is the foundation of human agency (Bandura, 2016). People exercise agency through four core capacities (Bandura, 2001). They can generate new thoughts: by choosing and attending to helpful thoughts they can change their inner world even when the outer world is not ideal. They have forethought: they plan, set goals and imagine futures that guide later behaviour. They have self-reaction, which is central to self-regulation: when they notice a wrong course, they compare their performance with their own and others' standards and adjust. And they have reflective self-consciousness: examining their own actions, thoughts and feelings before, during and after a performance lets them influence what they think and do next.

At the heart of the theory is the self-system: the mental structures we use to perceive, evaluate and compare ourselves (Bandura, 1978), which guide behaviour. Bandura (2016) argued that no view of oneself is more devastating than self-loathing, and the chapter suggests this is likely the main reason self-regulatory processes switch on: what mainly drives motivation and self-regulation is how much of one's self-regard is at stake.

The most influential of these self-judgments is self-efficacy: a person's evaluation of her capability to reach what she wants. It is a critical determinant of self-regulated behaviour (Bandura, 1997; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2014; Zimmerman, 2013). People who see themselves as capable set challenging goals, monitor their progress and take corrective steps; learners with high self-efficacy are better self-regulated, think more positively, expect success and persevere through difficulty. Those who doubt their efficacy are less motivated, feel their effort is pointless and give up easily. Bandura (2016) called self-efficacy the foundation of human aspirations, motivation and accomplishment, and the authors stress it throughout the chapter.

The plan. The chapter first describes the social cognitive theory of self-regulation (environments, agency), then the three subfunctions of self-regulation, the cycle of self-regulation, and how self-regulation develops. Next it reviews research on six components (cognitive, behavioural, motivational, emotional, social and environmental), and it ends with future research and implications for practice.

⚠ The trap✗ Social cognitive theory says behaviour is mainly caused by the environment, through models and reinforcement.✓ It says personal, behavioural and environmental factors are co-determinants whose influence runs in both directions. It explicitly rejects the behaviourist view that behaviour is simply a product of external influence and reinforcement.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the social cognitive view of human functioning and how it differs from behaviourist and psychodynamic views.

Model answer: Social cognitive theory holds that human functioning results from the reciprocal, interacting influence of personal factors (biological, affective, cognitive), environmental factors and behaviour; all three are co-determinants. Behaviourist theories saw behaviour as a product of external influences and reinforcement; psychodynamic theories saw it as driven by hidden internal drives. Social cognitive theory adds human agency: people can intentionally shape their circumstances and their reactions, through capacities such as forethought and self-reaction.

STEP2
Environments that are imposed, selected and created

You cannot pick your school, but you can pick a lot inside it

❓ This step answers: How much of their environment can students shape, and why do many not use that power?
🏫 Follow Sofia's week: sort each moment into the kind of environment it is, then decide why some students hand control to others.

Some words first. Imposed means put on you, without your choice. Agentic means coming from your own agency, your own intentional action. To relegate a task is to hand it down to someone or something else. Aversive means unpleasant, something you want to avoid. To internalize a standard is to make it your own. In the picture: three rooms of Sofia's environment, from the one she cannot choose to the one she builds herself. Each moment of her week goes into one room.

Social cognitive theory recognizes that the environments people live in are partly under their own influence: environments can be imposed, selected and created (Bandura, 1986). Students spend much of their time in the imposed environment of school. Yet within that same imposed environment, their experiences can differ widely because of their selected environment: by choosing certain classes, social groups and activities they gain some leverage over their academic surroundings, and this selected environment may strongly influence whether a student thrives or struggles and pulls back. Students can also create their learning environments with physical, social or technological resources (for example social media); such a self-built, self-tailored environment requires a high degree of agentic control.

Having the potential for agency does not mean students use it routinely or everywhere. In fact, many people find too much self-control unpleasant and grow comfortable handing many tasks to external controls (Bandura, 1986, 2016). Parents and teachers may wish young people took more initiative, but if students do not believe that their own thinking and doing can improve their situation, they may never adopt high standards as their own or steer themselves toward them. A healthy self-regulatory repertoire takes great energy and cognitive, motivational and emotional resources.

So the environment is not just something that happens to a learner: she can choose within it and build parts of it. Whether she does depends on her belief that her own action makes a difference, which is self-efficacy again. The next step opens up what self-regulation is made of inside the person: three subfunctions.

⚠ The trap✗ Because students can shape their environment, they will do so whenever it helps them.✓ The potential for agency does not mean it is used: many find too much self-control aversive and leave tasks to external controls, and unless students believe their own actions can improve their circumstances, they may not internalize high standards.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the three kinds of environment in social cognitive theory, with a school example of each.

Model answer: An imposed environment is put on the person, for example school itself, which students must attend. A selected environment is chosen within it, for example the classes, friendship groups and activities a student picks, which can strongly shape whether she thrives or withdraws. A created environment is built by the student with physical, social or technological resources, for example a study group or a self-designed study space; it requires a high degree of agentic control.

STEP3
Self-observation, self-evaluation and self-reaction

Notice it, judge it, respond to it

❓ This step answers: What are the three subfunctions of self-regulation in social cognitive theory, and why is noticing your own behaviour not enough to change it?
🔍 Watch Omar train for a race: decide which subfunction each moment shows, then explain why awareness alone did not change him.

Some words first. A subfunction is one working part of a larger process. A standard is the level you measure yourself against. Tacit means unspoken but understood. A mediating factor is something in between that decides whether one thing leads to another. Contingent means depending on a condition ("only if I finish"). In the picture: three gears that turn together: an eye (observe), a scale (evaluate) and a hand (react). Each moment lights up one gear.

In social cognitive theory, self-regulated actions develop and start as the product of three cognitive subfunctions: self-observation, self-judgment or self-evaluation, and self-reaction (Bandura, 1986; Figure 2.1). Self-observation gives the information needed for self-directed change: on a new task, learners must pay attention to their thoughts and behaviour. This capacity for intentional self-awareness is affected by many things: one's emotional state, how memory reconstructs events, and existing self-beliefs. Task demands and prior knowledge matter too; the more complex the task, the more it costs in attention and working memory (Hoyle & Dent, 2018; Sweller, 2010). Self-awareness provides what is needed to diagnose problems, break long-held habits and set realistic standards and goals.

If being aware of oneself were enough, people would be spared many hours of hard effort. In fact, whether self-observation leads to change depends on several mediating factors. Its timing matters (before, during and/or after an event), as Zimmerman's model will show. Whether one feels change is controllable or valued decides whether change is attempted: a teenager who spends hours gaming online may be aware of his sedentary lifestyle but may not feel much control over it or value physical activity. Motivation toward the activity also contributes. So self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation (Bandura, 1986).

The second subfunction is self-evaluation: people judge themselves and their situations against external and internal standards. External standards set by schools, parents and society are conveyed explicitly (for example cut-off scores for academic programmes) and tacitly (for example social recognition for meeting high expectations). Parents and teachers teach some standards directly, hoping children will reach and internalize them; standards are also passed on indirectly by models: a girl who sees her mother expect nothing short of perfection may set the same standard and judge herself harshly.

Whether a standard is internalized depends on several factors (Bandura, 1986). Standards taught at home and at school that are consistent and explicit are often internalized easily; a teacher who sets clear standards and follows through with consistent feedback teaches children to evaluate themselves independently. When standards (imposed, modelled or self-set) conflict, a person may feel unsure how to act. But people are not weathervanes turned by whatever wind is blowing (Bandura, 1997): thanks to agency they can judge themselves against standards they have weighed, chosen or imagined. Their own past performances can also shift their standards, hence the motivating power of personal bests. Self-evaluation also depends on how much an activity is personally valued: people evaluate themselves routinely where they are invested and pay less attention where they care little. Self-set or internalized standards usually have more influence because they touch the self-system; without adequate self-standards, people show little self-direction (Bandura, 1986).

The third subfunction is self-reaction, the capacity to react to one's own thoughts, feelings and actions. One way is to give oneself tangible rewards contingent on the desired performance, which matters most when outside demands are weak or absent: an author who decides to write a book, a runner setting off on a 10-mile course; the promise of a break, free time or a tasty snack helps them push through. The wish to keep a positive self-regard can also mobilize self-regulation: effort and persistence go up when self-satisfaction hinges on meeting one's own standards (Bandura, 1986), and anticipating a positive self-evaluation can keep someone at a dull task for hours.

So the three subfunctions work as a chain: noticing gives the information, standards turn it into a judgment, and the reaction (a reward, satisfaction, an adjustment) moves behaviour. When a link is missing, for example no feeling of control or no valued standard, awareness alone does not reliably lead to change. The next step shows that these three are at work in every phase of the cycle.

⚠ The trap✗ Making students aware of their behaviour, for example by having them track it, is enough for them to regulate it.✓ Self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation. Whether it leads to change depends on its timing, on whether change feels controllable and valued, and on motivation, like the teenager who knows he sits too much but feels no control and does not value exercise.
📝 Exam-style questionA student sees in her planner that she studied far less than planned this week, but nothing changes. Use the three subfunctions to explain why.

Model answer: She has self-observation: she noticed the gap. But self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation. Change also needs self-evaluation against a standard she has internalized and values (if the planned study time is not her own valued standard, the gap means little), and a self-reaction, such as a self-set reward contingent on meeting the plan or self-satisfaction tied to her standard. She may also not feel that change is under her control. Without these links, awareness does not lead to change.

STEP4
Zimmerman's cyclical model · Figure 2.1

Before, during and after: forethought, performance, reflection

❓ This step answers: What happens in each phase of Zimmerman's cycle, and how do the three subfunctions fit into it?
🔄 Rebuild Figure 2.1: follow Emma through a biology exam and place each of her moves in the right phase.

Some words first. A phase is a stage in a sequence. Task analysis is working out what a task requires. An outcome expectation is a belief about what an action will lead to. An implicit theory of ability is a person's unspoken belief about whether ability is fixed or can grow. A causal attribution is what someone sees as the cause of a result. In the picture: Figure 2.1 as a wheel of three phases around the three subfunctions; each move fills its phase.

Figure 2.1 (p. 22) shows Zimmerman's (2000) cyclical model of self-regulation, which runs across three phases of learning: forethought, performance and reflection. In the figure, forethought holds task analysis (planning, goal setting, visualization) and motivation and beliefs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, implicit theory of ability, stereotype awareness); performance holds monitoring (of cognition and emotion, of task and environmental demands, of one's efforts) and strategy use (note taking, help-seeking, self-talk); reflection holds assessment (causal attribution, strategic review) and self-reaction (rewards and sanctions, emotional response, revision of goals). In the centre sit the three subfunctions.

Skilled self-regulators spend considerable time thinking and planning in the forethought phase, before taking any action. Here they analyse the task and motivate themselves through what they believe about themselves and their situation. Task analysis means considering what success will require, breaking complex tasks into manageable parts and choosing the strategies to use. Beliefs about one's efficacy (or inefficacy) to plan and carry out these activities influence which strategies a person pursues, and those strategies influence performance, so self-efficacy plays a key role in starting and guiding behaviour: unless people believe they can do a task, they are unlikely to try it. People may judge their efficacy for several kinds of task: a teenager may judge whether she can read and understand a long 19th-century novel for literature class (reading self-efficacy), and also whether she can set aside enough time and attention to read it (self-efficacy for self-regulation).

Other motivational tools come into play during forethought. People set goals that marshal their attention and energy during performance; goals also affect persistence and how well people plan and prepare (Locke & Latham, 2013). A goal of finishing a research paper by the end of the semester might prompt a student to book an early meeting with a librarian, do background reading and plan time for writing and revising. One's goals and reasons for an activity also shape how setbacks are handled. People also weigh how interested they are in a topic and how much success they expect; both shape how well they go on to perform, and forethought lets them visualize possible outcomes.

In the performance phase, self-regulation means monitoring one's thoughts and behaviour in the situation and choosing or changing strategies. Self-monitoring is observing one's thoughts, feelings and actions and adapting when needed; people must also monitor changing task or environmental demands. Success often requires effective strategies: athletes record and review their performances to fine-tune movements; marine biologists keep careful notes of conditions, behaviour and data. People must decide whether, when and how to seek help, and some use strategic self-talk to get through difficulty; children's egocentric speech during play serves a primary regulatory function (Vygotsky).

After performance comes self-reflection: people review the outcomes of their efforts and the behaviours that led to them. They search for attributions, perceived causes. If they attribute the result to internal, changeable factors, they can consider alternative plans: a student with a mediocre biology grade might attribute it to inadequate study approaches and work out where her strategies fell short. They also assess how effective their strategies were. And they react: self-reaction is a powerful factor in self-regulation and agency (Bandura, 2016). People reward or sanction themselves depending on whether they met their goals, which often starts a new cycle. Emotional responses can help or hinder: for one student, frustration after a hard task leads to renewed determination; for another, to withdrawal of effort. Reflection can also involve reappraising efficacy-relevant information to revise self-efficacy in that domain (Schunk & Usher, 2011).

The three subfunctions (self-observation, self-evaluation and self-reaction) are involved in each of the three phases. Because the environment, one's mental and physical landscape and one's behaviour are to some extent under one's control through self-regulatory effort, regulation is not only about overt behaviour: not acting can take as much effort as acting, and people often need to regulate their thoughts and emotions to reach their goals. The cycle applies to large and small events alike, so it can take minutes, days or years, depending on the activity (Zimmerman, 2011), and each self-regulatory event involves many interacting components.

So the cycle turns the subfunctions into a timeline: think ahead (and believe you can), watch yourself while doing it, then judge, explain and react, which feeds the next round of forethought. Self-efficacy runs through all of it. The next step asks where these skills come from in the first place.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-observation belongs to the performance phase, self-evaluation to reflection, and self-reaction to the end of the cycle.✓ In Figure 2.1 the three subfunctions sit in the centre: they are involved in each of the three phases, before, during and after performance.
📝 Exam-style questionA student gets a mediocre grade and decides her flashcards were the wrong strategy for this course. In which phase is she, and which processes from Figure 2.1 is she using?

Model answer: She is in the self-reflection phase. She makes a causal attribution to an internal, changeable factor (her study approach) and carries out a strategic review of the strategies she used. Because the cause is changeable, she can plan alternatives, which feeds the forethought phase of the next cycle (for example revised goals and a new plan).

STEP5
Development of self-regulatory competence · social-self progression

From watching a model to running it yourself

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop according to social cognitive theory, and what role does self-efficacy play at each level?
🪜 Climb the four levels: Lucas learns to write an essay. Place each moment on its level, from outside help to his own control.

Some words first. A model here is a person whose behaviour others watch and learn from. Vicariously means through someone else's experience. To emulate is to imitate. Social persuasion is encouragement from others that you can do it. In the picture: a staircase of four levels; the bottom two are lit by outside (social) help, the top two by the learner's own control.

Most self-regulation skills do not appear by themselves; they must be learned. Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) described a developmental model in which learners move through four levels: observation, emulation, self-control and self-regulation. In the first two the source of influence is social (outside the learner); in the last two it becomes internalized. That is why the sequence is called the social-self progression (Schunk, 1999).

At the observation level, social models such as teachers and peers show how to perform a task and how to carry out the forethought, performance and reflection processes. Models also give self-efficacy information, vicariously and through social persuasion: students watch their teacher model a task successfully, and the teacher shows not only the behaviour but also her efficacy to perform it. At the emulation level, students practise the observed behaviours; teachers strengthen their self-efficacy with feedback and encouragement, and students build self-efficacy as they perform the task the way it was modelled.

At the self-control level, learners start to build and shape their own efficacy beliefs (Schunk, 1999). They are internalizing what they observed, but they still use the model's representational patterns to perform. At the self-regulation level, learners systematically adapt their performance to different environmental and personal conditions and are motivated by their own efficacy beliefs (Zimmerman, 2000): they can start strategies themselves, adjust to the situation and evaluate their performance. As they go through the three phases of the cycle, their skills and self-efficacy develop further.

Schunk and Zimmerman did not claim this progression is the only way to develop self-regulatory competence, but it gives a social cognitive account of how it develops, and it stresses personal agency and learner motivation through the development of self-efficacy in all phases (the chapter's word).

So self-regulation begins outside the learner, in a model and a coach, and moves inside: first copying, then doing it alone the model's way, then adapting it to new conditions. At every level, what grows alongside the skill is the belief "I can". The next step looks at the six components that research shows feed into self-regulation.

⚠ The trap✗ At the self-control level, the learner adapts the strategy flexibly to new situations.✓ At self-control the learner performs on her own but still uses the model's representational patterns. Systematic adaptation to changing personal and environmental conditions marks the fourth level, self-regulation.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the social-self progression and explain why it has that name.

Model answer: Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) proposed four levels of self-regulatory competence: observation (learning from a model's demonstration, including the model's efficacy), emulation (practising the modelled behaviour with feedback and encouragement), self-control (performing independently but still following the model's patterns) and self-regulation (adapting performance to changing conditions, motivated by one's own efficacy beliefs). It is called social-self progression because the source of influence is social in the first two levels and becomes internalized, within the self, in the last two.

STEP6
Research evidence on the components of self-regulated learning

Six components that work together

❓ This step answers: Which six components of self-regulation does the research describe, and what does each one add?
🧩 Predict the findings: for each study, predict the result. Its component lights up on the board.

Some words first. Executive functions are mental control skills: holding information in mind (working memory), stopping an impulse (inhibitory control) and switching between tasks flexibly. Cognitive load is how heavily a task taxes working memory. Self-handicapping is creating an obstacle in advance so that a failure can be blamed on it. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. A coping model shows her own struggle and effort, not effortless success. Stereotype threat is the worry of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group. In the picture: a board of six components; each finding you predict lights up its component.

Bandura and Zimmerman described six components of self-regulation that can affect learning and performance: cognitive, behavioural, motivational, emotional, social and environmental. Social cognitive theory says they do not act independently but together: each can be the goal of an effort, a subgoal of another effort, or both.

Cognition. All self-regulation needs some cognitive engagement. Long-term and working memory are both essential (Choi, van Merriënboer & Paas, 2014). With complex or novel material, learners activate schemas in long-term memory, which relieve working memory, quickly overloaded in unfamiliar, high-load situations. Self-regulatory success depends on three executive functions: working memory, inhibitory control and task-switching flexibility (Hofmann, Schmeichel & Baddeley, 2012); a student with poor inhibitory control might mindlessly follow a bad crowd. Blair and Diamond (2008) explained that, through the interplay of person, behaviour and environment, students with better executive function are praised more for their compliance, perform better and are more motivated, while those with poor executive function likely struggle more. Learner, task and environment features together decide how burdened cognitive resources are (Choi et al., 2014): a chaotic environment makes it hard to concentrate and keep intrusive thoughts out, which self-efficacy and prior knowledge can soften, and under high cognitive demand people give fewer resources to monitoring and self-regulation. Self-regulation also depends on metacognition, thinking about one's own thoughts, feelings and actions: anticipating outcomes and reflecting on past attempts makes people more or less likely to plan. The need for monitoring may be highest when a skill is new; with experience less is needed, and once proficient, heightened self-monitoring might even impede performance (Mace, Belfiore & Hutchinson, 2001). Experienced athletes are coached not to over-think habitual movements, and artists and writers stress incubation, stopping cognitive monitoring at advanced stages of creative work (Hao et al., 2015). In this sense, self-regulation draws on cognitive processes similar to those of complex problem solving (Zimmerman & Campillo, 2003).

Behaviour. Much self-regulation is covert, but overt self-regulated behaviours form a wide repertoire: organizing one's physical environment, taking notes, recording one's performance, rehearsing, minimizing distraction and punishing or rewarding oneself. Most occur during performance, some during forethought and reflection. Zimmerman (1998) gave examples of self-regulatory processes used across domains such as writing, sport and academics: scheduling one's time, delaying immediate gratification, reviewing one's work and seeking help. Avoidant or addictive behaviours can undermine self-regulation. Klassen, Krawchuk and Rajani (2008) found that undergraduates with lower self-efficacy for self-regulation were more likely to procrastinate. Learners who strongly fear failing their own or others' expectations tend to self-handicap, so they can blame an expected failure on an external cause (Urdan & Midgley, 2001): withdrawing effort, making excuses, choosing poor study settings or compulsive behaviours such as online gaming or substance abuse. A meta-analysis of over 25,000 students found self-handicapping related to poorer academic performance (Schwinger et al., 2014).

Motivation. People can usually describe their ideal performance; without the motivational power to start and sustain the necessary behaviour, potential remains just potential. In social cognitive theory, self-efficacy is central to whether people self-regulate: the efficacious persist through challenges, those beset with self-doubt recoil when things get hard (Schunk, 2012; Schunk & Pajares, 2009). Outcome expectations also matter: expecting that one's actions will bring desired outcomes motivates using strategies, while expecting failure can lead to giving up early. Both may be insufficient if people see little value in the activity; and intrinsically interesting activities take less cognitive effort to sustain (Renninger & Hidi, 2015). Achievement goal orientations matter too: students who aim to increase their learning and mastery use more adaptive strategies and trust that their competence is growing (Schunk & Ertmer, 2000); Yeager et al. (2014) found that a self-transcendent purpose (a reason to benefit others) went with more persistence and self-control in adolescents. Students whose main aim is to outdo others or show competence may be discouraged by challenges and blame inner shortcomings, an orientation often fuelled by believing competence is inborn (Dweck, 2006), and students aiming to avoid failure tend to use defensive strategies that, paradoxically, bring about what they fear. Finally, self-set, task-contingent rewards motivate people to stick to a plan; rewards people promise themselves have been shown to motivate more than rewards from others, such as praise or money (Bandura, 1986). Withholding freely available rewards until one's performance is acceptable is a critical feature of regulation by self-reaction; most people combine intrinsic and extrinsic motivators, but the more they rely on external regulators, the lower their motivation and the less likely they are to reach long-term goals.

Emotion. How people feel before, during and after performance influences their self-regulation. How well students can manage their emotions goes together with how well they take in information and learn (Pekrun, 1992), and emotions can reinforce helpful and harmful practices alike. Emotions are most noticeable after performance, during reflection, but they influence all phases: anticipatory feelings often guide whether and how a person acts. The more one's standards, ideals and self-worth are tied to a task, the more emotional weight it carries (Bandura, 1997), and starting may require overcoming fear of failure; yet fear can also motivate action, and for many, emotions such as inspiration grow with the difficulty faced, so challenge can become the catalyst of self-regulation. Happiness and pride, with a sense of personal responsibility, can lead to renewed effort and higher aspirations; shame, depression and anxiety, especially when causes feel uncontrollable, can lead to self-destructive strategies such as task avoidance and withdrawal (Pekrun, Goetz, Titz & Perry, 2002). Emotional or physical blocks lower coping self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997), but people who become skilled and confident at managing stressors are less likely to activate distress systems such as arousal and depressive rumination (Bandura, 1989).

Social. Because humans depend on each other, regulation is socially mediated and has social consequences (Bandura, 1986). Supportive social networks help learners withstand challenges. Proficient models offer better strategies for planning, monitoring and reaching goals (Zimmerman, 2013). Coping models, who talk about their own hard-won progress, can persuade onlookers that effort will work for them too (Pajares, 2006); films about people who overcome adversity show the power of modelling on self-efficacy for self-regulation, and seeing a similar peer succeed through effort can also be motivating (Bandura, 1997). People also seek out aspirational models on their own initiative (Bandura, 2011). Part of an effective skill set is knowing when and from whom to seek help: teachers scaffold and give feedback, coaches build skills with variety, groups set collective goals and co-regulate effort, and parents talk children through the steps to take; this socially mediated regulation becomes internalized and used alone. Announcing one's goals to others (a poetry book, a long-distance race) creates a motivating social accountability system, while the self remains the primary agent; the mental picture of one's social group works partly as an approval-contingency (Bandura, 1986). Students can also select social environments with helpful social contagion, such as studying in a library among focused students or joining a fitness club full of dedicated models; connective technologies have put such proxy agents at one's fingertips.

Environment. Micro- and macro-level factors, such as living arrangements, economic status, school climate and exposure to threats, strongly influence self-regulation. Because juggling many demands takes great effort, especially with novel and complex tasks, people often rely on external structures such as deadlines, social pressure or sanctions (Bandura, 1986). Where external demands are high, students need their own skills less and may become habituated to external regulation, though teachers and parents hope they will internalize the standards over time; direct instruction can develop the subfunctions by helping students gauge progress against an external standard, through self-observation and self-evaluation (Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2014). Environmental stressors such as poverty and stereotype threat can also influence self-regulation: some redouble their efforts, others give up or freeze; a strong sense of efficacy can buffer, and children must rely on self-encouragement when outside support is missing. Where social messages feel threatening, students spend effort managing the threat and have less cognitive power left for the task (Schmader, Johns & Forbes, 2008): emotion regulation comes at the expense of cognitive regulation. Yet the environment is only one determinant: effective self-regulators tailor their environments with timers, goal sheets, planners and self-scheduled reinforcements; laptops and phones can support self-direction and monitoring, but the same gadgets are also used to avoid and distract, so they require self-directedness and self-observation (Schunk & Ertmer, 1999).

So the six components are not a list but a web: low self-efficacy (motivation) shows up as procrastination (behaviour), threat in the environment eats cognitive capacity (cognition) through emotion regulation (emotion), and a model (social) can rebuild the belief that started it. (These links are our illustration of the chapter's point that the components interact.) The last step asks what research and teachers should do with this.

📝 Exam-style questionExplain, with the chapter's evidence, how environmental stressors such as stereotype threat can undermine self-regulated learning through other components.

Model answer: In social cognitive theory the components interact. Where social messages are perceived as threatening, students must spend effort managing the threat (emotion regulation), which leaves less cognitive capacity for regulating their performance on the task (Schmader et al., 2008): emotion regulation comes at the expense of cognitive regulation. Whether students withstand such adversity depends partly on personal characteristics: a strong sense of self-efficacy can act as a buffer, while others give up or become paralyzed.

Some words first. Understudied means not yet researched much. A matched control is a comparison group similar in every respect except the treatment. A discrepancy creator is someone who sets new goals beyond the current state, creating a new gap to close. Scaffolding is temporary support that is removed as the learner becomes able. Open questions 1. The authors highlight parts of self-regulated learning that have not been studied as extensively. First, understudied contexts: most research took place at four-year universities or with convenience samples in largely middle-class public high schools; charter schools, early childhood centres, vocational training and distance education, with diverse populations such as community college students, English language learners and students in rural or high-poverty areas, would deepen understanding. Second, high-technology environments: are devices and online platforms improving students' and teachers' emotional, social, physical and academic well-being? Many school systems adopted open-use or one-device-per-student policies, but few studies used matched controls to test their effects on self-regulation (Zheng et al., 2016), and it is worth investigating how much guidance students get when told to self-direct with technology.

Open questions 2. Third, feedback, effective in many phases of learning (Hattie & Timperley, 2007): how do different forms of feedback influence the cycle? Activity-tracking watches prompt people to set new goals; what would such devices look like in education, under which feedback conditions do people give up or persist, and could tools such as meditation assistants give learners feedback on their cognitive and emotional arousal? Fourth, how people adopt standards and select environments: Bandura (2001) argued that, through self-set goals and standards, humans are discrepancy creators, not only reactors trying to reduce discrepancies; once one gap is closed, they set new challenges. Some students set very challenging goals while others are content with marginal effort; learners also select environments whose standards match their own. Which individual characteristics drive this selection?

Build self-efficacy. Supporting the motivational roots of self-regulation is an area where small interventions are likely to bring sizeable benefits. Teachers and parents should help students build healthy self-efficacy: by structuring opportunities for skill development and mastery, typically the strongest source of self-efficacy (Usher & Pajares, 2008); by using social models who overcame difficulty through persistent, self-regulated effort; and by giving performance feedback that highlights learners' capability to drive their own development, persuasive messages that can convince doubters. By attending to students' feelings, including adverse physical arousal, they can help students view their emotions adaptively and overcome negative feelings (Pajares, 2006).

Goals and executive function. Social cognitive theory suggests that self-set goals and incentives motivate self-regulation more powerfully than imposed goals and rewards, so helping students set goals and personal incentive structures acquaints them with their agency and autonomy; helping learners internalize their plans leads to more lasting effects, and highly customizable apps may suit self-regulation of learning. Training in executive functioning may also improve self-regulatory capacity: Blair and Diamond (2008) name role play, giving children a say in decisions about their learning, and supporting their social and emotional growth. Lessons that scaffold instruction and show suitable examples significantly reduce cognitive load, so students can allocate their resources elsewhere (Choi et al., 2014).

Know when to stop. Although it may be unpopular, the authors say one key to success is knowing when to give up: effort should generally be encouraged, but at some point it may work against a learner's ultimate aims; Dweck (2015) noted that some practitioners promoted a growth mindset without considering learners' long-term development. Knowing when to drop a strategy is part of being self-regulated, and adults can help learners let go of strategies that no longer work. Practitioners can help students frame self-evaluations adaptively, for example by redirecting anger at oneself toward the task, look for blocks in students' self-regulation and encourage new strategies, and help them focus on what they can control. As Bandura (1997) put it: "Focus on the controllable aspects of one's life makes the uncontrollable ones more bearable" (p. 31).

Attention and habits. Guiding students toward a lifelong self-regulatory repertoire takes more than teaching concepts and skills: unless people can pay attention when the moment for change comes, they will not use their self-regulatory powers. William James valued voluntary attention, the brief, effortful act needed to launch new routines, and called its exercise one of the most important parts of training in school, because habits formed early guide people throughout life. James estimated that habit makes up 99.99% of daily life and the remaining 0.01%, free will, is where new habits are launched, brief moments that he saw as decisive for a person's direction. Schools, he added, are ideal places to expose students to many possible ideals, and education is mainly a way of multiplying our ideals.

Learning to cook. You watch a chef (observation), cook along with the video (emulation), cook the recipe alone, exactly as shown (self-control), and finally adjust it to what is in your fridge (self-regulation). ✅ Comprehension check

1. Why is self-observation alone not reliably related to self-regulation?

Because whether it leads to change depends on mediating factors: its timing (before, during or after), whether change feels controllable and valued, and motivation for the activity. The gaming teenager is aware of his sedentary life but does not feel in control or value exercise. Change also needs self-evaluation against a valued standard and a self-reaction.

2. Why do the authors say self-set goals and incentives deserve a place in the classroom?

Social cognitive theory suggests self-set goals and incentives are more powerful motivators than externally imposed ones (self-incentives have been shown to be more effective than external rewards such as praise or money; Bandura, 1986), and reliance on external regulators goes with lower motivation. Helping students set their own goals and incentives acquaints them with their agency, and internalized plans have more lasting effects.

3. Give two ways an emotion can work in opposite directions in the cycle.

Frustration after a hard task leads one student to renewed determination and another to withdrawal of effort. Fear of failure can block the start of effort, yet fear can also motivate action, and challenge can become a catalyst. Pride with personal responsibility leads to renewed effort; shame, anxiety or depression, especially when causes seem uncontrollable, can lead to avoidance and withdrawal.
