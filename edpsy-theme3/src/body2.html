Self-regulated learning in reading

Thiede, K. W., & de Bruin, A. B. H. (2017). Self-regulated learning in reading. Chapter 8 in D. H. Schunk & J. A. Greene (Eds.), Handbook of self-regulation of learning and performance (2nd ed., pp. 124–137). Taylor & Francis.

STEP1
Self-regulation in reading, and what this chapter does

Reading well means checking whether you understand, and acting on it

❓ This step answers: What does self-regulated learning look like in reading, and why does the chapter put the accuracy of monitoring at the centre?
📖 Follow Lina's rereading: predict what she does with each text, then find the real problem.
Authors
Keith W. Thiede and Anique B. H. de Bruin; many of the studies in the chapter are their own
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 8 of the Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed., 2017), pp. 124–137
Built for
Self-regulated learning applied to one skill, reading: how accurately readers judge their own understanding, and how to improve that
Main message
Readers regulate well, but they monitor badly; better monitoring leads to better restudy choices and better comprehension

Think of a car's fuel gauge: you decide when to fill up by looking at it, and a wrong gauge leaves you stuck however well you drive. Reading works the same way. The reader monitors her understanding ("do I get this yet?") and regulates her study on that basis: she rereads, asks for help, or stops. Students who regulate their reading better understand more.

The loop only helps if the check is right (Winne & Perry, 2000). A wrong check leads to two mistakes: she stops too early, or she rereads the wrong text. How well a reader's judgments of her understanding match her actual test scores is called metacomprehension accuracy. The whole chapter is about this one thing.

Four questions: how is accuracy measured, why is it often poor, what improves it, and does better accuracy lead to better learning? It ends with open questions and advice for teachers. (Our link: this is the compare-and-fix loop of reading 1's information processing view, applied to one text.)

⚠ The trap✗ Readers who feel they understand a text usually do; the problem in reading is mainly that students do not reread.✓ The chapter's message is the opposite: inaccurate monitoring of comprehension is everywhere, even among college students, while the regulation of study (rereading what seems less learned) is already in place at a young age.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain why accurate metacognitive monitoring is crucial for self-regulated reading, using the two ways inaccurate monitoring can go wrong.

Model answer: In self-regulated reading, monitoring guides regulation: the reader rereads until her monitoring signals that the text is understood. If monitoring is inaccurate, she may direct her rereading to the wrong texts (misdirected effort) or stop reading before the text is fully understood. Accurate monitoring lets her choose the right texts to restudy, which improves comprehension.

STEP2
How metacomprehension accuracy is measured · Table 8.1

Two kinds of accuracy: how much, and which text

❓ This step answers: How do researchers measure how accurately readers judge their understanding, and what is the difference between absolute and relative accuracy?
📊 Read Table 8.1 yourself: four students predicted their scores on five texts. Judge each student's accuracy before the name appears.

Some words first. A judgment of comprehension is a reader's own rating of how well she understood a text. An inference question asks for something that is not stated literally but follows from the text. Bias is a systematic error in one direction, such as always expecting too much. A correlation is a number from −1 to +1 that shows how well two series go up and down together; an intra-individual correlation is computed within one person, across her texts. Orthogonal means independent: one can change without the other. In the picture: the chapter's Table 8.1 as bars. For each student you judge two things: did she expect too much on average, and did she put the texts in the right order?

The standard method (Glenberg & Epstein, 1985): read several short texts, rate how well you understood each, then answer an inference question about each text (the answer is not written in the text but follows from it). Accuracy is how well the ratings match the scores.

Picture two judges at a baking contest. One gives every cake 3 points too many but ranks them in exactly the right order; the other gets the average right but ranks them in reverse. Absolute accuracy: is your confidence the right size? Measured as bias (average predicted minus average actual). Relative accuracy: can you tell which texts you understood better? Measured as a correlation within one person, across her texts.

Four students predict 7, 7, 6, 5, 3. Student 1 scores exactly that: bias 0, relative +1.0. Student 2 scores 3 lower everywhere: bias +3, so over-confident (positive bias = over-confident, negative = under-confident), yet a perfect ranking (+1.0). Student 3: bias 0, but a reversed ranking (−1.0), so she would reread the wrong texts. Student 4: bias +3 and relative −1.0, poor on both.

The two are independent (orthogonal), so a factor can change one but not the other: domain knowledge affected absolute but not relative accuracy (Griffin, Jee & Wiley, 2009). From here on the chapter studies relative accuracy. Our reasoning: to choose what to reread, you only need to know which text you understood least.

⚠ The trap✗ A student whose average prediction equals her average score has accurate monitoring.✓ That is only absolute accuracy (no bias). Student 3 in Table 8.1 has no bias at all, yet her relative accuracy is −1.0: she ranks the texts in exactly the wrong order, so she would reread the wrong ones.
📝 Exam-style questionA student predicts 8, 6 and 4 on three texts and scores 5, 3 and 1. Describe her absolute and her relative accuracy.

Model answer: Her average prediction (6) is 3 points above her average score (3), so her absolute accuracy is poor: a confidence bias of +3, over-confident. But her predictions rank the texts in the same order as her scores (highest, middle, lowest), so her relative accuracy is perfect (+1.0), like Student 2 in Table 8.1. The two kinds of accuracy are independent.

STEP3
Why judgments go wrong · cue-utilization and construction-integration

You judge your understanding from clues, and many clues mislead

❓ This step answers: On what do readers base their judgments of comprehension, which cues predict a comprehension test, and why?
🔎 Sort the clues: seven readers say why they think they understood a text. Decide which clues predict a comprehension test.

Some words first. A cue is a clue you use to make a judgment. A cue is diagnostic when it actually predicts the outcome. Inferential means worked out indirectly, not seen directly. A proposition is a small unit of meaning (who did what). Salience is how much something stands out. In the picture: on the left, three levels of understanding a text, from the words on the page to a deep "situation model"; on the right, two baskets: clues that predict a comprehension test, and clues that mislead. Each reader's clue lands in the basket you choose.

You cannot look inside your own head, so you judge your understanding from clues, like judging a cake by its icing (a misleading cue) or by tasting a slice (a diagnostic one). Koriat's (1997) cue-utilization framework: judgments are inferred from cues, and they are accurate only when the cues are diagnostic, meaning they predict the test. To know which cues are diagnostic, you also need a theory of comprehension (Weaver, 1990; Rawson, Dunlosky & Thiede, 2000).

Kintsch's (1988) construction-integration model: we understand a text at three levels. The surface holds the words; the textbase holds the ideas and their links; the situation model connects the ideas to what you already know, so you can draw conclusions. Comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model (McNamara et al., 1996). So the good cue is "can I explain this?", and a poor one is "it felt easy".

Thiede et al. (2010) asked college students which cues they used; the 30 reported cues fell into five types: being able to explain, being able to recall, prior knowledge, interest, and features of the text. Found: students who judged by whether they could understand or explain the text were more accurate; those who relied on text features were less accurate. In plain words: "can I explain it?" works; "it was short and easy" misleads.

van Loon et al. (2014) measured each cue's diagnosticity (how well it predicts the test) and utilization (how strongly it drives the judgment). Found: differences in accuracy came more from utilization than from diagnosticity. In plain words: accuracy depends mostly on which cues readers lean on.

To help readers judge better, steer them to the right cue. The chapter shows two ways: retrieve before judging (step 4) and read in a way that builds the situation model (step 5).

⚠ The trap✗ Being able to recall many details of a text is the best sign that you understood it.✓ Comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model, and accuracy was highest for readers who used comprehension-based cues, their ability to understand or explain the text. Recall is a separate cue type in Thiede et al. (2010).
📝 Exam-style questionUse the cue-utilization framework and the construction-integration model to explain why readers' judgments of comprehension are often inaccurate.

Model answer: According to the cue-utilization framework, judgments are inferential: readers cannot inspect their comprehension directly, so they rely on available cues, and accuracy depends on how diagnostic those cues are of test performance. The construction-integration model says comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model, the deepest level, where text is connected to prior knowledge. Many readers judge from cues tied to the surface or textbase, such as ease of reading or text length, which are not diagnostic of situation-model understanding, so their judgments are inaccurate.

STEP4
Improving accuracy 1 · retrieve before you judge, after a delay

Summaries, keywords and diagrams help, but only later, not right away

❓ This step answers: How does retrieving information before judging improve accuracy, and why does it work only after a delay?
⏳ Run the delay lab: predict four experiments, then explain the result with the mind's "warm" and "cold" memory.

Some words first. Retrieval is getting information back out of memory. A retrieval attempt here is a task such as writing a summary or keywords. Activation is how "switched on" information is in memory right now; it fades over time. Homogeneous means all alike; heterogeneous means varied. At-risk readers are students whose reading puts them at risk of failing. In the picture: a "warm" memory right after reading and a "cold" memory after a delay, and four small experiments whose results you predict.

The modified-feedback hypothesis (Glenberg et al., 1987): your judgment reflects how much you can retrieve at the moment you judge. So, before rating your understanding, get the text back out of your head: a short summary, five keywords, or a cause-and-effect diagram. What comes back is a fairer cue than a feeling.

Our example first: right after an album you can hum every song; later, only the ones that stuck come back. Activation theories (Fletcher, van den Broek & Arthur, 1996): right after reading, much of a text is still active in working memory, even a text you did not understand, so retrieval cues are homogeneous (all alike). After a delay only long-term memory is left: little comes back for weak texts, much for strong ones. The cues become heterogeneous (clearly different), and match what the test draws on.

Thiede and Anderson (2003): six texts. One group summarized each text right after reading it, one read all six first and then summarized, and a control group wrote nothing. Found: the delayed-summary group was the most accurate. Anderson and Thiede (2008): summaries helped both typical and at-risk readers; their cue reports, analysed by Thiede et al. (2010), showed that at-risk readers relied more often on surface cues. In plain words: summarize later, not straight away.

Thiede et al. (2003): five keywords per text, written after a delay, gave the most accurate judgments. Thiede et al. (2005): the crucial gap is between reading and writing the keywords: by then surface and textbase information has faded, so what comes back reflects the situation model. van Loon et al. (2014): completing a cause-and-effect diagram after a delay beat doing it immediately or not at all. In plain words: same rule, quicker tasks.

Test yourself before judging, but not straight away. This has worked for college students and down to grade 6, but may not work in grade 4 (de Bruin et al., 2011). Note the contrast: grade-4 pupils already pick the right texts to restudy (step 6); it is the retrieval trick that may not yet help them.

⚠ The trap✗ Writing a summary right after reading each text makes readers better judges of their understanding.✓ In Thiede and Anderson (2003) only the delayed-summary group became more accurate. Right after reading, much is still active in working memory even for poorly understood texts, so immediate retrieval gives cues that do not tell texts apart.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy did delayed keyword generation improve metacomprehension accuracy while immediate keyword generation did not? Refer to activation and to the kind of cues produced.

Model answer: According to activation theories, immediately after reading, information from the text is still highly active in working memory, even for poorly understood texts, so generating keywords may feel similar for all texts: the cues are homogeneous and do not discriminate, and they do not reflect the delayed test. After a delay, activation has decayed and only information in long-term memory is retrievable, so for poorly understood texts little information comes back and generating keywords is hard, while for well understood texts much more comes back: heterogeneous cues that differentiate the texts and match what is available at the test. Thiede et al. (2005) showed that the delay between reading and generating keywords was the crucial factor.

STEP5
Improving accuracy 2 · read in a way that builds the situation model

Map it, explain it, and know what the test will ask

❓ This step answers: Which ways of reading build the situation model, and how do concept mapping, self-explanation and test expectancy improve accuracy?
🗺️ Choose the reading task: predict five experiments on how students read, then check what they show.

Some words first. A concept map is a diagram of the ideas in a text and how they connect. To self-explain is to tell yourself what each sentence means and how it fits with the rest. Test expectancy is what kind of test you expect. Metamemory accuracy is how well you predict a test of facts; metacomprehension accuracy, how well you predict a test of understanding. In the picture: three ways of reading, each with an experiment. You predict the result; the panel then shows which group judged its understanding best.

The second route changes how you read. If reading builds the situation model, its cue ("can I explain this?") is right there when you judge yourself. Think of furniture: assembling it yourself shows which joints you understood; looking at someone else's finished piece does not.

A concept map is a drawing of a text's ideas and how they connect; mapping can help less able readers most (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006). Thiede et al. (2010, Experiment 2): at-risk readers judged more accurately when they made maps. Redford et al. (2012): 7th graders who made maps judged better, but those given ready-made maps were no better than pupils without maps. In plain words: the making is what helps.

Self-explaining (Chi, 2000) means telling yourself what each sentence means and how it fits the whole. Griffin, Wiley and Thiede (2008): college students who self-explained judged more accurately than students who read the text twice in the same time, or once. In plain words: explaining, not extra time, makes the difference.

Thiede, Griffin and Wiley (2011): after practice texts with either fact tests or understanding tests, students rated new texts. Found: expecting understanding tests raised metacomprehension accuracy; expecting fact tests raised metamemory accuracy (predicting memory for facts). Thiede et al. (2012) found the same in grades 7 and 8. In plain words: what you expect the test to ask changes how you read and judge.

Making your own map, explaining to yourself and expecting a deep test all point readers to situation-model cues. Step 6 asks whether better judging actually leads to better learning.

⚠ The trap✗ Giving students a ready-made concept map of the text improves their judgments of comprehension as much as making one.✓ In Redford et al. (2012), 7th graders who examined already-constructed maps were no more accurate than a control group; the act of generating the map appears to be crucial.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the test-expectancy study of Thiede, Griffin and Wiley (2011) and what its two results show about monitoring.

Model answer: College students first read three practice texts followed by either memory tests of facts or inference tests of comprehension, building an expectation of one kind of test. They then read new texts and predicted their performance. Metamemory accuracy (predicting fact tests) was greater for the memory-expectancy group, and metacomprehension accuracy (predicting inference tests) was greater for the inference-expectancy group. So what students expect to be tested shapes how they read and monitor, and students focused on deep comprehension can accurately predict comprehension tests.

STEP6
Does it pay? · from accurate monitoring to better comprehension

Better judgments, better restudy choices, better understanding

❓ This step answers: How do students use their monitoring to decide what to restudy, and does better monitoring really improve comprehension?
🔗 Build the chain: place each finding on the link it supports, from accurate judgment to better comprehension.

Some words first. To allocate study time is to decide how much time goes to which material. A discrepancy is a gap, here between how well you have learned something and how well you want to know it. To restudy is to study again. In the picture: a chain of three links, from accurate judgment to good restudy choices to better comprehension. Each finding goes on the link it supports.

The discrepancy-reduction model (Dunlosky & Hertzog, 1997): students keep studying until the gap between "how well I know it" and "how well I want to know it" is closed. Its prediction: harder, less-learned material is chosen for restudy more often. Much data fits this (Son & Kornell, 2008), and it appears to make study time most effective (Tullis & Benjamin, 2011).

Students are fairly good at acting on their judgments. Pupils in grades 4, 6 and 7 picked their less-learned texts for restudy (de Bruin et al., 2011), at-risk readers did too (Anderson & Thiede, 2008), and even grade-1 children gave harder items more time (Dufresne & Kobasigawa, 1989). Some still find it hard to turn monitoring into action (Sussan & Son, 2014).

Thiede et al. (2003): the delayed-keyword group judged more accurately, then chose its weaker texts to reread, and ended with better overall comprehension. It was the first evidence that accuracy affects learning. Thiede et al. (2012) found the same in grades 7 and 8 (see also Rawson et al., 2011). In plain words: judge right, reread right, understand more.

The main weak link is the judging, not the rereading. That is exactly what the chapter's advice to teachers (step 7) builds on.

⚠ The trap✗ Young readers need training mainly in choosing what to restudy.✓ Regulation appears to be in place early: pupils in grades 4, 6 and 7 picked their less learned texts for restudy, and Grade 1 children gave harder items more time. What needs improving is the accuracy of monitoring.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the discrepancy-reduction model and the prediction it makes about which material students restudy.

Model answer: Students set a desired level of learning, monitor how well material has been learned, and continue studying until the discrepancy between the current and desired state reaches zero. Because the discrepancy is larger for material that is harder to learn, the model predicts that harder, less learned material will be selected for restudy more often than easier material. Much research on study-time allocation is consistent with this, and allocating study by this rule appears to make study time most effective.

STEP7
Future research, implications for teachers, and the chapter in one table

Pictures, screens, and what teachers can do tomorrow

❓ This step answers: Which questions are still open, and what should teachers do to support self-regulated reading?
🧑‍🏫 Advise the teacher: seven classroom plans. Keep the ones the chapter supports, drop the ones it argues against.

Some words first. An uninformative picture decorates a text without explaining anything. Seductive details are interesting but irrelevant additions that pull attention away from what matters. Calibration is the match between how well you think you did and how well you did. Self-paced means the learner decides how long to study. In the picture: two columns, "keep" and "drop", for six classroom plans.

Helpful diagrams raised both judgments and scores (Serra & Dunlosky, 2010). Later research found that uninformative pictures raised judgments without raising scores. Ackerman and Leiser (2014): this harm was limited to lower performers; higher performers even profited, possibly because they worked harder to make sense of them. Why such seductive details (Jaeger & Wiley, 2014) mislead is still open.

Ackerman and Goldsmith (2011): calibration (absolute accuracy) was better on paper than on screen, with the same scores. When students studied at their own pace, paper also led to better regulation and higher scores. Because real study is self-paced, students need extra support when reading on screen.

The good news: pupils already reread sensibly, so teachers need not train the rereading. The bad news: inaccurate monitoring is everywhere; even college students struggle to tell which texts they know well. So teachers should train the judging: say what students should learn, describe the test explicitly, and test deep understanding rather than facts. Students adjust how they read and monitor to the test they expect.

Teach students to make their own concept maps, promote self-testing later (keywords, summaries, cause-and-effect diagrams), and show how these relate to the test, so students learn which cues predict it. Teachers should also practise good self-regulation themselves.

💭 Think of it this way

A weather forecaster. Absolute accuracy is whether she gets the temperature right; relative accuracy is whether she can say which day will be the warmest. A forecaster who looks only at the colour of the sky (a surface cue) is often wrong; one who reads the pressure maps (the situation model) predicts well. And only a good forecast tells you on which day to take your umbrella, which is what regulation does with monitoring.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why does the chapter say teachers need not intervene to improve regulation, but should intervene to improve monitoring?

Because even young students already allocate more study time to harder material and choose less learned texts for restudy, which is effective regulation (for example de Bruin et al., 2011; Dufresne & Kobasigawa, 1989). Monitoring, by contrast, is inaccurate everywhere, even among college students, and accurate monitoring is what makes the regulation effective.

2. Name the two routes to better metacomprehension accuracy and one technique for each.

Route 1: retrieving information before judging comprehension, after a delay, for example writing delayed summaries, five keywords or completing a cause-and-effect diagram. Route 2: encoding the text so that the situation model is built during reading, for example generating a concept map, self-explaining, or building an expectation of inference tests.

3. What did Thiede et al. (2003) show that earlier studies had not?

The first empirical evidence that metacomprehension accuracy affects overall comprehension: the delayed-keyword group judged more accurately, used a discrepancy-reduction rule to restudy less learned texts, and after rereading had significantly better overall comprehension than the other groups.
