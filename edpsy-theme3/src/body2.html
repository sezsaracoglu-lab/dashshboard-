Self-regulated learning in reading

Thiede, K. W., & de Bruin, A. B. H. (2017). Self-regulated learning in reading. Chapter 8 in D. H. Schunk & J. A. Greene (Eds.), Handbook of self-regulation of learning and performance (2nd ed., pp. 124–137). Taylor & Francis.

STEP1
Self-regulation in reading, and what this chapter does

Reading well means checking whether you understand, and acting on it

❓ This step answers: What does self-regulated learning look like in reading, and why does the chapter put the accuracy of monitoring at the centre?
Authors
Keith W. Thiede and Anique B. H. de Bruin; many of the studies in the chapter are their own
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 8 of the Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed., 2017), pp. 124–137
Built for
Self-regulated learning applied to one skill, reading: how accurately readers judge their own understanding, and how to improve that
Main message
Readers regulate well, but they monitor badly; better monitoring leads to better restudy choices and better comprehension

Reading is widely seen as a basic building block for all other learning, and reading well takes two skills: decoding (turning letters into words) and comprehension (understanding what the words mean together). Self-regulation guides comprehension: students who regulate their reading better understand more (for example Thiede, Anderson & Therriault, 2003; Zimmerman, 1990). In particular, students have to monitor and control their reading to understand texts (Cromley & Azevedo, 2007).

Many models of self-regulated learning describe learning as an interplay between metacognitive monitoring and the regulation of study (for example Ariel, Dunlosky & Bailey, 2009; Butler & Winne, 1995). A student reads to understand a text and keeps track of how close she is to understanding it. If her monitoring says she is not there yet, she will probably reread (or seek help) until her monitoring says she has mastered it, and then she stops. (Our link: this is the same compare-and-fix loop as the information processing view in reading 1, applied to one text.)

The loop only works if the monitoring is accurate (Winne & Perry, 2000). If a student misjudges her comprehension, she may reread the wrong text or stop before she has really understood. If she judges it accurately, she will regulate her study well and understand more (for example Rawson, O'Neil & Dunlosky, 2011; Thiede & Anderson, 2003). So it matters to find ways to improve the accuracy of comprehension monitoring, which the chapter calls metacomprehension accuracy.

The plan. The chapter first explains how metacomprehension accuracy is measured and which factors influence it, using a theory of monitoring (the cue-utilization framework) and a theory of comprehension (the construction-integration model). It then reviews two kinds of interventions that improve accuracy, then the evidence that better monitoring leads to better regulation and better comprehension, and it ends with future research and implications for teachers. In the authors' words, they also aim to validate models of self-regulated learning by showing how much monitoring and regulation matter for reading comprehension.

⚠ The trap✗ Readers who feel they understand a text usually do; the problem in reading is mainly that students do not reread.✓ The chapter's message is the opposite: inaccurate monitoring of comprehension is everywhere, even among college students, while the regulation of study (rereading what seems less learned) is already in place at a young age.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain why accurate metacognitive monitoring is crucial for self-regulated reading, using the two ways inaccurate monitoring can go wrong.

Model answer: In self-regulated reading, monitoring guides regulation: the reader rereads until her monitoring signals that the text is understood. If monitoring is inaccurate, she may direct her rereading to the wrong texts (misdirected effort) or stop reading before the text is fully understood. Accurate monitoring lets her choose the right texts to restudy, which improves comprehension.

STEP2
How metacomprehension accuracy is measured · Table 8.1

Two kinds of accuracy: how much, and which text

❓ This step answers: How do researchers measure how accurately readers judge their understanding, and what is the difference between absolute and relative accuracy?
📊 Read Table 8.1 yourself: four students predicted their scores on five texts. Judge each student's accuracy before the name appears.

Some words first. A judgment of comprehension is a reader's own rating of how well she understood a text. An inference question asks for something that is not stated literally but follows from the text. Bias is a systematic error in one direction, such as always expecting too much. A correlation is a number from −1 to +1 that shows how well two series go up and down together; an intra-individual correlation is computed within one person, across her texts. Orthogonal means independent: one can change without the other. In the picture: the chapter's Table 8.1 as bars. For each student you judge two things: did she expect too much on average, and did she put the texts in the right order?

Glenberg and Epstein (1985) developed the standard way to measure accuracy. Participants read a series of short texts, judge how well they understood each one, and then answer an inference question about each text. Metacomprehension accuracy is how well those judgments relate to the test scores.

Accuracy can be described in two distinct ways (Dunlosky & Thiede, 2013). Absolute accuracy asks whether the size of the judgments matches the size of actual performance. It is often reported as confidence bias: the average predicted score minus the average actual score across texts (positive = over-confident). Relative accuracy asks whether the judgments tell apart the better and worse understood texts. It is reported as the correlation, within one person, between predicted and actual scores across texts. The two kinds are theoretically orthogonal: independent of each other.

Table 8.1 shows four students, each predicting scores of 7, 7, 6, 5 and 3 on five texts. Student 1 scored exactly what she predicted: perfect absolute accuracy (bias 0) and perfect relative accuracy (+1.0). Student 2 scored 3 points lower on every text: consistently over-confident (bias +3), yet her predictions still ranked the texts perfectly (+1.0). Student 3 was over-confident on texts 1 and 2 and under-confident on texts 4 and 5, so the errors cancel out: bias 0, yet her ranking was exactly reversed (−1.0). Student 4 was poor on both counts (+3 and −1.0). So a student can be well calibrated and poor at discriminating texts, or the reverse.

The distinction matters because a variable can affect one kind of accuracy and not the other: domain knowledge, for example, has been shown to influence absolute accuracy but not relative accuracy (Griffin, Jee & Wiley, 2009). So researchers have to say which kind they study. The rest of the chapter, and the rest of this walk-through, is about relative accuracy: can a reader tell which texts she understood better?

Our reasoning: relative accuracy is the kind that matters for regulation: to decide which text to reread, a reader does not need to know her exact score, only which texts she understood less well. The next step asks why readers are so often wrong about that.

⚠ The trap✗ A student whose average prediction equals her average score has accurate monitoring.✓ That is only absolute accuracy (no bias). Student 3 in Table 8.1 has no bias at all, yet her relative accuracy is −1.0: she ranks the texts in exactly the wrong order, so she would reread the wrong ones.
📝 Exam-style questionA student predicts 8, 6 and 4 on three texts and scores 5, 3 and 1. Describe her absolute and her relative accuracy.

Model answer: Her average prediction (6) is 3 points above her average score (3), so her absolute accuracy is poor: a confidence bias of +3, over-confident. But her predictions rank the texts in the same order as her scores (highest, middle, lowest), so her relative accuracy is perfect (+1.0), like Student 2 in Table 8.1. The two kinds of accuracy are independent.

STEP3
Why judgments go wrong · cue-utilization and construction-integration

You judge your understanding from clues, and many clues mislead

❓ This step answers: On what do readers base their judgments of comprehension, which cues predict a comprehension test, and why?
🔎 Sort the clues: seven readers say why they think they understood a text. Decide which clues predict a comprehension test.

Some words first. A cue is a clue you use to make a judgment. A cue is diagnostic when it actually predicts the outcome. Inferential means worked out indirectly, not seen directly. A proposition is a small unit of meaning (who did what). Salience is how much something stands out. In the picture: on the left, three levels of understanding a text, from the words on the page to a deep "situation model"; on the right, two baskets: clues that predict a comprehension test, and clues that mislead. Each reader's clue lands in the basket you choose.

To understand what affects accuracy you need a theory of monitoring and a theory of comprehension (Weaver, 1990). Rawson, Dunlosky and Thiede (2000) combined Koriat's (1997) cue-utilization framework with Kintsch's (1988) construction-integration model. The cue-utilization framework says that metacognitive judgments are inferential: people have no direct view of their own memory and comprehension, so they base their judgments on whatever cues are available. A judgment is accurate to the extent that the cues it uses are diagnostic of performance on the comprehension test.

The construction-integration model tells us which cues are diagnostic. Readers build meaning at three levels. The lexical or surface level holds the words and phrases as they appear in the text. The textbase level is built as the text is broken into propositions that are linked through overlapping arguments and other explicit features of the text. The situation model is the deepest level: text information connected to the reader's prior knowledge and used to draw inferences and implications. Performance on comprehension tests depends largely on the situation model (McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch, 1996). So judgments based on cues from the situation model, rather than from the textbase, should predict test performance better (Rawson et al., 2000; Wiley, Griffin & Thiede, 2005).

Thiede, Griffin, Wiley and Anderson (2010) asked college students, right after judging the last of five texts, which cues they had used. The 30 cues reported fell into five types: (A) being able to explain the meaning; (B) being able to recall information from the text; (C) prior knowledge of the topic; (D) interest in the topic; and (E) features of the text, such as difficulty, ease of processing, length and vocabulary. Earlier research had shown that judgments are influenced by prior knowledge or domain familiarity, by text features such as perceived readability or whether a text is narrative or expository, and by the ability to recall information about a text.

Accuracy depended on which cues students used. Students who judged by comprehension-based cues, that is by whether they could understand or explain the text, were more accurate than students using any other kind of cue; students who leaned on surface features of the text were less accurate. Van Loon, de Bruin, van Gog, van Merriënboer and Dunlosky (2014) developed a way to compute both cue diagnosticity (how well a cue predicts the test) and cue utilization (how strongly a cue is related to the judgments), and found that differences in accuracy were explained more by differences in utilization than in diagnosticity: what matters most is whether readers actually use the good cues. Looking at both diagnosticity and utilization, the authors add, could also show how interventions work.

So readers often misjudge their understanding when they lean on surface clues, such as how easy the text was to process. The cure is to direct them to the diagnostic clue: can I explain this, at the level of the situation model? The chapter reviews two ways to do that. Some interventions make valid cues stand out by having readers retrieve information before judging (step 4); others have readers encode the text in a way that builds the situation model (step 5).

⚠ The trap✗ Being able to recall many details of a text is the best sign that you understood it.✓ Comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model, and accuracy was highest for readers who used comprehension-based cues, their ability to understand or explain the text. Recall is a separate cue type in Thiede et al. (2010).
📝 Exam-style questionUse the cue-utilization framework and the construction-integration model to explain why readers' judgments of comprehension are often inaccurate.

Model answer: According to the cue-utilization framework, judgments are inferential: readers cannot inspect their comprehension directly, so they rely on available cues, and accuracy depends on how diagnostic those cues are of test performance. The construction-integration model says comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model, the deepest level, where text is connected to prior knowledge. Many readers judge from cues tied to the surface or textbase, such as ease of reading or text length, which are not diagnostic of situation-model understanding, so their judgments are inaccurate.

STEP4
Improving accuracy 1 · retrieve before you judge, after a delay

Summaries, keywords and diagrams help, but only later, not right away

❓ This step answers: How does retrieving information before judging improve accuracy, and why does it work only after a delay?
⏳ Run the delay lab: predict four experiments, then explain the result with the mind's "warm" and "cold" memory.

Some words first. Retrieval is getting information back out of memory. A retrieval attempt here is a task such as writing a summary or keywords. Activation is how "switched on" information is in memory right now; it fades over time. Homogeneous means all alike; heterogeneous means varied. At-risk readers are students whose reading puts them at risk of failing. In the picture: a "warm" memory right after reading and a "cold" memory after a delay, and four small experiments whose results you predict.

Glenberg and colleagues (1987) proposed the modified-feedback hypothesis: judgments of comprehension are influenced by how well you can retrieve information at the moment of judging. So researchers added a retrieval attempt to the standard procedure: after reading, participants write a summary (or another retrieval task) for each text, then judge their comprehension, then take the test. In cue-utilization terms, retrieving lets readers reflect on how well it went: a text gets a high rating if a lot came back, a low rating if they struggled. If what is available during retrieval is related to what will be available at the test, judgments should become more accurate.

When the retrieval happens matters, and activation theories of text comprehension (for example Fletcher, van den Broek & Arthur, 1996) explain why. While reading, activation spreads, so right after reading a lot is still active in working memory, even for a text you did not understand well. According to these theories, retrieving immediately may then feel much the same for well and poorly understood texts: the cues may be homogeneous, may not tell texts apart, and may say little about a test that comes later, so one might predict poor accuracy. After a delay, the activation has faded and only what is in long-term memory is left: for a poorly understood text little comes back, for a well understood one much more. The cues should become heterogeneous and highlight the difference between texts, and because both the delayed retrieval and the test draw on long-term memory, they should predict test performance well.

Writing summaries can improve comprehension in several ways that researchers have proposed: by linking concepts with each other and with prior knowledge, by focusing attention on the important information, and by prompting self-testing that can reveal comprehension breaks and invite fix-up strategies. It also improves comprehension by improving accuracy and making self-regulated study more effective (Thiede & Anderson, 2003). In that study, a control group read the texts, judged them and took the tests; an immediate-summary group summarized each text right after reading it; a delayed-summary group read all six texts first and then wrote the summaries. Accuracy was greater for the delayed-summary group than for the other two. With a repeated-measures design, Anderson and Thiede (2008) showed that summarizing improved accuracy for both typical college students and at-risk readers; the cue data, analysed by Thiede et al. (2010), showed that at-risk readers relied more often on less valid surface-feature cues than typical readers did.

Because summaries take a lot of time, Thiede et al. (2003) tried a quicker task: writing five keywords that capture the essence of each text. Accuracy was greater for the delayed-keyword group than for the immediate-keyword and control groups; Chen (2009) replicated this with college readers, and it has been extended to younger children (de Bruin, Thiede, Camp & Redford, 2011). Thiede, Dunlosky, Griffin and Wiley (2005) varied the time between reading and writing keywords and between keywords and judging, and found that the delay between reading and keyword generation was the crucial ingredient: generating keywords once textbase information had faded produced a robust positive effect. Van Loon et al. (2014) had students fill in the missing parts of a cause-and-effect diagram of a text, immediately or after a delay: accuracy was greater for the delayed-diagramming group than for the immediate and no-diagramming groups, which the authors attributed to better use of diagnostic cues. Anderson and Thiede (2008) also hypothesized that retrieval tasks may make situation-model cues stand out at the moment of judging.

So the rule is: test yourself before you judge, but not right away. Retrieval improves accuracy only when it happens after a delay, when surface features have had a chance to fade. The effects appear robust: they have been found for typical and at-risk college students and for students as young as 6th grade, but retrieval tasks may not work for students as young as 4th grade (de Bruin et al., 2011). The next step shows the second route: changing how students read in the first place.

⚠ The trap✗ Writing a summary right after reading each text makes readers better judges of their understanding.✓ In Thiede and Anderson (2003) only the delayed-summary group became more accurate. Right after reading, much is still active in working memory even for poorly understood texts, so immediate retrieval gives cues that do not tell texts apart.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy did delayed keyword generation improve metacomprehension accuracy while immediate keyword generation did not? Refer to activation and to the kind of cues produced.

Model answer: According to activation theories, immediately after reading, information from the text is still highly active in working memory, even for poorly understood texts, so generating keywords may feel similar for all texts: the cues are homogeneous and do not discriminate, and they do not reflect the delayed test. After a delay, activation has decayed and only information in long-term memory is retrievable, so for poorly understood texts little information comes back and generating keywords is hard, while for well understood texts much more comes back: heterogeneous cues that differentiate the texts and match what is available at the test. Thiede et al. (2005) showed that the delay between reading and generating keywords was the crucial factor.

STEP5
Improving accuracy 2 · read in a way that builds the situation model

Map it, explain it, and know what the test will ask

❓ This step answers: Which ways of reading build the situation model, and how do concept mapping, self-explanation and test expectancy improve accuracy?
🗺️ Choose the reading task: predict five experiments on how students read, then check what they show.

Some words first. A concept map is a diagram of the ideas in a text and how they connect. To self-explain is to tell yourself what each sentence means and how it fits with the rest. Test expectancy is what kind of test you expect. Metamemory accuracy is how well you predict a test of facts; metacomprehension accuracy, how well you predict a test of understanding. In the picture: three ways of reading, each with an experiment. You predict the result; the panel then shows which group judged its understanding best.

The second route is to give reading instructions that promote building the situation model. If readers build it while reading, cues from the situation model should stand out when they judge their comprehension, which should improve accuracy.

Concept mapping. A concept map shows the underlying structure of a text's meaning; making one is an organizational strategy that helps readers connect the concepts in a text (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986), and it can be particularly helpful for less able readers (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006). Thiede et al. (2010, Experiment 2) found that at-risk readers judged more accurately when they constructed concept maps than when they did not. Redford, Thiede, Wiley and Griffin (2012) showed the same with 7th graders, and added a telling detail: students who were given ready-made concept maps to look at while reading were no more accurate than a control group. So the act of generating the map appears to be crucial; the authors speculated that making it raises the salience of situation-model cues.

Self-explanation. Chi (2000) developed self-explanation to improve comprehension: while reading, students explain to themselves what each sentence or paragraph means and how it relates to the whole and to earlier information, which focuses them on their situation model. Griffin, Wiley and Thiede (2008) predicted that self-explaining would also focus students on situation-model cues when judging, and found that college students who self-explained were more accurate than a group who read the texts twice (with the same study time) and a control group who read them once.

Test expectancy. Another way is to give students experience with tests that assess deep comprehension, because expecting a certain kind of test changes how texts are encoded and monitored (Thomas & McDaniel, 2007). Thiede, Griffin and Wiley (2011) gave college students three practice texts with either memory tests of facts or inference tests of comprehension, then new texts to predict. Metamemory accuracy was greater for the group expecting memory tests; metacomprehension accuracy was greater for the group expecting inference tests. So test expectancy influences how students monitor, and students focused on deep comprehension can predict comprehension tests accurately. Thiede et al. (2012) found similar effects among 7th and 8th graders, with test expectancy shaped by their school curricula.

So the second route works by changing the purpose of reading: elaborate encoding tasks such as mapping or self-explaining, and even simply telling students what kind of learning the test will assess. The authors see these effects as robust, found with typical and at-risk college students and with younger students. Step 6 asks the question that makes all this matter: does better monitoring actually lead to better learning?

⚠ The trap✗ Giving students a ready-made concept map of the text improves their judgments of comprehension as much as making one.✓ In Redford et al. (2012), 7th graders who examined already-constructed maps were no more accurate than a control group; the act of generating the map appears to be crucial.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the test-expectancy study of Thiede, Griffin and Wiley (2011) and what its two results show about monitoring.

Model answer: College students first read three practice texts followed by either memory tests of facts or inference tests of comprehension, building an expectation of one kind of test. They then read new texts and predicted their performance. Metamemory accuracy (predicting fact tests) was greater for the memory-expectancy group, and metacomprehension accuracy (predicting inference tests) was greater for the inference-expectancy group. So what students expect to be tested shapes how they read and monitor, and students focused on deep comprehension can accurately predict comprehension tests.

STEP6
Does it pay? · from accurate monitoring to better comprehension

Better judgments, better restudy choices, better understanding

❓ This step answers: How do students use their monitoring to decide what to restudy, and does better monitoring really improve comprehension?
🔗 Build the chain: place each finding on the link it supports, from accurate judgment to better comprehension.

Some words first. To allocate study time is to decide how much time goes to which material. A discrepancy is a gap, here between how well you have learned something and how well you want to know it. To restudy is to study again. In the picture: a chain of three links, from accurate judgment to good restudy choices to better comprehension. Each finding goes on the link it supports.

Many models describe learning as the interplay of monitoring and regulation, and in reading, accurate monitoring is crucial for choosing the right material to study again. Much of the research has therefore focused on improving accuracy; far less has asked how students use their monitoring to regulate study (Metcalfe, 2009).

Several models describe how students regulate their study (reviewed by Dunlosky & Ariel, 2011). The discrepancy-reduction model (for example Dunlosky & Hertzog, 1997) says that students set a desired level of learning, monitor how well they have learned the material, and keep studying until the gap between the current and the desired state is zero. Because the gap is larger for harder material, the model predicts that harder material will be chosen for restudy more often. Much of the data on how students divide their study time fits this prediction (Son & Kornell, 2008), and allocating time by this rule appears to make study time most effective and lead to more learning (Tullis & Benjamin, 2011).

There is some evidence that students find it hard to turn monitoring into study behaviour (Sussan & Son, 2014), but overall students seem fairly good at using their monitoring to choose less learned material. In de Bruin et al. (2011), pupils in grades 4, 6 and 7 all picked the texts they knew less well for restudy; Anderson and Thiede (2008) found the same for at-risk readers; and children as young as Grade 1 gave more study time to harder items (Dufresne & Kobasigawa, 1989). So regulatory skills appear to be in place early, while monitoring needs help. That suggests that improving accuracy will lead to more effective regulation and, in turn, better comprehension.

Thiede et al. (2003) gave the first empirical evidence that accuracy affects overall comprehension. The delayed-keyword group judged more accurately than the other groups; this led to more effective regulation, because they applied a discrepancy-reduction rule and chose to reread their less learned texts; and after rereading, their overall comprehension was significantly better than that of the other groups. Thiede et al. (2012) showed that better accuracy helped 7th and 8th graders choose better texts to restudy, which in turn produced better comprehension (see also Rawson et al., 2011). The authors call this strong support for the importance of metacognitive monitoring: improving accuracy makes self-regulated learning more effective and improves learning, though work remains to be done. If such techniques became widespread, they add, they could improve educational outcomes.

So the chain holds: judge accurately, choose the right texts to reread, understand more. And the weak link is the first one, not the second. That is exactly what the chapter's advice to teachers in step 7 builds on.

⚠ The trap✗ Young readers need training mainly in choosing what to restudy.✓ Regulation appears to be in place early: pupils in grades 4, 6 and 7 picked their less learned texts for restudy, and Grade 1 children gave harder items more time. What needs improving is the accuracy of monitoring.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the discrepancy-reduction model and the prediction it makes about which material students restudy.

Model answer: Students set a desired level of learning, monitor how well material has been learned, and continue studying until the discrepancy between the current and desired state reaches zero. Because the discrepancy is larger for material that is harder to learn, the model predicts that harder, less learned material will be selected for restudy more often than easier material. Much research on study-time allocation is consistent with this, and allocating study by this rule appears to make study time most effective.

STEP7
Future research, implications for teachers, and the chapter in one table

Pictures, screens, and what teachers can do tomorrow

❓ This step answers: Which questions are still open, and what should teachers do to support self-regulated reading?
🧑‍🏫 Advise the teacher: seven classroom plans. Keep the ones the chapter supports, drop the ones it argues against.

Some words first. An uninformative picture decorates a text without explaining anything. Seductive details are interesting but irrelevant additions that pull attention away from what matters. Calibration is the match between how well you think you did and how well you did. Self-paced means the learner decides how long to study. In the picture: two columns, "keep" and "drop", for six classroom plans.

Pictures in texts. Most studies used plain written text, but real texts contain pictures, photographs, diagrams and drawings, and these influence monitoring. Serra and Dunlosky (2010) found that adding diagrams led to higher judgments of comprehension and higher test performance. But follow-up research found that uninformative pictures also raised judgments of comprehension without improving test scores. Ackerman and Leiser (2014) showed that this harmful effect on monitoring was limited to lower-performing students; higher performers even profited from the uninformative pictures, perhaps because they worked harder to make sense of them. More research is needed on why illustrations act as what Jaeger and Wiley (2014) called "seductive details" and lead to poor monitoring, especially for low performers.

Paper or screen. Learning increasingly happens from screens. Ackerman and Goldsmith (2011) found better calibration of comprehension judgments when students read on paper than on screen, without a difference in comprehension scores. When students could study at their own pace, the better calibration on paper turned into better regulation and higher test scores. Because real study is usually self-paced, the authors conclude that students need extra support to avoid miscalibration and poor regulation when studying texts on screen.

What teachers should do. Self-regulated reading needs accurate monitoring and effective regulation. The good news: even young students give more study time to harder material, which is effective regulation (Tullis & Benjamin, 2011), so teachers need not intervene to improve regulation. The bad news: inaccurate monitoring is everywhere; even college students struggle to tell well learned from less learned texts. So teachers have an important role in improving monitoring. Following the cue-utilization framework, the key is to focus readers on cues that predict test performance. Teachers should be clear about what they want students to learn, construct tests that assess that learning, describe the nature of the tests explicitly, and help students identify predictive cues. Perhaps the best way, the authors suggest, is to stress deep comprehension rather than extracting facts, by building tests that assess it: students adjust their reading and monitoring to the demands of the test, and when they know a test will ask them to connect ideas across texts, they read to make those connections and monitor that level of learning accurately.

More tools for teachers. Teachers can also use tasks that build an elaborate situation model, such as teaching students to make concept maps while reading, and show how the maps relate to comprehension tests, which may help students find and use valid cues. They should practise good self-regulation themselves and in their students, promote self-testing (retrieval attempts) to improve monitoring, give students chances to write summaries or create cause-and-effect diagrams, and show how these map onto comprehension tests. If students use those cues, the authors expect their accuracy to improve.

💭 Think of it this way

A weather forecaster. Absolute accuracy is whether she gets the temperature right; relative accuracy is whether she can say which day will be the warmest. A forecaster who looks only at the colour of the sky (a surface cue) is often wrong; one who reads the pressure maps (the situation model) predicts well. And only a good forecast tells you on which day to take your umbrella, which is what regulation does with monitoring.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why does the chapter say teachers need not intervene to improve regulation, but should intervene to improve monitoring?

Because even young students already allocate more study time to harder material and choose less learned texts for restudy, which is effective regulation (for example de Bruin et al., 2011; Dufresne & Kobasigawa, 1989). Monitoring, by contrast, is inaccurate everywhere, even among college students, and accurate monitoring is what makes the regulation effective.

2. Name the two routes to better metacomprehension accuracy and one technique for each.

Route 1: retrieving information before judging comprehension, after a delay, for example writing delayed summaries, five keywords or completing a cause-and-effect diagram. Route 2: encoding the text so that the situation model is built during reading, for example generating a concept map, self-explaining, or building an expectation of inference tests.

3. What did Thiede et al. (2003) show that earlier studies had not?

The first empirical evidence that metacomprehension accuracy affects overall comprehension: the delayed-keyword group judged more accurately, used a discrepancy-reduction rule to restudy less learned texts, and after rereading had significantly better overall comprehension than the other groups.
