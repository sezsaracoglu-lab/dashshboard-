Theme 3 · Self-regulation and metacognition · Prepared for Sezen

How does a learner take charge of her own learning, and how can a teacher help?

Two students are taught the same strategy. One makes it her own: she plans, checks how it goes and adjusts. The other drops it as soon as nobody asks for it. This theme explains the difference, and what teachers can do about it.

Self-regulation and learning

Schunk, D. H., & Zimmerman, B. J. (2012). Self-regulation and learning. Chapter 3 in I. B. Weiner, W. M. Reynolds & G. E. Miller (Eds.), Handbook of psychology: Educational psychology (2nd ed., pp. 45–68). Wiley.

STEP1
What self-regulated learning is, and what this chapter does

The learner steers: goals, checking, adjusting, and the will to do it

❓ This step answers: How is self-regulated learning defined, which features do all theories share, and how is the chapter built?
🧭 Watch Noor study: decide whether each move is self-regulated, and which shared feature it shows.
Authors
Dale H. Schunk and Barry J. Zimmerman, two of the central researchers of self-regulated learning; many of the studies in this chapter are their own
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 3 of the Handbook of Psychology, volume Educational Psychology (2nd ed., 2012), pp. 45–68
Built for
Overview: five theories of self-regulation, the research on self-regulatory processes, two interventions, and future directions
Main message
Self-regulation can be explained from several theories, it can be studied, and it can be taught

Think of learning to drive: at first the instructor decides everything; later you set the route, watch the road and correct yourself. Self-regulated learning is the learning version: learning that comes from your own thoughts and actions, systematically aimed at your own goals (Zimmerman, 2000). The chapter's examples mix thinking, motivation and people: attending to instruction, linking new material to what you know, believing you can learn, and building good working relations and places.

All theories agree on three things (Zimmerman, 2001). Active: the learner is involved in her behaviour, her metacognition (steering her own thinking) and her motivation. Cyclical: it runs in loops: set a goal, monitor progress, respond, for example by working harder or changing strategy. Why: the theories also ask why people choose to regulate, so managing motivation and emotions belongs to it. In plain words: you drive, you check the map, and you keep yourself going.

The chapter has four parts: five theories (operant, information processing, developmental, social constructivist, social cognitive), research on which processes students use, two programmes that teach self-regulation, and open questions. The topic grew out of research on behavioural self-control (Karoly & Kanfer, 1982). Ideas from Theme 2 (self-efficacy, attributions, goals, mindsets) return here as tools the learner uses on herself.

⚠ The trap✗ A self-regulated learner learns alone and never needs help from others.✓ Self-regulation does not mean social independence. It means increasing internalization and self-direction; seeking help from teachers, peers or parents is itself one of the self-regulatory processes students use.
📝 Exam-style questionName the three features that theories of self-regulation share, and give an example of each.

Model answer: (1) Learners are behaviourally, metacognitively and motivationally active in their own learning, for example checking whether they understand a text. (2) Self-regulation is cyclical, made of feedback loops: learners set a goal, monitor progress and respond, for example by working harder or switching strategy. (3) The theories address why people choose to self-regulate: besides knowing what and how, learners must regulate their motivation and emotions, for example keeping going after a disappointing grade.

The map · five theories of self-regulation

Five lenses on the same learner

❓ This step answers: Which five theories explain self-regulation, and what does each one look at?
🗺️ Sort the situations: six learners, five lenses. For each situation, tap the lens that explains it best.

Some words first. A theory is an organized explanation that research can test. Operant behaviour, in Skinner's theory, is voluntary behaviour shaped by its consequences. A cognitive theory looks at what happens in the mind: attention, memory, thinking. Metacognition is knowledge about, and control over, your own thinking. A cultural tool is something a culture gives you to think with, such as language or symbols. In the picture: five lenses, each with the question it asks. Six learners appear one by one. You tap the lens that explains the situation best; only then does the lens show its name.

Five photographers can shoot the same student from five angles. Each photo is true, but each shows something else. The chapter explains self-regulation through five theories: operant, information processing, developmental, social constructivist and social cognitive. Operant theory comes first, because much early research came from it and because the other four, which all stress the mind, were built on its context.

Table 3.1 in one line each. Operant: behaviours you can arrange (self-monitoring, self-instruction, self-reinforcement). Information processing: what the mind does (attention, coding, storage, retrieval, metacognition) and learning strategies. Developmental: what grows with age, including private speech. Social constructivist: theories learners build about themselves, tasks and strategies; cultural tools such as languages and symbols; the zone of proximal development. Social cognitive: person, behaviour and environment influencing each other, in a cycle of forethought, performance control and self-reflection.

The five are not separate boxes. Private speech appears in developmental theory (Luria) and in Vygotsky's social constructivist theory. Metacognition appears in information processing and returns in developmental theory. Our tip: learn what each theory puts in the foreground, because an exam question about a situation tests exactly that.

Each lens suggests a different kind of help (our summary). Operant: arrange cues, records and rewards. Information processing: teach strategies and comprehension monitoring. Developmental: fit demands to what the child can control. Social constructivist: help in the zone and watch the theories she builds about herself. Social cognitive: models, goals, feedback and the cycle. The next steps take the theories one by one.

📝 Exam-style questionA teacher has a pupil talk himself through each step of a sum out loud. Which two theories in the chapter give private speech a central place, and how do they differ?

Model answer: Developmental theory and social constructivist theory. Developmental theory (for example Luria) describes stages in which speech gradually comes to start, direct and stop a child's actions, and studies why children fail to use helpful verbalizations (production, mediational and continued-use deficiencies). Vygotsky's social constructivist theory sees language, a cultural tool, as central, and private speech as what develops thought: children internalize word meanings from social interaction and use them to direct their own behaviour, and overt speech gradually goes inward.

STEP3
Theory 1 · operant theory

Cue it, record it, say it, reward it

❓ This step answers: How does operant theory explain self-regulation, what are its three key processes, and what is its limitation?
🎯 Build Brad's study plan (the chapter's own example), then pick the right way to self-monitor in four new cases.

Some words first. Operant behaviour is voluntary behaviour. A discriminative stimulus is a cue in the situation to which people may respond: it signals that a behaviour can now be followed by a consequence. A reinforcer is a consequence that makes a behaviour more likely in future; a punishment makes it less likely. A contingency is a rule that links a behaviour to a consequence. In the picture: on the left, Brad's study plan with three empty slots; on the right, a monitoring notebook. You fill in the plan, then choose how four other students should keep track of their own behaviour.

Think of training a puppy: cues before, a treat after. Operant theory (Skinner, 1953) applies that logic to yourself. Behaviour happens in the presence of cues (discriminative stimuli) and depends on its consequences: praise that makes a pupil answer more often is a reinforcer; criticism that makes her answer less is a punishment. Self-regulation means choosing among actions, usually by giving up an immediate reward for a later, usually greater one (Mace, Belfiore & Shea, 1989).

The chapter's Brad studies too little. With his counsellor he sets a fixed time and place (6 to 9 p.m. in his room, two short breaks), puts his phone away, and earns a point per successful night; 10 points buy a night off. The routine in four moves (Mace et al., 2001): choose the behaviour, set up cues, compare performance with a standard, reinforce. Three key processes follow: self-monitoring, self-instruction and self-reinforcement.

Self-monitoring is deliberately watching one aspect of your own behaviour, usually with a record. Six methods: narrations, frequency counts, duration measures, time-sampling, behaviour ratings, and lasting records such as worksheets completed. Two criteria (Bandura, 1986): regularity (record continually) and proximity (record right away). In plain words: a step counter works because it counts every day, straight away. Self-monitoring alone can already improve behaviour (Belfiore & Hornyak, 1998).

Self-instruction means setting up cues that trigger the right action: a note to yourself before bed, or statements you say while working. Schunk and Rice (1987): weak readers said a six-step strategy aloud before reading each passage ("What do I have to do?" … "Reread if I don't know the answer"). Strategy instruction like this improved comprehension and achievement beliefs. In plain words: talking yourself through the steps keeps you on task.

Self-reinforcement is rewarding yourself after a response, so the response becomes more likely. But it is unclear whether rewarding yourself works better than being rewarded by a teacher: in class, students may stay on task because of the teacher's control, so the effect is hard to isolate (Brigham, 1982). The reward may matter more than who gives it. Explicit reinforcement may matter more while skills are still being learned.

Behavioural methods help in the short term, for example more time on task. But by looking only at behaviour, operant theory ignores the cognitive and motivational side: beliefs and emotions. Long-term goals, such as finishing a degree, need a richer view. In plain words: the puppy logic explains what you do, not what you think while you do it.

Operant theory gives teachers practical tools: cues, honest records, self-talk and self-rewards. Self-monitoring returns later as self-observation in social cognitive theory. What the theory cannot explain is what goes on in the student's head, and that is where the next theory starts.

⚠ The trap✗ Research shows that rewards students give themselves work better than rewards from the teacher.✓ It is unclear whether self-reinforcement is more effective than external reinforcement; the reinforcement may be more important than who gives it, and external reinforcement may matter more while skills are being learned.
📝 Exam-style questionA student records once a week, from memory, how much he studied. Which two criteria for self-monitoring does he break, and why do they matter?

Model answer: Regularity and proximity (Bandura, 1986). Regularity means observing behaviour continually, such as a daily record, rather than intermittently; proximity means recording close in time to the behaviour rather than long afterwards. Non-regular and delayed recording depends on memory, which is selective, so it often gives misleading results.

STEP4
Theory 2 · information processing theory

The mind checks its own work

❓ This step answers: How does information processing theory describe self-regulation, and what are the TOTE model and Winne and Hadwin's model?
🔁 Run Jenny's loop, then Kai's assignment: say what the mind does next, then place each moment in a phase.

Some words first. Encoding means putting information into memory. Long-term memory is the large, lasting store; working memory (short-term memory) is the small store of what you are consciously handling right now. A standard is the level you compare your work with. A discrepancy is a gap between where you are and the standard. A schema or script is an organized pattern in memory for doing something. In the picture: at the top, a loop of four boxes; you tell Jenny's mind what to do next. Below, four stepping stones with plain questions; you place Kai's moments on them, and each stone then shows its official name.

Think of a thermostat: it compares the room with the setting and heats until they match. Information processing theory sees self-regulation as roughly metacognition: monitoring, directing and regulating your own thinking toward a goal. It uses three kinds of knowledge: about the task, about yourself and about strategies, plus the know-how to check your learning and decide when to change approach or whether you are ready for a test.

The TOTE model (Miller, Galanter & Pribram, 1960): Test (compare now with the standard), Operate (change something if they differ), Test again, Exit when they match. The chapter's Jenny reads history and stops to summarize. Her summary falls short of her standard ("accurate, all topics"), so she finds the problem, the second paragraph, and rereads it. Then she reads on.

Winne and Hadwin (1998, 2008) describe four phases: (1) define the task, (2) set goals and plans, (3) use study tactics, and optional (4) adapt, which is skipped when the plan works. To define the task, learners use task conditions (from outside, such as the teacher's instructions) and cognitive conditions (from memory, such as earlier results and how competent they feel). In plain words: work out what is asked, plan, do it, and change course only if it is not working.

Inside each phase, the mind does five things, SMART: searching memory for what you know, monitoring (comparing what you found with what you want), assembling (linking new to old), rehearsing (repeating so it is not lost) and translating (changing the format, for example from images to words).

Every task has five slots, COPES, which are what a student "copes" with: conditions (beliefs, motivation, prior knowledge), operations (strategies), products (what comes out), evaluations (does it meet the standard?) and standards (the qualities it should have). The verdict becomes a new condition for the next round. Why it matters: the model was built with real study tasks and includes motivation.

Information processing puts a small inspector inside the learner: compare, find the gap, fix it, compare again. The inspector needs tools, and those tools are learning strategies (second tab).

⚠ The trap✗ Winne and Hadwin's model has four phases that every learner always goes through.✓ Three phases are necessary (definition of the task, goals and plans, studying tactics); the fourth, adaptations, is optional: it is not needed if the original plan is successful.
📝 Exam-style questionIn Winne and Hadwin's model, a student defines a task using two sources of information. Name and explain both, with an example.

Model answer: Task conditions: information about the task that learners interpret from the external environment, for example the teacher's directions for an assignment. Cognitive conditions: information learners retrieve from long-term memory, for example how they did on prior work and motivational variables such as perceived competence and attributions.

❓ This step answers: Which learning strategies does the chapter describe, when do they work, and how does a learner monitor her comprehension?
🧰 Fill the toolbox: nine students use a study method. Tap the drawer it belongs in, then judge two claims.

Some words first. A learning strategy is a plan for learning something well. Rote learning means learning by repetition, without linking to meaning. A mnemonic is a memory aid; an acronym is a word made from first letters. Verbatim means word for word. Comprehension means understanding. In the picture: a toolbox with four drawers, each with a plain description. Students use a study method one by one; you tap the drawer it belongs in, and the drawer shows its official name.

Learning strategies are mental plans for doing a task well (Weinstein & Mayer, 1986): selecting, organizing, rehearsing and linking information, but also handling test anxiety or building confidence. They are part of self-regulation because they give learners control over their own processing. Think of them as a toolbox with four drawers.

Rehearsal means repeating, underlining or summarizing. Repetition works for rote tasks, such as capitals or song lyrics, but it links nothing to what you know, so it is hard to retrieve later. Underlining and summarizing help only when you are selective: mark or keep only the main ideas. In plain words: highlighting the whole page is the same as highlighting nothing.

Elaboration adds meaning: mental images, mnemonics (HOMES for the five Great Lakes), asking yourself questions, and notes in your own words. Copying word for word is rehearsal, not elaboration. Organization builds structure: outlining, and mapping the main ideas and how they connect. Mapping improves retention (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006).

Comprehension monitoring checks whether it worked. Four moves: self-questioning (who, what, why, how), rereading when you cannot answer, checking consistency (do the parts fit?) and paraphrasing (if you can say it in your own words, no need to reread). Greene and Azevedo (2009): in a hypermedia science environment, monitoring, especially self-questioning, clearly improved students' understanding.

Rehearsal holds information, elaboration adds meaning, organization builds structure, and monitoring checks the result. Monitoring is what makes the toolbox self-regulated: the learner decides when to reach for another tool. Reading 2 is entirely about that drawer.

⚠ The trap✗ Copying the important sentences of the textbook into your notes is elaboration.✓ Copying counts as rehearsal: it may help recall but adds no meaning. Elaborative notes put the most important ideas in your own words and connect them with other information.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy is rote rehearsal a weak strategy for complex learning, according to the chapter, and what should rehearsal involve instead?

Model answer: Rote repetition does not link information with what the learner already knows and does not organize it, so long-term memory does not store it in a meaningful way and retrieval after some time can be difficult. For complex learning, rehearsal must involve more than repeating, for example judicious underlining of the points most relevant to the learning goals, or short summaries in your own words that force you to identify the main ideas.

Theories 3 and 4 · developmental and social constructivist theory

Growing into self-regulation, and building theories about yourself

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop in children, what role does private speech play, and why do children sometimes not use a strategy that would help them?

How does a baby who controls almost nothing become a child who can steer herself, and why do children sometimes not use a strategy that would help them?Why this matters. Operant and information processing theory describe how self-regulation works. Developmental theory asks a different question: how does it grow? ⭐ Self-regulation is not a switch you turn on. It grows: as children develop, their thinking changes in ways that give them more and more control over their thoughts, feelings and actions.That is how developmental theorists define it (Paris, Byrnes & Paris, 2001). What does "control" mean in practice? Kopp (1982) spells it out: starting and stopping an action, making speech or movement more or less frequent or intense, waiting before acting on a goal, and behaving in ways others approve of. Our example: a toddler sees a biscuit and grabs it; a four-year-old sees the same biscuit and waits until after dinner. Same wish, more control.So this means: what you can ask of a learner depends on how far her control has grown. Researchers in this tradition study two things: how control changes with age, and the key role of private speech, talking to yourself.→ Next: when does that control first appear?

⭐ The first voluntary control appears at 9 to 12 months, when the baby does what a caregiver asks. From there control grows in phases and moves from outside the child (a parent says "stop") to inside (she stops herself), reaching self-regulation from about 36 months.Kopp (1982) describes the phases. Read them as a ladder where each phase adds a new source of control: first the body, then reactions to the world, then a relationship, then rules, then language, then thought.0–3mNeurophysiological modulation. The baby controls only her arousal and simple acts such as reaching; maturation and parents' routines, such as feeding, shape her behaviour.3–9mSensorimotor modulation. Her behaviour changes in response to what happens around her.9–12mCompliance. The first voluntary control: doing what a caregiver asks. It depends on how receptive she is and on the quality of the mother-child relationship.12–18mImpulse control (second year). She notices what a situation demands and can start, continue and stop actions; she starts to act on purpose, towards goals. The second year is the critical shift from outside to inside control (Kochanska et al., 1998): parents discipline more, and her compliance becomes linked to internalizing rules, taking them in as her own.24–36mSelf-control (third year). She reacts more to adults' commands, and growing language lets words direct her (the "directive function of speech"); taking in adults' guidance becomes common.36m+Self-regulation (fourth year). She adopts rules that guide her, takes in even more of others' guidance, thinking starts to steer her behaviour, and she adapts when demands change.So this means: self-regulation is the end of a long path from outside control to inside control. Keep this thread, "outside → inside"; it returns with Vygotsky in the next tab and in reading 3.👀 In the picture: place the four missing phases on the timeline.→ Next: by about three, the basics of control are there. What keeps growing after that?

⭐ By early childhood the basic thinking processes are in place. What grows next is how well and how quickly children use them.Developmental research now looks mostly through an information processing lens: how children take in, store and retrieve information (Samuelson & Smith, 2000). Attention, storing, retrieving and metacognition improve with age, and so does their speed (Kail & Ferrer, 2007). Young children find it hard to keep their attention on one thing, to attend to what matters and ignore the rest, and to switch quickly between activities. As they learn to control attention, their working memory (what you can hold in mind right now) improves. A simple test is the digit span: a researcher reads digits at one per second and the child repeats them. An average 5-year-old repeats four; by 12 it is six or seven (Meece, 2002). Behind this, capacity and strategies work together: as capacity grows, better strategies can be used (Matlin, 2009). Rehearsal (repeating) appears early (Flavell, 1999); organizing and linking information improve with age and can be taught; older children search their memory longer before giving up.So this means: older children don't just have more room in their heads; they use what they have better.👀 In the picture: predict the memory span.→ Next: what happens when a skill starts to run by itself?

⭐ When a skill becomes automatic, it stops costing attention, so the child's effort is free for the harder part of the task.This is automaticity: a process starts to run by itself, without effort. Less effort is then needed in the early stages of processing: once a child decodes words automatically, her effort is free for understanding the text; poor readers spend so much effort decoding that their comprehension suffers. Our example: a new driver can't chat while changing gears; an experienced one can.So this means: young learners have limited attention to spend, so teachers warn them what to pay attention to, give outlines and study guides beforehand, and use prompts, questions and feedback to keep them focused (Meece, 2002).→ Next: the change that matters most for self-regulation, thinking about your own thinking.

⭐ Metacognition, knowing and steering your own thinking, expands greatly between ages 5 and 10.Metacognition is the "inner coach" of self-regulation, so this growth matters most (Flavell, 1999). Children learn methods for monitoring (checking how well they understand), asking themselves questions about what they read, and summarizing (Dinsmore et al., 2008). They learn which strategy fits which task, and they come to believe that using a strategy improves performance. They do switch to a new strategy, slowly, once it gives more accurate answers again and again (Siegler & Svetina, 2006). Alexander, Carr and Schwanenflugel (1995) found steady improvement with age in metacognitive knowledge and in monitoring and regulating strategy use. In plain words: the inner coach gets better year by year, gradually.So this means: a teacher can support self-monitoring from outside with self-recording, such as diaries and checklists of the task's steps (Zimmerman, Bonner & Kovach, 1996), for example: read the passage, find the main characters, decide on the main action.→ Next: how does a child's own voice become that inner coach?

⭐ Language. Children learn to steer themselves by talking to themselves: private speech.Private speech is speech that steers your own behaviour rather than communicating with someone (Fuson, 1979), and cognitive developmental theory links it strongly to the growth of self-regulation (Berk, 1986). The idea goes back to Pavlov (1927), who separated a first, perceptual signal system from a second, language-based one. He thought language and thought explain why humans are conditioned differently from animals: we don't simply react to a stimulus, we interpret it with words and experience. He never studied the second system himself; later research supported him. Luria (1961) traced how words take over a child's actions in three stages. Ages 1½ to 2½: other people's words direct the child. Ages 3 to 4: her own words can start an action but cannot reliably stop it. Ages 4½ to 5½: her private speech can start, direct and stop what she does. Luria believed this inner speech steers behaviour through neurophysiological (brain) mechanisms. Our example: a child doing a puzzle says "corners first… no, not that one", and her hand follows her words.So this means: the voice that guides the child moves from her parents to her own head. It is the same outside-to-inside thread as Kopp's phases, now with words as the tool. Vygotsky's theory builds this second signal system in; you meet it in the next tab.→ Next: if self-talk helps, why don't children always use it?

⭐ Three different problems: the child doesn't say it, says it but it doesn't steer her, or stops saying it once nobody asks her to.Production deficiency: she doesn't produce task-relevant words (a rule, a strategy, what to remember) when they would help. Mediational deficiency: she says them, but they don't change what she does next (Fuson, 1979). Young children often talk without it steering them; later their words can steer them, but they don't always talk at the right moment. This neat order fits simple self-talk, such as repeating a list; with complex self-talk both problems can occur together. Continued-use deficiency: trained children often stop talking themselves through a task once they are no longer asked to (Schunk, 1982). Our examples: one child stays silent during a memory task (production); another keeps saying "carry the one" but never carries (mediational); a third used her checklist in training and dropped it the week after (continued use).So this means: "she doesn't use the strategy" has three different causes, and each needs a different fix.👀 In the picture: diagnose the three children.→ Next: how a teacher makes a trained strategy last.

⭐ Find out why the child stopped, then fix that cause: too little practice, a strategy stuck to one task, or no belief that it helps.The chapter names three causes of a continued-use deficiency, each with its remedy. 1. She doesn't understand the strategy well enough, after too little instruction and practice → repeated instruction and practice, with spaced review sessions. 2. She ties it to the training task and doesn't see how to use it elsewhere → train on several tasks, and teach explicitly how to adapt the strategy. 3. She doesn't believe it improves her performance; she may think effort or available time matter more (Fabricius & Hagen, 1984) → give strategy value information (Baker & Brown, 1984; Schunk & Rice, 1987): tell her the strategy will help, that it helped other students, and give feedback that links her strategy use to her progress. Research shows that strategy value information improves performance, continued use and transfer to other tasks (Lodico et al., 1983; Paris, Newman & McVey, 1982).So this means: teaching a strategy is not enough; students also need to know why and when it works. Cause and remedy pairs are a classic exam question for this step.→ Last card: what you can now answer.

Theory 5 · social cognitive theory

Person, behaviour and setting, in a cycle that becomes your own

❓ This step answers: How does social cognitive theory see self-regulation, what are reciprocal interactions, and what happens when learners judge and react to their own progress?
🔺 Follow the arrows: five classroom moments. For each, say which way the influence runs; then decide what choice and self-judgment do.

Some words first. Reciprocal means going both ways. Self-efficacy (Theme 2) is the belief that you can learn or perform something at a given level. A standard is the level you hold your work against. In the picture: a triangle of person (your thoughts and beliefs), behaviour (what you do) and environment (the people and setting around you), with arrows both ways along each side. For each moment you pick the direction of the influence and the arrow lights up. On the right, the six areas in which a learner can make choices.

In social cognitive theory, self-regulation lives where learners have choices. Zimmerman (1998) names six areas: motives, methods, time, outcomes, physical environment and social environment. If a teacher fixes all six, students can still learn, but the control lies outside them. In plain words: no choice, no self-regulation.

Bandura's (1986) reciprocal interactions (Figure 3.1): person, behaviour and environment influence each other. Self-efficacy, your belief that you can learn or perform at a given level, shows it. Person ↔ behaviour: self-efficacy steers effort, and progress raises self-efficacy. Person ↔ environment: low expectations of a pupil with a learning disability can lower her self-efficacy; "I know you can do this" can raise it. Behaviour ↔ environment: pupils look where the teacher points; wrong answers make the teacher reteach.

Three classic processes (Bandura, 1986): self-observation (much like self-monitoring), self-judgment (comparing your performance with your goal) and self-reaction (how you respond). Seeing progress raises self-efficacy and motivation. Even a poor result need not lower motivation, if you believe you can improve. Higher goals bring more effort, as long as you believe you can reach them (Locke & Latham, 2002).

The social cognitive learner is never a closed system: beliefs steer behaviour, behaviour changes the setting, and the setting talks back to beliefs. The next tab orders these processes in time.

⚠ The trap✗ A negative self-evaluation always lowers a student's motivation.✓ Negative evaluations need not decrease motivation if students believe they are capable of improving, for example with more effort or another strategy. Motivation does not increase if they believe they lack the ability to succeed.

Model answer: Personal → behaviour: self-efficacy influences task choice, effort, persistence and achievement. Behaviour → personal: noticing progress on a term paper raises self-efficacy. Environment → personal: teachers who expect less of students with learning disabilities give feedback that can lower their self-efficacy (and persuasive statements can raise it). Behaviour → environment: incorrect answers lead the teacher to reteach. Environment → behaviour: students look at a visual the teacher points to.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-efficacy only plays a role after the task, when students judge how they did.✓ In Zimmerman's model, a sense of self-efficacy for learning is part of the forethought phase, before the task, together with goal setting and strategic planning.

Nobody learns to cook alone from scratch: you watch, copy, cook alone with the recipe, then improvise. Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) proposed that self-regulation develops first from social sources and then from self-sources, in four levels.

Observation: watching models shows the main features of a strategy. Emulation: practising the general pattern with guidance and feedback, imitating the style rather than copying. Even advanced learners keep using teachers, coaches and peers to refine their skills (Zimmerman, 2000). Self-regulation does not mean social independence; it means taking more and more control of your own learning. Learning alone is possible, but it misses what the social environment offers.

The levels give a teaching plan: show it, let students try it with feedback, let them do it alone, then let them adapt it. Steps 8 and 9 show experiments and a programme built on this.

⚠ The trap✗ At the emulation level, the learner copies the model's actions and words exactly.✓ Emulation means the performance approximates the general form of the model's: learners imitate general patterns or styles (the type of question), not the model's exact words.
📝 Exam-style questionWhat distinguishes the self-control level from the self-regulation level in Schunk and Zimmerman's progression?

Model answer: At the self-control level, learners use a strategy independently on transfer tasks, but their use still follows representational standards of the modeled performance and self-reinforcement. ❓ This step answers: Which properties of goals improve self-regulated learning, according to the experiments in the chapter?
🎯 Run the goal lab: four experiments. Before each result appears, predict which group came out on top.

Some words first. A proximal goal is near in time (finish this set today); a distant goal is far away (finish everything by the end). A process goal (learning goal) is about learning how to do something; a product goal (performance goal) is about the result, such as a score. A condition is one of the groups in an experiment. In the picture: four small lab benches, one per experiment. You predict which condition did best; then the bars show the order.

Goal setting is the core of forethought. But which goals work best? Researchers varied the properties of goals (Locke & Latham, 2002): how near they are, how hard, and whether they aim at learning a process or at a result.

Bandura and Schunk (1981): children practising subtraction aimed at one set per session (near goal), all sets by the end (distant goal), or simply working hard (general goal). Found: near goals gave the best practice, self-efficacy and achievement; the distant goal was no better than the general one. Schunk (1983a): difficult but reachable goals raised motivation and achievement; being told "you can do this" raised self-efficacy. In plain words: close and challenging beats far away and vague.

Schunk and Swartz (1993): children learning to write paragraphs had a process goal (learn the strategy), a product goal (write paragraphs) or a general goal (do your best); half the process-goal children also got progress feedback. Found: process goal plus feedback was most effective on self-efficacy, writing, self-evaluated progress and strategy use; gains held after 6 weeks and transferred to new paragraph types. In plain words: aim at learning the method and see your progress.

Zimmerman and Kitsantas (1996, 1997): girls learning darts focused on the throwing steps (process goal) or on their score (product goal). Process goals and self-recording raised self-efficacy and performance. A shifting goal, first the steps and then the score once the steps were automatic, worked best of all. In plain words: learn the technique first, then aim for results.

Four lessons (our summary): make goals near; make them hard but reachable; start with learning the process, with progress feedback; switch to outcome goals once the process is automatic. That switch is the move from self-control to self-regulation.

⚠ The trap✗ Any goal helps: even a distant goal is better than being told to work productively.✓ In Bandura and Schunk (1981), the distant goal resulted in no benefits compared with the general goal; only the proximal goal improved self-regulated practice, self-efficacy and achievement.

STEP9
Teaching self-regulation · two programmes

Self-regulation can be taught: a writing programme and a reflection programme

❓ This step answers: What is Self-Regulated Strategy Development, how was it used to teach story writing, and what did it achieve?
✍️ Run the writing course: five classroom moments. Put each on the right stage of the programme, then check two details.

Some words first. An intervention is a programme designed to change something, tested in research. ADHD is attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder. A criterion is the level a learner must reach. Maintenance means the gains last after the programme ends; generalization means they spread to other settings. In the picture: five stepping stones with plain names. You place five classroom moments on the right stone; each stone then shows the programme's own name.

Self-regulation does not simply grow with age; it has to be taught. Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD; Harris, Graham & Mason, 2006) teaches a task strategy together with self-regulation (goals, self-monitoring, managing effort), through teacher modeling, practice with peers and independent practice, with support that fades. It has been applied to writing, reading and mathematics, with positive effects on learning, motivation and strategy use.

Reid and Lienemann (2006) used SRSD for story writing with children with ADHD. Stories had to contain seven parts: WWW, What = 2, How = 2 (who, where, when; what they want, what happens next; how it ends, how they feel). Planning followed POW: Pick my ideas, Organize my notes, Write and say more.

(1) Develop background knowledge: learn the mnemonics and find the parts in stories. (2) Discuss it: check your own stories and chart your parts. (3) Model it: the teacher writes a story aloud with self-statements ("What is my goal?", "I like that part!"), and students make their own. (4) Support it: write together with organizers. (5) Independent performance: write alone and graph your progress.

Stories grew significantly in parts and words, and the gains held 3 and 6 weeks later. SRSD in general improves strategy use, performance and motivation, and supports maintenance and transfer. Nobody measured Zimmerman's four levels, but the stages build in observation, emulation and self-control, and the results hint that many students may have reached self-regulation.

Our summary: SRSD is the five theories turned into a lesson plan: self-monitoring and self-reward (operant), strategies (information processing), fading support (Vygotsky), and models and social-to-self levels (social cognitive).

⚠ The trap✗ SRSD studies showed that students passed through all four of Zimmerman's levels of self-regulatory skill.✓ SRSD studies have not evaluated development according to the levels; the descriptions show that observation, emulation and self-control are addressed, and the results suggest many students may have reached the self-regulation level.
📝 Exam-style questionName the five stages of the SRSD writing instruction in Reid and Lienemann (2006), and explain how responsibility shifts across them.

Model answer: Develop background knowledge, Discuss it, Model it, Support it, Independent performance. The teacher first explains and models (the mnemonics, the self-statements), then teacher and students write together with scaffolds such as graphic organizers, and finally students write independently and graph their own progress. Responsibility shifts gradually from teacher to student as scaffolding is faded.

❓ This step answers: How did Zimmerman and colleagues (2011) train at-risk college students to reflect on their mathematics errors, and what were the results?
🧾 Fill in the reflection form, then predict what the experiment found.

Some words first. At-risk students are students with a high chance of failing or dropping out. Calibration (Theme 2) is the match between how well you think you will do and how well you actually do. Over-estimation bias means you think you will do better than you do. A randomized controlled design assigns participants by chance to the programme or to a control group, so that differences can be attributed to the programme. A gateway examination is an exam students must pass to move on. In the picture: the reflection form the students filled in after each quiz, and the results, which you predict.

Zimmerman, Moylan, Hudesman, White and Flugman (2011) worked with at-risk undergraduates in mathematics at an urban technological college, where only 21% finished an associate degree within 6 years. Many were over-confident: their self-efficacy was poorly calibrated, which can get in the way of learning from feedback.

A semester-long randomized controlled study, built on the reflection phase. Instructors modeled how to correct errors. After every quiz, students filled in a correction form: compare your confidence with your result, explain which strategy failed, choose a better one, and rate your confidence for a similar problem. Completing the form earned extra points. The message: errors are chances to learn, not end points.

The trained group outperformed the control group on maths exams, over-estimated itself less before and after solving problems, and passed a national gateway maths exam 25 percentage points more often. Within the trained classes, students who reflected more scored higher. Because students were randomly assigned, the effects can be read as causal, and they were large or near large. In plain words: learning from each quiz turned over-confidence into better results.

This programme strengthens the self-reflection phase: holding confidence against results and planning a better strategy turns feedback into the forethought of the next round.

⚠ The trap✗ The at-risk students' problem was low self-efficacy, so the programme tried to raise their confidence.✓ Many of them reported overly confident self-efficacy. The programme aimed at better calibration: comparing confidence with actual outcomes, which reduced over-estimation bias before and after solving problems.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy can the results of Zimmerman et al. (2011) be interpreted causally, and what were the two main outcomes for achievement and for self-efficacy?

Model answer: It was a true experiment with random assignment of at-risk students to classes, so differences can be attributed to the self-regulation training. Achievement: the trained students outperformed control students on instructor-developed exams and their pass rate on a national gateway mathematics exam rose by 25 percentage points. Self-efficacy: they showed less over-estimation bias in self-efficacy before solving problems and in self-evaluations afterwards.

What is shown, and what is still open

(2009): more and better interaction between students may help learning; the authors suggest tutorials on interacting with peers before a distance course starts.

Self-regulation is now a central topic in learning research. Each of the theories has important implications for research and practice, and the authors expect more intervention studies to show how best to teach it. In plain words: we know self-regulation matters and can be taught; the next questions are how, for whom and where.

1. A student uses a strategy perfectly in class but never at home. Which theories in the chapter help explain this, and what would they advise?

Authors
Keith W. Thiede and Anique B. H. de Bruin; many of the studies in the chapter are their own
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 8 of the Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed., 2017), pp. 124–137
Built for

Main message
Readers regulate well, but they monitor badly; better monitoring leads to better restudy choices and better comprehension

Think of a car's fuel gauge: you decide when to fill up by looking at it, and a wrong gauge leaves you stuck however well you drive. Reading works the same way. The reader monitors her understanding ("do I get this yet?") and regulates her study on that basis: she rereads, asks for help, or stops. Students who regulate their reading better understand more.

The loop only helps if the check is right (Winne & Perry, 2000). A wrong check leads to two mistakes: she stops too early, or she rereads the wrong text. How well a reader's judgments of her understanding match her actual test scores is called metacomprehension accuracy. The whole chapter is about this one thing.

Four questions: how is accuracy measured, why is it often poor, what improves it, and does better accuracy lead to better learning? It ends with open questions and advice for teachers. (Our link: this is the compare-and-fix loop of reading 1's information processing view, applied to one text.)

⚠ The trap✗ Readers who feel they understand a text usually do; the problem in reading is mainly that students do not reread.✓ The chapter's message is the opposite: inaccurate monitoring of comprehension is everywhere, even among college students, while the regulation of study (rereading what seems less learned) is already in place at a young age.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain why accurate metacognitive monitoring is crucial for self-regulated reading, using the two ways inaccurate monitoring can go wrong.

Model answer: In self-regulated reading, monitoring guides regulation: the reader rereads until her monitoring signals that the text is understood. If monitoring is inaccurate, she may direct her rereading to the wrong texts (misdirected effort) or stop reading before the text is fully understood. Accurate monitoring lets her choose the right texts to restudy, which improves comprehension.

STEP2
How metacomprehension accuracy is measured · Table 8.1

Two kinds of accuracy: how much, and which text

❓ This step answers: How do researchers measure how accurately readers judge their understanding, and what is the difference between absolute and relative accuracy?
📊 Read Table 8.1 yourself: four students predicted their scores on five texts. Judge each student's accuracy before the name appears.

Some words first. A judgment of comprehension is a reader's own rating of how well she understood a text. An inference question asks for something that is not stated literally but follows from the text. Bias is a systematic error in one direction, such as always expecting too much. A correlation is a number from −1 to +1 that shows how well two series go up and down together; an intra-individual correlation is computed within one person, across her texts. Orthogonal means independent: one can change without the other. In the picture: the chapter's Table 8.1 as bars. For each student you judge two things: did she expect too much on average, and did she put the texts in the right order?

The standard method (Glenberg & Epstein, 1985): read several short texts, rate how well you understood each, then answer an inference question about each text (the answer is not written in the text but follows from it). Accuracy is how well the ratings match the scores.

Picture two judges at a baking contest. One gives every cake 3 points too many but ranks them in exactly the right order; the other gets the average right but ranks them in reverse. Absolute accuracy: is your confidence the right size? Measured as bias (average predicted minus average actual). Relative accuracy: can you tell which texts you understood better? Measured as a correlation within one person, across her texts.

Four students predict 7, 7, 6, 5, 3. Student 1 scores exactly that: bias 0, relative +1.0. Student 2 scores 3 lower everywhere: bias +3, so over-confident (positive bias = over-confident, negative = under-confident), yet a perfect ranking (+1.0). Student 3: bias 0, but a reversed ranking (−1.0), so she would reread the wrong texts. Student 4: bias +3 and relative −1.0, poor on both.

The two are independent (orthogonal), so a factor can change one but not the other: domain knowledge affected absolute but not relative accuracy (Griffin, Jee & Wiley, 2009). From here on the chapter studies relative accuracy. Our reasoning: to choose what to reread, you only need to know which text you understood least.

⚠ The trap✗ A student whose average prediction equals her average score has accurate monitoring.✓ That is only absolute accuracy (no bias). Student 3 in Table 8.1 has no bias at all, yet her relative accuracy is −1.0: she ranks the texts in exactly the wrong order, so she would reread the wrong ones.
📝 Exam-style questionA student predicts 8, 6 and 4 on three texts and scores 5, 3 and 1. Describe her absolute and her relative accuracy.

Model answer: Her average prediction (6) is 3 points above her average score (3), so her absolute accuracy is poor: a confidence bias of +3, over-confident. But her predictions rank the texts in the same order as her scores (highest, middle, lowest), so her relative accuracy is perfect (+1.0), like Student 2 in Table 8.1. The two kinds of accuracy are independent.

STEP3
Why judgments go wrong · cue-utilization and construction-integration

You judge your understanding from clues, and many clues mislead

❓ This step answers: On what do readers base their judgments of comprehension, which cues predict a comprehension test, and why?
🔎 Sort the clues: seven readers say why they think they understood a text. Decide which clues predict a comprehension test.

Some words first. A cue is a clue you use to make a judgment. A cue is diagnostic when it actually predicts the outcome. Inferential means worked out indirectly, not seen directly. A proposition is a small unit of meaning (who did what). Salience is how much something stands out. In the picture: on the left, three levels of understanding a text, from the words on the page to a deep "situation model"; on the right, two baskets: clues that predict a comprehension test, and clues that mislead. Each reader's clue lands in the basket you choose.

You cannot look inside your own head, so you judge your understanding from clues, like judging a cake by its icing (a misleading cue) or by tasting a slice (a diagnostic one). Koriat's (1997) cue-utilization framework: judgments are inferred from cues, and they are accurate only when the cues are diagnostic, meaning they predict the test. To know which cues are diagnostic, you also need a theory of comprehension (Weaver, 1990; Rawson, Dunlosky & Thiede, 2000).

Kintsch's (1988) construction-integration model: we understand a text at three levels. The surface holds the words; the textbase holds the ideas and their links; the situation model connects the ideas to what you already know, so you can draw conclusions. Comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model (McNamara et al., 1996). So the good cue is "can I explain this?", and a poor one is "it felt easy".

Thiede et al. (2010) asked college students which cues they used; the 30 reported cues fell into five types: being able to explain, being able to recall, prior knowledge, interest, and features of the text. Found: students who judged by whether they could understand or explain the text were more accurate; those who relied on text features were less accurate. In plain words: "can I explain it?" works; "it was short and easy" misleads.

van Loon et al. (2014) measured each cue's diagnosticity (how well it predicts the test) and utilization (how strongly it drives the judgment). Found: differences in accuracy came more from utilization than from diagnosticity. In plain words: accuracy depends mostly on which cues readers lean on.

To help readers judge better, steer them to the right cue. The chapter shows two ways: retrieve before judging (step 4) and read in a way that builds the situation model (step 5).

⚠ The trap✗ Being able to recall many details of a text is the best sign that you understood it.✓ Comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model, and accuracy was highest for readers who used comprehension-based cues, their ability to understand or explain the text. Recall is a separate cue type in Thiede et al. (2010).
📝 Exam-style questionUse the cue-utilization framework and the construction-integration model to explain why readers' judgments of comprehension are often inaccurate.

Model answer: According to the cue-utilization framework, judgments are inferential: readers cannot inspect their comprehension directly, so they rely on available cues, and accuracy depends on how diagnostic those cues are of test performance. The construction-integration model says comprehension tests depend mainly on the situation model, the deepest level, where text is connected to prior knowledge. Many readers judge from cues tied to the surface or textbase, such as ease of reading or text length, which are not diagnostic of situation-model understanding, so their judgments are inaccurate.

STEP4
Improving accuracy 1 · retrieve before you judge, after a delay

Summaries, keywords and diagrams help, but only later, not right away

❓ This step answers: How does retrieving information before judging improve accuracy, and why does it work only after a delay?
⏳ Run the delay lab: predict four experiments, then explain the result with the mind's "warm" and "cold" memory.

Some words first. Retrieval is getting information back out of memory. A retrieval attempt here is a task such as writing a summary or keywords. Activation is how "switched on" information is in memory right now; it fades over time. Homogeneous means all alike; heterogeneous means varied. At-risk readers are students whose reading puts them at risk of failing. In the picture: a "warm" memory right after reading and a "cold" memory after a delay, and four small experiments whose results you predict.

The modified-feedback hypothesis (Glenberg et al., 1987): your judgment reflects how much you can retrieve at the moment you judge. So, before rating your understanding, get the text back out of your head: a short summary, five keywords, or a cause-and-effect diagram. What comes back is a fairer cue than a feeling.

Our example first: right after an album you can hum every song; later, only the ones that stuck come back. Activation theories (Fletcher, van den Broek & Arthur, 1996): right after reading, much of a text is still active in working memory, even a text you did not understand, so retrieval cues are homogeneous (all alike). After a delay only long-term memory is left: little comes back for weak texts, much for strong ones. The cues become heterogeneous (clearly different), and match what the test draws on.

Thiede and Anderson (2003): six texts. One group summarized each text right after reading it, one read all six first and then summarized, and a control group wrote nothing. Found: the delayed-summary group was the most accurate. Anderson and Thiede (2008): summaries helped both typical and at-risk readers; their cue reports, analysed by Thiede et al. (2010), showed that at-risk readers relied more often on surface cues. In plain words: summarize later, not straight away.

Thiede et al. (2003): five keywords per text, written after a delay, gave the most accurate judgments. Thiede et al. (2005): the crucial gap is between reading and writing the keywords: by then surface and textbase information has faded, so what comes back reflects the situation model. van Loon et al. (2014): completing a cause-and-effect diagram after a delay beat doing it immediately or not at all. In plain words: same rule, quicker tasks.

Test yourself before judging, but not straight away. This has worked for college students and down to grade 6, but may not work in grade 4 (de Bruin et al., 2011). Note the contrast: grade-4 pupils already pick the right texts to restudy (step 6); it is the retrieval trick that may not yet help them.

⚠ The trap✗ Writing a summary right after reading each text makes readers better judges of their understanding.✓ In Thiede and Anderson (2003) only the delayed-summary group became more accurate. Right after reading, much is still active in working memory even for poorly understood texts, so immediate retrieval gives cues that do not tell texts apart.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy did delayed keyword generation improve metacomprehension accuracy while immediate keyword generation did not? Refer to activation and to the kind of cues produced.

Model answer: According to activation theories, immediately after reading, information from the text is still highly active in working memory, even for poorly understood texts, so generating keywords may feel similar for all texts: the cues are homogeneous and do not discriminate, and they do not reflect the delayed test. After a delay, activation has decayed and only information in long-term memory is retrievable, so for poorly understood texts little information comes back and generating keywords is hard, while for well understood texts much more comes back: heterogeneous cues that differentiate the texts and match what is available at the test. Thiede et al. (2005) showed that the delay between reading and generating keywords was the crucial factor.

STEP5
Improving accuracy 2 · read in a way that builds the situation model

Map it, explain it, and know what the test will ask

❓ This step answers: Which ways of reading build the situation model, and how do concept mapping, self-explanation and test expectancy improve accuracy?
🗺️ Choose the reading task: predict five experiments on how students read, then check what they show.

Some words first. A concept map is a diagram of the ideas in a text and how they connect. To self-explain is to tell yourself what each sentence means and how it fits with the rest. Test expectancy is what kind of test you expect. Metamemory accuracy is how well you predict a test of facts; metacomprehension accuracy, how well you predict a test of understanding. In the picture: three ways of reading, each with an experiment. You predict the result; the panel then shows which group judged its understanding best.

The second route changes how you read. If reading builds the situation model, its cue ("can I explain this?") is right there when you judge yourself. Think of furniture: assembling it yourself shows which joints you understood; looking at someone else's finished piece does not.

A concept map is a drawing of a text's ideas and how they connect; mapping can help less able readers most (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006). Thiede et al. (2010, Experiment 2): at-risk readers judged more accurately when they made maps. Redford et al. (2012): 7th graders who made maps judged better, but those given ready-made maps were no better than pupils without maps. In plain words: the making is what helps.

Self-explaining (Chi, 2000) means telling yourself what each sentence means and how it fits the whole. Griffin, Wiley and Thiede (2008): college students who self-explained judged more accurately than students who read the text twice in the same time, or once. In plain words: explaining, not extra time, makes the difference.

Thiede, Griffin and Wiley (2011): after practice texts with either fact tests or understanding tests, students rated new texts. Found: expecting understanding tests raised metacomprehension accuracy; expecting fact tests raised metamemory accuracy (predicting memory for facts). Thiede et al. (2012) found the same in grades 7 and 8. In plain words: what you expect the test to ask changes how you read and judge.

Making your own map, explaining to yourself and expecting a deep test all point readers to situation-model cues. Step 6 asks whether better judging actually leads to better learning.

⚠ The trap✗ Giving students a ready-made concept map of the text improves their judgments of comprehension as much as making one.✓ In Redford et al. (2012), 7th graders who examined already-constructed maps were no more accurate than a control group; the act of generating the map appears to be crucial.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the test-expectancy study of Thiede, Griffin and Wiley (2011) and what its two results show about monitoring.

Model answer: College students first read three practice texts followed by either memory tests of facts or inference tests of comprehension, building an expectation of one kind of test. They then read new texts and predicted their performance. Metamemory accuracy (predicting fact tests) was greater for the memory-expectancy group, and metacomprehension accuracy (predicting inference tests) was greater for the inference-expectancy group. So what students expect to be tested shapes how they read and monitor, and students focused on deep comprehension can accurately predict comprehension tests.

STEP6
Does it pay? · from accurate monitoring to better comprehension

Better judgments, better restudy choices, better understanding

❓ This step answers: How do students use their monitoring to decide what to restudy, and does better monitoring really improve comprehension?
🔗 Build the chain: place each finding on the link it supports, from accurate judgment to better comprehension.

Some words first. To allocate study time is to decide how much time goes to which material. A discrepancy is a gap, here between how well you have learned something and how well you want to know it. To restudy is to study again. In the picture: a chain of three links, from accurate judgment to good restudy choices to better comprehension. Each finding goes on the link it supports.

The discrepancy-reduction model (Dunlosky & Hertzog, 1997): students keep studying until the gap between "how well I know it" and "how well I want to know it" is closed. Its prediction: harder, less-learned material is chosen for restudy more often. Much data fits this (Son & Kornell, 2008), and it appears to make study time most effective (Tullis & Benjamin, 2011).

Students are fairly good at acting on their judgments. Pupils in grades 4, 6 and 7 picked their less-learned texts for restudy (de Bruin et al., 2011), at-risk readers did too (Anderson & Thiede, 2008), and even grade-1 children gave harder items more time (Dufresne & Kobasigawa, 1989). Some still find it hard to turn monitoring into action (Sussan & Son, 2014).

Thiede et al. (2003): the delayed-keyword group judged more accurately, then chose its weaker texts to reread, and ended with better overall comprehension. It was the first evidence that accuracy affects learning. Thiede et al. (2012) found the same in grades 7 and 8 (see also Rawson et al., 2011). In plain words: judge right, reread right, understand more.

The main weak link is the judging, not the rereading. That is exactly what the chapter's advice to teachers (step 7) builds on.

⚠ The trap✗ Young readers need training mainly in choosing what to restudy.✓ Regulation appears to be in place early: pupils in grades 4, 6 and 7 picked their less learned texts for restudy, and Grade 1 children gave harder items more time. What needs improving is the accuracy of monitoring.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the discrepancy-reduction model and the prediction it makes about which material students restudy.

Model answer: Students set a desired level of learning, monitor how well material has been learned, and continue studying until the discrepancy between the current and desired state reaches zero. Because the discrepancy is larger for material that is harder to learn, the model predicts that harder, less learned material will be selected for restudy more often than easier material. Much research on study-time allocation is consistent with this, and allocating study by this rule appears to make study time most effective.

Pictures, screens, and what teachers can do tomorrow

❓ This step answers: Which questions are still open, and what should teachers do to support self-regulated reading?

Some words first. An uninformative picture decorates a text without explaining anything. Seductive details are interesting but irrelevant additions that pull attention away from what matters. Helpful diagrams raised both judgments and scores (Serra & Dunlosky, 2010). Later research found that uninformative pictures raised judgments without raising scores. Ackerman and Leiser (2014): this harm was limited to lower performers; higher performers even profited, possibly because they worked harder to make sense of them. Why such seductive details (Jaeger & Wiley, 2014) mislead is still open.

Ackerman and Goldsmith (2011): calibration (absolute accuracy) was better on paper than on screen, with the same scores. When students studied at their own pace, paper also led to better regulation and higher scores. Because real study is self-paced, students need extra support when reading on screen.

The good news: pupils already reread sensibly, so teachers need not train the rereading. The bad news: inaccurate monitoring is everywhere; even college students struggle to tell which texts they know well. So teachers should train the judging: say what students should learn, describe the test explicitly, and test deep understanding rather than facts. Students adjust how they read and monitor to the test they expect.

Teach students to make their own concept maps, promote self-testing later (keywords, summaries, cause-and-effect diagrams), and show how these relate to the test, so students learn which cues predict it. Teachers should also practise good self-regulation themselves.

STEP1
Social cognitive theory, agency and self-efficacy

People are shaped by their world, and they shape it back

❓ This step answers: How does social cognitive theory explain human functioning, and why do agency and self-efficacy sit at the heart of its view of self-regulation?
🔺 Follow Sofia's first statistics month: predict each next link, and watch the triangle build.
Authors
Ellen L. Usher and Dale H. Schunk
Type
Handbook chapter: Chapter 2 of the Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed., 2017), pp. 19–35
Built on
The theorizing of Albert Bandura and Barry Zimmerman
Main message
Self-regulation is learned, it runs in a cycle before, during and after a task, and belief in one's own capability (self-efficacy) drives it

Self-regulation is systematically organizing your thoughts, feelings and actions to reach your goals. Think of a sailor: wind and waves push the boat, her course changes what she meets next, and her skill and confidence decide how she sets the sails. Social cognitive theory says human functioning comes from three interacting influences: personal, behavioural and environmental (Bandura, 1986, 2001). They are co-determinants: each shapes the others, in both directions.

The theory sets itself apart from three older views. Behaviour is not only a product of outside rewards (behaviourism), not only driven by hidden inner drives (Freud), and not only free choice (many humanists). In plain words: you are neither a puppet of your surroundings nor completely free of them.

Human agency is the ability to steer events in your life on purpose and to choose your reactions (Bandura, 2016). It works through four capacities: generating new thoughts (choosing helpful thoughts), forethought (planning, goals, imagining the future), self-reaction (comparing with standards and adjusting) and reflective self-consciousness (examining your own actions, thoughts and feelings).

At the heart lies the self-system: how people perceive, judge and compare themselves. The most influential self-judgment is self-efficacy, your belief that you can reach what you aim for. It is a critical determinant of self-regulation: confident learners set hard goals, monitor and correct, and persist; doubters give up easily. For Bandura (2016) it is the bedrock of aspiration, motivation and achievement.

The chapter covers environments and agency, the three subfunctions, the cycle (Figure 2.1), how self-regulation develops, six components, and finally research directions and practice.

⚠ The trap✗ Social cognitive theory says behaviour is mainly caused by the environment, through models and reinforcement.✓ It says personal, behavioural and environmental factors are co-determinants whose influence runs in both directions. It explicitly rejects the behaviourist view that behaviour is simply a product of external influence and reinforcement.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain the social cognitive view of human functioning and how it differs from behaviourist and psychodynamic views.

Model answer: Social cognitive theory holds that human functioning results from the reciprocal, interacting influence of personal factors (biological, affective, cognitive), environmental factors and behaviour; all three are co-determinants. Behaviourist theories saw behaviour as a product of external influences and reinforcement; psychodynamic theories saw it as driven by hidden internal drives. Social cognitive theory adds human agency: people can intentionally shape their circumstances and their reactions, through capacities such as forethought and self-reaction.

Environments that are imposed, selected and created

❓ This step answers: How much of their environment can students shape, and why do many not use that power?

Some words first. Imposed means put on you, without your choice. Agentic means coming from your own agency, your own intentional action. To relegate a task is to hand it down to someone or something else. Aversive means unpleasant, something you want to avoid. To internalize a standard is to make it your own. Environments can be imposed, selected or created (Bandura, 1986). School is imposed. Within it, students select classes, friends and activities, and this selection may strongly shape whether they thrive or withdraw. They can also create their own learning environment with physical, social or technological resources, which takes a lot of agentic control. Our example: a city you did not choose, the neighbourhood you pick, the home you furnish.

Having agency does not mean using it. Many people find too much self-control unpleasant and leave tasks to external controls, such as deadlines and reminders (Bandura, 1986, 2016). Unless students believe their own thinking and doing can improve their situation, they may not adopt high standards as their own. Self-regulation costs energy and cognitive, motivational and emotional resources.

The environment is not just something that happens to a learner: she can choose within it and build parts of it, if she believes her actions make a difference. Next: what self-regulation is made of inside the person.

⚠ The trap✗ Because students can shape their environment, they will do so whenever it helps them.✓ The potential for agency does not mean it is used: many find too much self-control aversive and leave tasks to external controls, and unless students believe their own actions can improve their circumstances, they may not internalize high standards.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the three kinds of environment in social cognitive theory, with a school example of each.

Model answer: An imposed environment is put on the person, for example school itself, which students must attend. A selected environment is chosen within it, for example the classes, friendship groups and activities a student picks, which can strongly shape whether she thrives or withdraws. A created environment is built by the student with physical, social or technological resources, for example a study group or a self-designed study space; it requires a high degree of agentic control.

STEP3
Self-observation, self-evaluation and self-reaction

Notice it, judge it, respond to it

❓ This step answers: What are the three subfunctions of self-regulation in social cognitive theory, and why is noticing your own behaviour not enough to change it?
🔍 Watch Omar train for a race: decide which subfunction each moment shows, then explain why awareness alone did not change him.

Some words first. A subfunction is one working part of a larger process. A standard is the level you measure yourself against. Tacit means unspoken but understood. A mediating factor is something in between that decides whether one thing leads to another. Contingent means depending on a condition ("only if I finish"). In the picture: three gears that turn together: an eye (observe), a scale (evaluate) and a hand (react). Each moment lights up one gear.

Think of a smart scale: it shows your weight (you notice), compares it with your target (you judge), but nothing changes until you act (you react). Social cognitive theory names three subfunctions (Bandura, 1986): self-observation, self-evaluation and self-reaction.

Noticing your own thoughts and behaviour gives the information needed for change. It is coloured by your mood, memory and self-beliefs, and the more complex the task, the more attention and working memory it costs. But noticing is not enough: whether it leads to change depends on timing, on whether change feels controllable and valued, and on motivation. The chapter's teenager knows he games for hours, but feels no control and does not value exercise.

You judge yourself against standards: external ones, taught directly or picked up from models (a girl who sees her mother expect perfection), and your own. Consistent, explicit standards are internalized most easily; conflicting standards leave a person unsure how to act. People are not weathervanes: they can choose their standards, and personal bests become new ones. Self-set or internalized standards weigh most; without them there is little self-direction.

You respond to your own performance. One way is a tangible self-reward, such as a break or a treat after finishing, which helps most when outside pressure is absent (the author, the runner). Another is self-satisfaction: effort and persistence go up when satisfaction depends on meeting your own standards (Bandura, 1986).

The three work as a chain: notice, judge, respond. When a link is missing, for example no sense of control or no valued standard, noticing alone does not reliably lead to change. The next step shows that all three work in every phase of the cycle.

⚠ The trap✗ Making students aware of their behaviour, for example by having them track it, is enough for them to regulate it.✓ Self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation. Whether it leads to change depends on its timing, on whether change feels controllable and valued, and on motivation, like the teenager who knows he sits too much but feels no control and does not value exercise.
📝 Exam-style questionA student sees in her planner that she studied far less than planned this week, but nothing changes. Use the three subfunctions to explain why.

Model answer: She has self-observation: she noticed the gap. But self-observation alone is not reliably related to self-regulation. Change also needs self-evaluation against a standard she has internalized and values (if the planned study time is not her own valued standard, the gap means little), and a self-reaction, such as a self-set reward contingent on meeting the plan or self-satisfaction tied to her standard. She may also not feel that change is under her control. Without these links, awareness does not lead to change.

STEP4
Zimmerman's cyclical model · Figure 2.1

Before, during and after: forethought, performance, reflection

❓ This step answers: What happens in each phase of Zimmerman's cycle, and how do the three subfunctions fit into it?
🔄 Rebuild Figure 2.1: follow Emma through a biology exam and place each of her moves in the right phase.

Some words first. A phase is a stage in a sequence. Task analysis is working out what a task requires. An outcome expectation is a belief about what an action will lead to. An implicit theory of ability is a person's unspoken belief about whether ability is fixed or can grow. A causal attribution is what someone sees as the cause of a result. In the picture: Figure 2.1 as a wheel of three phases around the three subfunctions; each move fills its phase.

Figure 2.1 (p. 22) shows Zimmerman's cycle. Forethought: task analysis (planning, goal setting, visualization) and motivation and beliefs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, implicit theory of ability, stereotype awareness). Performance: monitoring and strategy use. Reflection: assessment and self-reaction. The three subfunctions sit in the centre. Think of a sports match: preparation, the match, the review that shapes the next preparation.

Before acting, skilled learners analyse the task (plan, set goals, visualize) and motivate themselves. Self-efficacy is key: unless people believe they can do a task, they are unlikely to try it. There are kinds of efficacy: the chapter's teenager judges whether she can understand a long 19th-century novel (reading self-efficacy) and whether she can make time to read it (self-efficacy for self-regulation). Goals (Locke & Latham, 2013), interest and outcome expectations also steer effort.

During the task, learners monitor their thinking, feelings, effort and the task's demands, and use strategies: note taking, help-seeking, self-talk. Athletes review recordings of their performance; marine biologists keep careful notes. Children's egocentric speech during play also serves to regulate them (Vygotsky).

Afterwards, learners look for causes (attributions). Attributing a mediocre biology grade to an internal, changeable cause, such as "my study approach", lets them plan differently. They review their strategies, reward or sanction themselves, and react emotionally: frustration makes one student try harder and another give up. Reflection can also revise self-efficacy (Schunk & Usher, 2011).

The three subfunctions work in every phase. Regulation covers thoughts and emotions too, and sometimes not acting, which can take as much effort as acting. A cycle can take minutes, days or years (Zimmerman, 2011).

The cycle turns the subfunctions into a timeline: think ahead and believe you can, watch yourself while doing it, then explain, react and set new goals. Next: where these skills come from.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-observation belongs to the performance phase, self-evaluation to reflection, and self-reaction to the end of the cycle.✓ In Figure 2.1 the three subfunctions sit in the centre: they are involved in each of the three phases, before, during and after performance.
📝 Exam-style questionA student gets a mediocre grade and decides her flashcards were the wrong strategy for this course. In which phase is she, and which processes from Figure 2.1 is she using?

Model answer: She is in the self-reflection phase. She makes a causal attribution to an internal, changeable factor (her study approach) and carries out a strategic review of the strategies she used. Because the cause is changeable, she can plan alternatives, which feeds the forethought phase of the next cycle (for example revised goals and a new plan).

STEP5
Development of self-regulatory competence · social-self progression

From watching a model to running it yourself

❓ This step answers: How does self-regulation develop according to social cognitive theory, and what role does self-efficacy play at each level?
🪜 Climb the four levels: Lucas learns to write an essay. Place each moment on its level, from outside help to his own control.

Some words first. A model here is a person whose behaviour others watch and learn from. Vicariously means through someone else's experience. To emulate is to imitate. Social persuasion is encouragement from others that you can do it. In the picture: a staircase of four levels; the bottom two are lit by outside (social) help, the top two by the learner's own control.

Self-regulation skills are mostly learned. Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) describe four levels: observation, emulation, self-control and self-regulation. In the first two, the source of influence is social; in the last two, it is internal: the social-self progression (Schunk, 1999). Our example: watch a chef, cook along with the video, cook the recipe alone, then adapt it to what is in your fridge.

Observation: a teacher or peer models the skill and the forethought, performance and reflection behind it, and also shows her own confidence, which passes self-efficacy on vicariously. Emulation: students practise the modelled behaviour while the teacher gives feedback and encouragement, so their self-efficacy grows.

Self-control: learners perform on their own and start to build their own efficacy beliefs, but still follow the model's pattern. Self-regulation: they systematically adapt the skill to new personal and situational conditions, start strategies themselves, evaluate their performance, and are driven by their own efficacy beliefs (Zimmerman, 2000).

Schunk and Zimmerman did not claim this is the only way to develop self-regulation. It is a social cognitive account, and it stresses agency and self-efficacy throughout.

Self-regulation starts outside the learner, in a model and a coach, and moves inside: watch, copy, do it alone, adapt it. Next: six components that research shows feed into self-regulation.

⚠ The trap✗ At the self-control level, the learner adapts the strategy flexibly to new situations.✓ At self-control the learner performs on her own but still uses the model's representational patterns. Systematic adaptation to changing personal and environmental conditions marks the fourth level, self-regulation.
📝 Exam-style questionDescribe the social-self progression and explain why it has that name.

Model answer: Schunk and Zimmerman (1997) proposed four levels of self-regulatory competence: observation (learning from a model's demonstration, including the model's efficacy), emulation (practising the modelled behaviour with feedback and encouragement), self-control (performing independently but still following the model's patterns) and self-regulation (adapting performance to changing conditions, motivated by one's own efficacy beliefs). It is called social-self progression because the source of influence is social in the first two levels and becomes internalized, within the self, in the last two.

STEP6
Research evidence on the components of self-regulated learning

Six components that work together

❓ This step answers: Which six components of self-regulation does the research describe, and what does each one add?
🧩 Predict the findings: for each study, predict the result. Its component lights up on the board.

Some words first. Executive functions are mental control skills: holding information in mind (working memory), stopping an impulse (inhibitory control) and switching between tasks flexibly. Cognitive load is how heavily a task taxes working memory. Self-handicapping is creating an obstacle in advance so that a failure can be blamed on it. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. A coping model shows her own struggle and effort, not effortless success. Stereotype threat is the worry of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group. In the picture: a board of six components; each finding you predict lights up its component.

Bandura and Zimmerman name six components of self-regulation: cognition, behaviour, motivation, emotion, social and environment. They act together: each can be the goal of an effort or a subgoal of another. In plain words: self-regulation is not just willpower; your thinking, doing, wanting, feeling, people and surroundings all take part and affect each other.

Self-regulation needs working and long-term memory, and schemas that lighten the load. It depends on three executive functions: working memory, inhibitory control and task switching (Hofmann et al., 2012). A chaotic environment or a heavy task leaves fewer resources for monitoring; self-efficacy and prior knowledge cushion this. Monitoring matters most while a skill is new; once you are skilled, heavy monitoring might impede performance (Mace et al., 2001). So athletes avoid over-thinking, and artists value incubation (pausing monitoring).

Visible self-regulation includes organizing your space, taking notes, recording progress, limiting distractions and rewarding yourself. Klassen et al. (2008): students with low self-efficacy for self-regulation procrastinated more. Self-handicapping means creating an obstacle in advance so an expected failure can be blamed on an outside cause rather than yourself (Urdan & Midgley, 2001). A meta-analysis of over 25,000 students linked it to poorer performance (Schwinger et al., 2014).

Self-efficacy decides whether people persist or give up; outcome expectations, value and interest matter too. Mastery goals go with better strategies (Schunk & Ertmer, 2000). Performance goals (beating others) make challenges discouraging and are often fuelled by believing ability is fixed (Dweck, 2006); avoidance goals breed defensive strategies that bring about what students fear. Self-set rewards motivate more than praise or money from others (Bandura, 1986); leaning on outside regulators lowers motivation.

Emotions affect every phase, and managing them goes with better learning (Pekrun, 1992). Fear can block a start, or spur action; challenge can be a catalyst. Pride with a sense of responsibility leads to renewed effort; shame or anxiety, especially when causes feel uncontrollable, can lead to avoidance and withdrawal (Pekrun et al., 2002).

Regulation is socially mediated. Coping models, who talk about their own hard-won progress, can persuade onlookers that effort will work for them too (Pajares, 2006); a similar peer can also be motivating. Knowing when and whom to ask for help is a skill. Parents and teachers co-regulate, and the learner later uses that guidance alone. Announcing goals creates accountability, and people choose settings for their social pull, such as a library or a gym.

Living situation, school climate and stressors all play a role. People often lean on external structures such as deadlines; with heavy outside demands, students need their own regulation less and may come to depend on outside control, though direct instruction can build self-observation and self-evaluation. Schmader et al. (2008): under stereotype threat, managing the threat leaves less cognitive capacity for the task; strong self-efficacy can buffer. Good self-regulators tailor their environment with timers and planners.

The six form a web: low self-efficacy (motivation) shows up as procrastination (behaviour); a threat (environment) costs cognitive capacity through emotion; a model (social) can rebuild belief. These links are our illustration of the chapter's point that the components interact.

⚠ The trap✗ The more closely a learner monitors herself, the better she performs, at every stage of skill.✓ The need for self-monitoring may be highest while a skill is being developed; once proficient, heightened self-monitoring might even impede performance (Mace et al., 2001), which is why athletes are coached not to over-think and writers value incubation.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain, with the chapter's evidence, how environmental stressors such as stereotype threat can undermine self-regulated learning through other components.

Model answer: In social cognitive theory the components interact. Where social messages are perceived as threatening, students must spend effort managing the threat (emotion regulation), which leaves less cognitive capacity for regulating their performance on the task (Schmader et al., 2008): emotion regulation comes at the expense of cognitive regulation. Whether students withstand such adversity depends partly on personal characteristics: a strong sense of self-efficacy can act as a buffer, while others give up or become paralyzed.

Scaffolding with suitable examples reduces cognitive load (Choi et al., 2014).

Effort is good, but knowing when to give up a goal or strategy is part of self-regulation; even growth-mindset messages can be misused (Dweck, 2015). Teachers can help students frame self-evaluation adaptively, for example by turning anger at oneself toward the task, and focus on what they can control.

William James saw voluntary attention as the brief effort that launches new habits, and estimated that habit makes up 99.99% of daily life. Schools are ideal places to train that attention and to show students many possible ideals.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Why is self-observation alone not reliably related to self-regulation?

Because whether it leads to change depends on mediating factors: its timing (before, during or after), whether change feels controllable and valued, and motivation for the activity. The gaming teenager is aware of his sedentary life but does not feel in control or value exercise. Change also needs self-evaluation against a valued standard and a self-reaction.

2. Why do the authors say self-set goals and incentives deserve a place in the classroom?

Social cognitive theory suggests self-set goals and incentives are more powerful motivators than externally imposed ones (self-incentives have been shown to be more effective than external rewards such as praise or money; Bandura, 1986), and reliance on external regulators goes with lower motivation. Helping students set their own goals and incentives acquaints them with their agency, and internalized plans have more lasting effects.

3. Give two ways an emotion can work in opposite directions in the cycle.

Frustration after a hard task leads one student to renewed determination and another to withdrawal of effort. Fear of failure can block the start of effort, yet fear can also motivate action, and challenge can become a catalyst. Pride with personal responsibility leads to renewed effort; shame, anxiety or depression, especially when causes seem uncontrollable, can lead to avoidance and withdrawal.
