Block A · Large Language Models as a Research Tool

❓ What are large language models, how can psychologists adapt them for research, and what must be solved before they can be trusted?

🔮A.1

What LLMs Are and How They Work

❓ This lecture answers: What is an LLM, how is it fitted, and why does it produce plausible rather than correct language?
A.1.1
From counting words to meaning in context

📜 Word counting (1960s) → latent semantic analysis (1990s) → LIWC, topic models (2010s) → LLMs. Bag-of-words ignores context; LLMs approximate meaning in context, trained on hundreds of billions of utterances. 40% of US teachers used ChatGPT weekly after 3 months.

A.1.2
How the models are fitted

🧮 Transformer ≈ a massive system of nonlinear regressions. Pre-training: masked language modelling + next-sentence classification; backpropagation. Newer models add instruction tuning and RLHF.

A.1.3
Plausible, not correct: the college-advising example

🎓 The LLM advisor suggests a timeline and online courses (no effect in a randomised trial) and “reduce stress”: a stress-is-debilitating mindset. LLMs parrot their training culture; that bias can be studied as digital ethnography.

A.1.4
What LLMs could do for psychology

🧰 Measurement (score belonging, suicide risk), experimentation (stimuli, items, synthetic pilots), practice (therapist training, teacher feedback). Each needs validation.

🎛️A.2

Adapting LLMs for Psychological Purposes

❓ This lecture answers: How can psychologists teach an LLM the scientific version of a construct?
A.2.1
Why off-the-shelf is not enough

🎯 Off-the-shelf = the internet’s view of a construct. Science needs the expert version, so tune on curated data and then evaluate. Example: DocsGPT for medical prose.

A.2.2
Fine-tuning

🔧 New data, parameters change; 5–100 examples vs hundreds of thousands from scratch. Box 2: 650 coded essays fine-tuned RoBERTa to score ≈11,000; belonging themes predicted better outcomes, more so for minoritized students.

A.2.3
Prompt-tuning

💬 In-context learning; parameters unchanged; 1–10 examples; prompt engineering. Box 3 (Clapper et al.): 5 expert speeches → ratings n.s. from experts (P > 0.05), 75% of the gap closed; ≈1,000 adolescent raters.

A.2.4
Tools for using LLMs

🛠️ APIs/online interfaces (easy, limited, paid) vs downloading/hosting (control, privacy, needs GPUs). Hugging Face, DLATK, openai, langchain, chainlit; R: text, TheOpenAIR, askgpt, reticulate.

✅ Quick check: why is prompt-tuning less likely to overcome bias than fine-tuning?

Because the model’s parameters are not updated; the biases learned in pre-training stay in the model, and a few examples in the prompt only steer its output.

⚠️A.3

Four Concerns and the Way Forward

❓ This lecture answers: What must be solved before psychologists can trust LLMs, and what should the field invest in?
A.3.1
How to evaluate an LLM

⚖️ Expert evaluation (agreement with experts) suffers from the intersubjectivity problem: coders guess, cross-group and power gaps, no single right answer (compliments and low self-esteem). Impact evaluation: effect on real people (attitudes before/after); failures are ambiguous. Use both iteratively.

A.3.2
Bias in LLMs

⚖️ Representational (portrayal) vs allocational (resources) harm. Web text is engagement-driven, and LLM text trains future LLMs. Audits vary names/pronouns but miss intersectional bias; guardrails can hide bias. AI-in-the-loop, humans in charge.

A.3.3
The black box

📦 Outputs are easy to read (unlike LIWC or topic models), but processes are opaque. Probing: vary one feature (“I care” vs “I don’t care”). Asking the LLM to explain itself is circular.

A.3.4
Reproducibility

🎲 Stochastic outputs; temperature 0 = always the most likely word. Treat like participant variability: generate many responses, publish parameters and outputs; don’t set temperature to 0.

A.3.5
Outlook: three investments

🏗️ Keystone datasets (Texas Mindset Initiative, 1,000 teachers), psychological benchmarks with privacy standards, equitable access (ChatGPT ≈ US $3 billion; licences, shared computing, Python training).

Block B · LLMs and Critical Thinking Under Time Constraints

❓ Does an LLM help or harm critical thinking, and does that depend on when you get it and how much time you have?

🧪B.1

The Question and the Design

❓ This lecture answers: How do you measure critical thinking, and how was the 4 × 2 experiment built?
B.1.1
Critical thinking, and how to measure it

📝 Analyse, evaluate, synthesise conflicting information. Standardised tests (Watson-Glaser, Halpern) point to the flaw; self-report overestimates; performance assessment (iPAL): realistic documents + essay, objective scoring.

B.1.2
Why time might matter

⏱️ Access timing: late help worked in maths (Kumar et al.) and writing (Qin et al.), but early scaffolding might help too. Time availability: pressure shifts people to heuristics (Gonthier). RQ1 and RQ2.

B.1.3
The 4 × 2 experiment

🧪 n = 393 (Prolific, preregistered). Early (first third) / Continuous / Late (final third) / No LLM × 10 or 30 min. GPT-4o limited to 7 documents. Water-contamination council decision. Measures: essay, myside bias, recall, evaluation, comprehension, self-report.

B.1.4
Grading and analysis

🤖 Essays graded by an LLM, checked against a human on 100 essays (ICC .70–.82; 94.7% agreement; κ = .76). ANCOVA with covariates; Tukey HSD.

🔀B.2

The Results: A Temporal Reversal

❓ This lecture answers: When did the LLM help, when did it hurt, and could people tell?
B.2.1
Essay quality: what helps under pressure hurts with time

🔀 10 min: Early 3.80, Continuous 3.49 > No 2.75, Late 1.86. 30 min: Late 5.77, No 5.76 > Continuous 4.71, Early 4.51. Time gain: Late +3.91, No +3.01, Continuous +1.22, Early ≈ 0.

B.2.2
Myside bias: late access as a balancing check

⚖️ 30 min: Late 3.42 vs No 4.41 (p < .05) with similar arguments (5.25 vs 5.12). Solo work grew more one-sided with time (+1.92). Low scores for Early/Continuous = fewer arguments.

B.2.3
Recall, evaluation and comprehension

🧠 30 min recall: Continuous 2.03 < No 2.99 (Early 2.23, marginal). Time raised recall 42% (No), 32% (Late), not for early access. Evaluation and comprehension improved with time; Continuous hurt trustworthiness judgements.

B.2.4
Self-report versus performance

🙈 Self-assessment barely varied while performance differed strongly: measure performance, not opinions.

⚓B.3

Mechanisms, Design Implications and Limits

❓ This lecture answers: Why did early access hurt when there was time, and what should AI tools do about it?
B.3.1
What participants actually did

🔍 Copying rare (0–15%), textual overlap common (64–94%). Early users saw more LLM arguments but added few unique ones (+0.28, +0.07 vs +1.80, +2.10). Late users used AI as a final check: 8 added ≈2.22 pro arguments. Fewer documents viewed while writing with early access.

B.3.2
Why: anchoring versus building your own schema

⚓ Early AI output anchors later thinking, even when used “only” to summarise. Working alone first builds a schema, so later AI use is strategic. Solo work can itself become one-sided.

B.3.3
Design implications

🛠️ Think-first nudges and friction; orientation not solutions (study mode); reduce anchoring under pressure (source diversity, several candidate answers); time-aware tools; practise without AI. Research: performance measures, consider time.

B.3.4
Limitations

⚠️ One civic task, count scheme, little domain expertise; Prolific crowdworkers in a timed lab-style setting; one way of operationalising time; single session. (Plus: an LLM graded the essays.)

Block C · LLMs and Creativity: The Goldilocks Effect

❓ Does collaborating more with AI make people more creative, or is there a best amount?

🐻C.1

The Goldilocks Hypothesis and the Experimental Design

❓ This lecture answers: Why might moderate AI use be best for creativity, and how was this tested?
C.1.1
From productivity to creativity

📈 AI raises productivity (quantity). Creativity = novel + useful ideas (Amabile, 1982); ≈60% use AI for creative purposes. Maybe “too much of a good thing” (Grant & Schwartz, 2011).

C.1.2
Why moderate should be best

🐻 Low: own view, time spent generating. Moderate: AI as inspiration + own thinking. High: standardised AI ideas, anchoring, threat. AI-vs-no-AI designs can only find straight lines.

C.1.3
Study 1: design and measures

💼 139 business professionals; Mike’s $10, 3-hour challenge; 15 min; ChatGPT-3.5 ≤1 / 4–6 / ≥9 uses (pilot ideal 4.04). Creativity: human judges (ICC .73), business value (ICC .71), AI rater. Coded: problem restructuring, knowledge diversity.

📊C.2

The Experiments: An Inverted U and Its Mechanism

❓ This lecture answers: Did moderate use win, and why?
C.2.1
Study 1 results

🏆 Human-rated: moderate 4.02 vs 3.49 and 3.39 (d = .43, .50). Business value: 5.67 vs 4.60 and 4.69. AI-rated: overall p = .061; moderate > low only.

C.2.2
Why? Knowledge diversity, not problem restructuring

🌈 Restructuring: no difference (p = .90). Knowledge diversity: 5.10 vs 4.30 and 4.57. Indirect effect through diversity: + at low (.38), ≈ 0 at moderate, − at high (−.31). Mostly “ghostwriter” use.

C.2.3
Study 2: a preregistered replication

🔁 319 working adults; crowdsourced ratings (≈10 judges per proposal). Crowd creativity 4.33 vs 4.04, 4.11; AI-rated 4.72 vs 4.39, 4.31; diversity 4.81 vs 4.45, 4.60; restructuring n.s.

An LLM produces the typical, plausible answer; it augments human thinking only when people bring their own thinking first and use it in the right dose. Used too early or too much, it narrows thought toward its own defaults, and people don’t notice.

📄 Theme 8 on one page

🔮A1

What LLMs are

Prediction machines trained on vast text; meaning in context. They produce plausible, not correct, language and repeat cultural myths.
🎛️A2

Adapting them

Fine-tuning retrains parameters (powerful, costly); prompt-tuning shows a few examples (fast, shallower). Five expert examples → near-expert speeches.
⚠️A3

Four concerns

Expert + impact evaluation; representational and allocational bias; black box (probing); stochastic outputs. Invest in keystone data, benchmarks, access.
🧪B1

The study

393 people, a pollution decision from seven documents, AI early / throughout / late / never × 10 or 30 minutes; performance measures.
🔀B2

Temporal reversal

Rushed: AI first wins. Time: think first wins; early AI cuts recall; late AI reduces one-sidedness. Self-ratings didn’t change.
⚓B3

Why

Early AI anchors: fewer own arguments, less source checking. Working alone builds a schema; then AI is a second opinion. Design “think first” tools.
🐻C1

Goldilocks idea

Too little AI: own ideas only; too much: standard ideas, anchoring; moderate: inspiration. Tested with ≤1 / 4–6 / ≥9 uses.
📊C2

The experiments

Moderate use gave the most creative, valuable ideas (n = 139, 319) by widening knowledge diversity, not by reframing the problem.
🎨C3

The field

188 creative workers: creativity rose up to ≈5 of 7, then bent down. Correlational; needed controls; weakens demand effects.
💬 The whole theme to a friend: “ChatGPT and friends are giant prediction machines: they give you the most typical, plausible answer, great for analysing language at scale but able to sound right while being wrong, and they absorb society’s biases. Psychologists can teach them proper scientific concepts and should test them on real people. When you use them yourself, timing and dose matter. Rushed? Use AI from the start. Have time? Think first and use AI at the end as a second opinion, because early AI quietly anchors your thinking and you won’t notice. And for creativity, a moderate amount gives the widest range of ideas; too much and your ideas start looking like everyone else’s.”
