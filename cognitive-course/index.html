How does a mind turn the world into thought?

This course is not eight separate topics. It is one journey in eight stops. Information gets in, is held, is stored, is organised, is pictured, is used and is turned into something new. At the end, a machine holds up a mirror. Read the story once before you study, and every detail will have a place to land.

Theme 1 · Attention

🚪 The front door

What gets in, and what never does?

Nothing reaches your mind unless attention lets it in, so the journey starts at the door. The lesson is a humbling one: people miss a gorilla walking through a basketball game, a fight they run right past, a change in front of their eyes. And most people are sure they would have noticed.

Big idea: you see far less than you think. The door is narrow: capacity is limited.
  • ASimons: failures of awareness (inattentional and change blindness)
  • BSternberg et al.: filter models. Broadbent, Treisman and Deutsch & Deutsch argue about where the door closes
  • L1Lecture 1: types of attention, vigilance and task switching

Theme 2 · Working memory

🛠️ The workbench

Where do you hold things while you think?

What gets through the door lands on a small workbench. First we look at the older idea of short-term memory as one box (Atkinson and Shiffrin). Then Baddeley shows the bench has separate parts: a loop for sounds, a sketchpad for images, a supervisor that directs the work and a buffer that links it all together.

Big idea: thinking happens in a small, limited space, and the size of that space matters in real life.
  • AMatlin & Farmer: short-term memory, chunking, serial position, forgetting
  • BEysenck & Keane: Baddeley's working memory model and executive functions
  • CMcGonnell et al. (2024): children's arithmetic depends on working memory
↩ Builds on 1: the door decides what reaches the bench. Both have a capacity limit.

Theme 3 · Autobiographical memory and amnesia

📚 The life archive

How do you store the story of your own life?

Some of what you work with is kept, and the most personal store is your own life story. You remember it unevenly: almost nothing before age three, a bump in your teens and twenties. Your goals and identity shape what comes back. Amnesia shows what happens when storing breaks, and even taking a photo changes what you remember.

Big idea: memory is not a recording. You rebuild it every time you remember.
  • AAutobiographical memory: retention function, reminiscence bump, childhood amnesia, Conway's self-memory system
  • BAmnesia: what breaks, what survives, consolidation
  • CSoares & Storm (2018): the photo-taking impairment
  • L2Lecture 2: how easily memory can be changed
↩ Builds on 2: what you work with on the bench is what gets stored.

Theme 4 · Concepts and categories

🗂️ The filing system

How is all that knowledge organised?

An archive full of single memories would be useless without a way to sort it. Categories have no neat definitions: a robin is a "better" bird than a penguin. Concepts are wired together in a network where activation spreads. And when the network falls apart in semantic dementia, a duck gets drawn with four legs.

Big idea: single memories become general knowledge, held together by overlapping features and similarity.
  • AMurphy: classical, prototype, exemplar and knowledge views; typicality and the basic level
  • BRobinson-Riegler: semantic networks, spreading activation and priming
  • CBozeat et al. (2003): semantic dementia and drawing
↩ Builds on 3: Theme 3 was memory for your life; this is knowledge about the world.

Theme 5 · Mental imagery

🖼️ What the files look like

Is knowledge stored as pictures or as descriptions?

Once we know knowledge is organised, the next question is its form. Rotating a shape in your mind takes longer the further you turn it, and scanning a mental map takes longer for longer distances: that looks like pictures. The propositional view says it is language-like underneath. And bizarre images have their own, measurable memory effects.

Big idea: the mind can work with images, but whether they are true pictures is the debate.
  • A–CSternberg et al. and Matlin: dual code vs propositional, rotation, scanning, imagery in the brain
  • DBesken & Mulligan (2021): the bizarreness effect
  • L4Lecture 4: the same question about words, language and thought
↩ Builds on 2 and 4: images live on the sketchpad of the workbench, and imagery is one format of the files.

Theme 6 · Judgement and decision making

⚖️ Using what you know

How do real people judge and choose?

Now the mind has a door, a bench, an archive and organised knowledge, so it is time to use them. Everything before catches up with you here. A narrow door and a small bench mean you take shortcuts. Rebuilt memory means what comes to mind easily feels more likely. Categories make you judge by how typical something looks.

Big idea: expected utility says how a perfectly logical person should choose; heuristics and prospect theory show how real people do.
  • A–BHeuristics and biases: representativeness, availability, anchoring, base rates
  • CDecision making: expected utility, prospect theory, framing, loss aversion
  • L3Lecture 3: cognition and emotion
  • L5Lecture 5: dual process, fast and slow thinking
↩ Builds on 1 to 4: availability comes from memory, representativeness from categories, shortcuts from limited capacity.

Theme 7 · Problem solving and creativity

💡 Going beyond what you know

How do you make an option that does not exist yet?

Deciding means choosing between options that already exist. Problem solving and creativity mean making a new one. This is the highest level of thinking in the course, and it uses everything before it: the bench to hold the problem, knowledge to borrow analogies from, and attention to keep old, stuck ideas from blocking new ones.

Big idea: what you already know can help you (analogy, expertise) and trap you (fixation, mental set).
  • A–BMatlin, Robinson-Riegler and Sternberg: problem space, strategies, analogy, insight, fixation
  • CCamarda et al. (2018): inhibitory control and creative ideas
↩ Builds on 2, 4 and 6: working memory holds the problem, concepts supply analogies, and decision shortcuts become problem-solving heuristics.

Theme 8 · Large language models in psychology

🤖 The mirror

What does a machine that seems to think teach us about our own minds?

We end with a machine that seems to do everything we studied: it attends, holds context, stores knowledge, judges and creates. That is why it comes last. You can only ask whether an AI "thinks" once you know what thinking involves. And the question turns back on us: what happens to our thinking when we lean on it?

Big idea: LLMs are a powerful tool for psychology, but when and how we use them changes our own thinking.
  • ADemszky et al. (2023): using LLMs in psychology research
  • BZhi, Kumar & Lee (2026): when you get AI access matters
  • CHuang (2025): does AI make us more or less creative?
↩ Loops back to 3: just as a photo changes what you remember, AI may change how you think.
