How do you study so it actually sticks?

This theme reads three articles that each answer one part of that question. Your case: you are the designer of a learning tool, building an app that teaches students how to study. Each article gives you one design rule.

The story of this theme · 7 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the three articles fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. This theme answers one question: why does some studying stick while the rest disappears? The core idea is simple. Your brain keeps what it has to work for. Rereading and highlighting feel busy, but the information mostly passes by. When you have to make something yourself (explain it, map it, answer a question) and you come back to it after you have started to forget, your brain builds stronger, better-connected memories.

Each article looks at one side of that idea. Chi & Wylie show what kind of work counts: the more you produce, the deeper you understand. Brod shows the work must fit the learner: a child's brain cannot yet do every kind of work an adult's can. Gerbier & Toppino show the work must be spread over time: a memory gets stronger each time you pull it back after a gap.

Why a psychologist learns this: helping people learn is part of many psychology jobs, with pupils, students, employees or patients learning new habits. The most common mistake people make is choosing what feels easy over what actually works. This theme gives you the science to spot that mistake and design something better.

Article 1 of 3 · The HOW

The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active Learning Outcomes

Chi, M. T. H., & Wylie, R. (2014). Educational Psychologist, 49(4), 219–243.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what kind of article is it?

Two learning scientists who wanted a usable definition

Before looking at any idea, it helps to know what kind of text you are reading. A review tells you something different from a single experiment. So we start with the authors, their goal and the shape of the article.

Michelene Chi and Ruth Wylie are learning scientists at Arizona State University. They study how students learn from what they do with material. Their article appeared in 2014 in Educational Psychologist, a journal of the American Psychological Association. It is a theoretical framework: an organised set of ideas that explains and predicts something. Here, it predicts how much students learn from different kinds of activity. Chi published an earlier version in 2009 under another name, DOLA (Differentiated Overt Learning Activities).

The article has three parts, shown in the figure. Section 1 builds the theory. Section 2 tests it against existing studies, plus one study from Chi's own lab. Section 3 asks where the theory can fail and how teachers can use it. Because most evidence comes from other researchers' studies, the main method is re-interpretation, not new experiments.

This shapes how you should read every claim in it. "Chi and Wylie found..." usually means "Chi and Wylie interpreted earlier studies as showing...". A framework earns its value by organising evidence and making clear predictions, not by producing new data. Next we look at the problem that made them write the article.

⚠ The trap✗ Chi and Wylie ran a series of experiments to prove ICAP.✓ They built a framework and tested it mainly by re-coding other researchers' studies. Menekse et al. (2013) is the only study run specifically to test all four modes.
★ ICAP is a framework that predicts learning from what students visibly do.
STEP2
What problem does the article try to solve?

"Be active" is good advice that tells a teacher nothing

❓ This step answers: What problem does ICAP try to solve?

We know who the authors are. But why did they need a new framework? Teachers already hear constantly that active learning beats passive learning.

The trouble is that "be active" is a slogan, not a plan. Think of a coach who tells you to "play better" but never says how. The authors name three gaps. Broad advice does not tell teachers how to create an active activity. Teachers have few criteria to choose which activity is best. And there are no guidelines to upgrade an assignment they already use.

The authors also narrow down what "engagement" means. Much research looks at motivational engagement (wanting to take part), behavioural engagement (attending, doing homework) or emotional engagement (liking school). Chi and Wylie study cognitive engagement: the mental work a student does with the material, judged from small visible behaviours. They separate the learning activity, the task the teacher chooses, from the engagement activity, what the student actually does with it.

So the article asks three linked questions. Can engagement be defined by overt behaviour, meaning what you can see? Can those behaviours be sorted into a few modes? Does each mode predict a different amount of learning? The next step answers the first two.

⚠ The trap✗ ICAP measures how motivated or interested students are.✓ ICAP sets motivation aside. It looks at cognitive engagement, judged from what students visibly do with the material.
📝 Exam-style questionA teacher says: "My students are clearly engaged: they attend every class and hand in all their homework." Which kind of engagement is she describing, and why is it not what ICAP measures?

Model answer: Behavioural engagement: large-grained participation. ICAP measures cognitive engagement, seen in small behaviours during learning, such as generating an explanation.

STEP3
Key concepts and definitions

Four modes, four mental processes, four outcomes

❓ This step answers: What are the four modes, and why should they lead to different amounts of learning?
👆 Tap a step of the staircase to see that mode's full row from the article's Table 2.
Tap a mode on the staircase to see its full row from Table 2.

We now know the goal: define engagement by what students do. So which behaviours count, and how do they differ? Chi and Wylie sort them into four modes. Each higher mode asks more of the learner.

In the passive mode, you receive information and do nothing else, like listening to a lecture without notes. In the active mode, you physically handle the material in a way that focuses attention: underlining, copying, pausing a video. In the constructive mode, you produce something that goes beyond the material: an explanation, a concept map, notes in your own words. In the interactive mode, two learners talk, both are constructive, and they take frequent turns.

Why should this matter? Each mode triggers a different knowledge-change process. Passive learners store information on its own, so it stays "inert". One student read how blood clots, yet when asked for her own theory of healing, she gave her old idea: the new knowledge sat apart. Active learners integrate: marking a sentence activates what they know, and the new part fills its gaps. Constructive learners infer: they add ideas and can repair a wrong model. Interactive learners co-infer: each builds on the other's ideas.

These processes predict four outcomes: recall, apply, transfer and co-create. Hence the ICAP hypothesis: Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive. Each mode also includes the one below it, because you cannot draw a diagram without moving your hand. Tap the staircase for each mode's full row. The next step gives the rules for using the modes correctly.

⚠ The trap✗ Group work is interactive.✓ Interactive needs a dialogue in which both partners are constructive and take turns. Two students copying each other's answers are only active. One explaining while the other says "uh-huh" is an individual dialogue.
📝 Exam-style questionA learner pauses an instructional video to watch a difficult part again. Which knowledge-change process does ICAP link to this behaviour?

Model answer: Integrating. Pausing and rewinding is an active manipulation. It focuses attention, activates prior knowledge, and new information is assimilated into it.

STEP4
The rules behind the taxonomy

Same reading task, four different modes

❓ This step answers: What rules must hold for the ICAP classification to work?

The four modes sound simple. But real classrooms are messy, and students don't always do what a task asks. So the taxonomy rests on eight assumptions that tell you how to apply it.

The first idea is that task and mode are independent. Any task can be done in any mode, as the grid shows. Reading silently is passive, underlining is active, explaining is constructive, questioning a partner is interactive. The mode depends on what the student does, not on the task chosen.

The assumption that matters most in practice is intended versus enacted. A teacher may intend a constructive task, such as a summary in your own words. A student can still enact it actively, by copying and deleting sentences. That is why teachers must analyse the outputs. Overt behaviour can also differ from covert thinking. A student who only underlines may be explaining it to herself silently. So ICAP only claims a greater probability, not certainty.

Two more assumptions explain the ranking. The modes form a hierarchy: higher modes include the lower ones. And the boundaries are fuzzy: plugging numbers into a formula is active, but re-deriving the formula is constructive. Tap the tiles for all eight. Then try the game, and see how the authors tested the hypothesis.

STEP5
Research method

They re-label other people's experiments

❓ This step answers: How do Chi and Wylie test whether I > C > A > P is true?

We now have a hypothesis and rules for applying it. But a framework built on reasoning needs evidence. How can you test it without running dozens of new experiments?

The authors re-label earlier studies. They take a published study, code each condition as P, A, C or I, and check whether the results fall in the predicted order. It is like re-scoring old football matches with a new system and checking whether it predicts who won. The coding rule is one question: did the learner produce something beyond the material given?

They collect four kinds of evidence. The strongest is one lab study with all four modes (Menekse et al., 2013). Next come two studies that happen to contain three modes. Then pairwise comparisons: the same activity done in two modes, for note taking, concept mapping and self-explaining. Finally, classroom studies, coded by their highest mode. One coding trap: a whole-class discussion counts as passive for most students, because only a few speak.

This method is clever, but the codes are assigned after seeing the studies. That is why the authors stress looking at what students actually did. Code the real conditions in the game, then see what the evidence showed.

⚠ The trap✗ A summary is always constructive.✓ Coleman et al. found many summaries did not go beyond the text, so summarizing was coded active. Only summaries that add inferences are constructive.
📝 Exam-style questionIn a study, group A draws diagrams from a text, group B writes summaries of it, and group C only reads it. Which order of learning does ICAP predict, and why?

Model answer: Diagrams > summaries > reading (C > A > P). Drawing generates something new, summarizing mostly selects, reading only receives. Gobert & Clement (1999) found this order.

We know how they tested it. So what did the studies show? Start with the strongest test, then look at studies with fewer modes.

Menekse and colleagues asked whether all four modes differ within one study. Students learned materials science by reading a text (passive), reading and highlighting (active), interpreting a graph alone (constructive) or with a peer (interactive). Pretest scores were similar, around 50%. Posttest scores rose in the predicted order, by about 8 to 10% per mode. In plain words: each step up the staircase added learning.

Coleman and colleagues had students rotate through three roles: listening to a classmate, summarizing and explaining. Explaining gave the best near and far transfer, then summarizing, then listening (see the chart). Gobert and Clement found the same order for drawing, summarizing and reading.

The pairwise studies tell a subtler story. When one activity moves up a mode, learning rises: underlining beats reading, free-form notes beat cut-and-paste. When two activities share a mode, there is no difference: highlighting equals copy-pasting. ICAP predicts both patterns. Zhang and Linn add a key lesson: pairs who drew molecules beat pairs who selected pictures. Working together was not enough; the task had to be generative. Next: where does the prediction fail?

⚠ The trap✗ The interactive group scored highest because it knew more at the start.✓ Pretest scores were about equal (around 50%). The differences appeared only after learning, which is what makes the study convincing.
📝 Exam-style questionStudents who highlight key sentences learn as much as students who copy and paste key sentences into their notes. How does ICAP explain this?

Model answer: Both activities are active: students select but do not generate. ICAP predicts no difference between activities in the same mode.

STEP7
Caveats

When ICAP's prediction can fail

❓ This step answers: When can ICAP's prediction fail?

The evidence looks strong. But Chi and Wylie admit that ICAP looks at only one thing: what students do. So when can other factors override it?

The first factor is the test. If questions only ask for recall, even passive learners can answer them. Aleven and Koedinger found that explaining helped on hard and transfer problems, but not on easy-to-guess items. In Menekse's study, too, easy multiple-choice items showed no differences. In plain words: a shallow test cannot reveal deep learning.

The second factor is the domain, the topic itself. Some rules are arbitrary, like when to use "a", "an" or "the". Explaining such rules did not help, because there is no deeper reason to infer (Wylie, 2011). Some topics are so new that learners have no schema to build on, like emergence in science. The third factor is task differences within a mode. Pairs who invented a method learned more than pairs who compared cases, though both were interactive. Inventing made students connect features (100% made triple-feature statements, versus 10% of comparers).

There are also built-in caveats. The knowledge-change processes are hypothetical: nobody measured them directly. And overt behaviour is only a proxy for thinking. Still, ICAP's core claim concerns differences across modes, and those mostly survive. Next: how the framework becomes a practical tool.

⚠ The trap✗ Two interactive tasks giving different results proves ICAP wrong.✓ ICAP's predictions are about differences across modes. Inventing and comparing both still beat lower modes.
📝 Exam-style questionA study finds no difference between a self-explanation group and a rereading group on a test of ten fact-recall questions. Give the most likely explanation in ICAP terms.

Model answer: The assessment is too shallow. Recall can be achieved with passive learning, so differences between modes cannot show up.

STEP8
ICAP in practice

Explain, choose a control, evaluate, design

❓ This step answers: How can teachers and researchers use ICAP?

We now know what ICAP predicts and where it fails. The last part of the article asks a practical question: what can you do with it? The authors name four uses.

First, ICAP can explain mixed findings. Some studies say summarizing helps, others say it doesn't. Coding the conditions solves the puzzle. Summarizing by answering who/what questions (constructive) beat reading aloud (passive). Summarizing by selecting sentences (active) lost to explaining (constructive). Whether summarizing "works" depends on how it is done and what it is compared with. Second, ICAP helps pick a control condition. If a constructive activity beats a passive one, you only know it was more engaging. To test its specific ingredient, compare it with another constructive activity.

Third, ICAP helps evaluate students' work. If students use only the six concepts a teacher gave them, their concept map is active; extra concepts make it constructive. Fourth, ICAP guides design: lecture, then guided notes (active), then a concept map (constructive), then building the map with a partner (interactive).

Finally, the authors compare ICAP with other theories. Like constructivism, it focuses on the learner, but it is more concrete. It often predicts the opposite of cognitive load theory: more effort, more learning. The two agree when the extra effort is germane load, as in self-explaining. And it complements Bloom's taxonomy, which describes goals (ends), while ICAP describes activities (means). Try upgrading a lesson in the game.

⚠ The trap✗ ICAP and cognitive load theory make the same predictions.✓ Load theory says more load makes learning harder. ICAP says more effortful modes give more learning. They agree only when the effort is germane load, such as self-explaining.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher shows that students who answer metacognitive prompts learn more than students who read study advice. What should she do before claiming that the prompts themselves caused the gain?

Model answer: Compare the prompts with another activity that also requires generative responses (same mode). Otherwise the result only shows that a constructive task beats a passive one.

STEP9
Conclusion and main take-aways

The article in one minute

❓ This step answers: What should you remember from the whole article?

We have walked through the whole article: the problem, the modes, the rules, the evidence, the limits and the uses. Here is how it fits together.

ICAP turns "be active" into four visible modes, each tied to a mental process and a level of understanding. The evidence, mostly re-coded from earlier studies, supports Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive. The prediction can fail with shallow tests, unusual topics or very different tasks within one mode. Used well, ICAP helps teachers and app designers raise engagement by changing what students must produce.

✅ Comprehension check

1. Explain why a summary made by copying and deleting sentences counts as active, even though the teacher asked for a constructive summary.

Because ICAP classifies the enacted behaviour. Copy-and-delete only selects existing sentences; nothing beyond the text is generated. The intended mode (constructive) differs from the enacted mode (active).

2. A study app asks learners to tap one of four answers after each video. Which mode is this, and how could you raise it to constructive?

Active: selecting from a menu generates nothing. Raise it by asking learners to type the answer or an explanation in their own words.

3. Explain why pairs who select pictures together are not interactive in ICAP terms.

Interactive requires both partners to be constructive. Selecting is active, so working together does not lift it to interactive (Zhang & Linn, 2013).

A developmental researcher reviews six strategies

❓ This step answers: Who wrote this review, and what does it include?
Author
Garvin Brod
Where
DIPF | Leibniz Institute for Research and Information in Education, and Goethe University, Frankfurt am Main, Germany

ICAP told us that constructive activities usually beat active ones. This second article zooms in on those constructive activities. First, who wrote it and what kind of text is it?

Garvin Brod is a developmental researcher at the DIPF Leibniz Institute and Goethe University in Frankfurt. Developmental science studies how people change as they grow up. His article is a review article: it collects existing studies and meta-analyses and weighs what they show together. It appeared in Educational Psychology Review (2021).

Brod sets a clear scope, shown in the figure. He reviews six strategies: concept mapping, explaining, predicting, questioning, practice testing and drawing. He only includes school-age learners, students generating on their own (not in groups), and learning of facts and concepts (declarative knowledge). He leaves out summarizing and enacting, because it is hard to tell whether they really add something new.

These choices matter. Leaving out group work makes age differences easier to see, because results then depend on the child, not on a partner. Next: the question that drives the whole review.

⚠ The trap✗ Brod compares collaborative and individual generative learning in children.✓ Collaborative generation was deliberately left out. The review only covers students generating on their own.

STEP2
What question does the article ask?

Are generative strategies equally effective at every age?

❓ This step answers: Why ask whether learning strategies depend on age?

We know the scope. But why is age the question? Big reviews had already ranked the best learning strategies.

Brod opens with a simple line: "Teaching an 8-year-old is different from teaching a 16-year-old." Yet most studies in those big reviews were done with university students. That is like testing a running shoe only on adult athletes and then selling it for toddlers.

So the review has two goals. First, for which ages has each strategy actually been shown to work? Second, are there age-related differences in how well it works? Earlier reviews, such as Dunlosky et al. (2013) and Fiorella and Mayer, looked for the strategy that works best for everyone. Brod asks for whom each one works.

This matters for anyone designing learning for children, including the app in your case. If evidence from students does not carry over to children, the advice may simply fail. Before looking at age, we need a precise definition of what counts as generative.

⚠ The trap✗ The review asks which generative strategy is the best one.✓ It asks for which ages each strategy works, and whether its effectiveness changes with age.
📝 Exam-style questionAn app developer cites research with university students to justify drawing tasks for 7-year-olds. Which concern does Brod's review raise?

Model answer: Most evidence comes from university students; it may not transfer to young children. For drawing, the evidence below grade 4 is in fact unfavorable.

STEP3
Key concepts

Generative = you produce something beyond what you were given

❓ This step answers: What exactly is a generative learning strategy?

We know what Brod wants to compare. But what makes a strategy generative in the first place? He needs a sharp definition to decide what goes in the review.

Brod defines generative learning strategies (GLSs) as activities that make learners produce something meaningful that goes beyond the information provided. Compare underlining a recipe with explaining to a friend why you add baking soda. Only the second makes you add something. To produce it, you must activate what you already know and link it to the new information. That linking is thought to integrate new information into your existing knowledge. The idea comes from constructivism: learning is active building on what you already know.

This is what separates GLSs from other methods. Passive methods, such as listening or rereading, only take information in. Active methods, such as highlighting or paraphrasing, handle the material but reproduce it. GLSs add to it. Brod notes this is "highly similar" to Chi's distinction between active and constructive, so GLSs are constructive activities. One surprise: practice testing counts, whether or not your answer is correct. Summarizing counts only if you enrich the text, not if you merely shorten it.

So the six strategies share one core: activate, link, integrate. But each also needs other skills, and those skills grow with age. That is the next step.

⚠ The trap✗ A practice test only counts as generative if you answer correctly.✓ It counts regardless of correctness. Producing an answer from memory is the generative part; even a failed attempt helps structure knowledge.
📝 Exam-style questionA student shortens a text to half its length without adding anything. Is this a generative learning strategy according to Brod (2021)? Explain.

Model answer: No. A summary only counts if it enriches the text with new content. Condensing or paraphrasing reproduces the given information.

STEP4
Development

Why children struggle with strategies: three deficiencies, three growth processes

❓ This step answers: Why might children profit less from strategies?

We have defined GLSs. Now the central puzzle: why would a strategy that helps a student fail for a child? Brod first summarises what developmental science knows about children and strategies.

Children use more strategies as they grow, and this explains much of their improvement in learning. Young children show three typical problems. With a production deficiency, a child does not use a useful strategy spontaneously. With a mediation deficiency, a child uses it when told, but lacks the prerequisites to profit, even after training. With a utilization deficiency, a child uses it spontaneously, but it helps less than it helps older learners.

Three developmental processes explain the growth. First, knowledge: more world knowledge gives a richer network to link new ideas to, and frees mental capacity. But studies that controlled for knowledge still found age effects. Second, executive functions: working memory, inhibition and shifting, which mature with the prefrontal cortex. They underlie reasoning and revising mental models, which every GLS needs. Third, metacognition: monitoring and controlling your own learning, which matures late.

There is also a hopeful twist. Because children use few strategies on their own, any strategy that does work for them may help them even more than adults. To find out which ones work, Brod reviews each strategy in turn.

⚠ The trap✗ Older children learn better simply because they know more.✓ Knowledge matters a lot, but studies controlling for prior knowledge still find age differences. Cognitive capacity plays a role too.

STEP7
Discussion

Different strategies need different prerequisites

❓ This step answers: Why do strategies differ in when they start to work?

We have seen that strategies start working at different ages. General development explains why all strategies work better as children grow. But it does not explain why they differ.

Brod's answer is prerequisites. Every strategy activates prior knowledge, but each also needs its own extra skills. Predicting and testing mainly need effortful retrieval: reaching into memory for a guess or an answer, then processing the feedback. Questioning and drawing need more. You must construct a diagnostic question or a drawing that can later help retrieval, and for that you need more prior knowledge and good metacognitive monitoring. Those develop late.

One study tests this idea directly. Breitwieser and Brod gave grade 4 and 5 children and university students a fact-learning task. Before seeing each fact, they generated either a prediction or an example. Students learned equally well both ways. Children learned more from predictions. The more adult-like a child's pattern, the higher the child's reasoning score. In plain words: generating examples needs reasoning, so it only pays off once reasoning has grown.

This leads to an important conclusion. Age itself explains nothing: an 8-year-old struggles because of missing prerequisites, not because of being 8. So the goal is to choose strategies for each learner. And where a prerequisite is missing, support can help, as the next step shows.

⚠ The trap✗ Young children fail at drawing because of their age.✓ Brod says age is not an explanatory variable. They lack prerequisites (analogical reasoning, monitoring); age is only a rough proxy.

We know younger learners lack some prerequisites. So should teachers give up on hard strategies for them? Not necessarily: they can change how much support they give.

The key move is giving learners part of the work. In Karpicke and colleagues' study, children who built a whole concept map made poor maps and learned nothing extra. Support works because it frees limited working memory for the generative part, and prompts take over monitoring that children cannot yet do. But there is a limit. If learners are given everything, they no longer generate anything, and the strategy stops working.

Brod is also honest about the review's limits. There are few age-comparative studies and even fewer that test the mechanisms. Most studies are published ones, so effects are probably overestimated. And there are very few studies below grade 4. His call: more research that links each strategy to the development of its prerequisites.

⚠ The trap✗ More support is always better for young children.✓ Support helps only up to a point. If everything is given, nothing is generated and the strategy is no longer generative.

STEP3
Key concepts

Same total time, spread out: the spacing effect

❓ This step answers: What is distributed practice, and how general is the effect?

We have the dilemma. Now we need precise words for it, because every finding in this review is described with them.

Distributed (spaced) practice means repeating the same material in sessions separated by time. Massed practice means repeating it in immediate succession. The total time stays the same. The advantage of spacing is called the spacing effect. The time between sessions is the lag, or inter-study interval. The time between the last session and the test is the retention interval.

A classroom study shows the size of the effect. Bloom and Shuell had high-school students learn foreign vocabulary. One group studied for one 30-minute session. Another studied for three 10-minute sessions over three days. Four days later, the spaced group remembered 35% more. Same time, better spread, more remembered. More generally, two spaced presentations can be twice as effective as two massed ones.

The effect is remarkably general. It appears for words, faces, pictures, texts, motor skills and maths. It appears in 5-month-old infants and in older adults. It even appears in fruit flies, sea slugs, bees and rodents. An effect found almost everywhere suggests a fundamental principle of memory, shaped by evolution. That calls for a general explanation, which the next steps look for.

⚠ The trap✗ Spaced practice works because you spend more time studying.✓ Total study time is held equal. Only the scheduling changes.

Model answer: The spaced class: the same total time distributed over sessions gives better retention (Bloom & Shuell found 35% better retention).

STEP5
Mechanisms

A repetition that comes too soon gets less processing

❓ This step answers: Why does spacing work? The first answer: the brain gives too-soon repeats less effort

We know the effect is real and general. Now the big question: why does it happen? The first explanation is about how much attention a repetition gets.

The deficient processing hypothesis says an immediately repeated item receives less processing the second time. Think of rereading a page you just read: it feels familiar, so your mind skims. Evidence supports this. Pupils dilate less (a sign of effort) at a massed repetition. People react faster to it, a sign of less attention. When learners set their own study time, they spend less time on massed repeats. Part of this is voluntary, but part is not: the effect survives even when people are paid to study harder.

Brain imaging shows the same thing. When an item repeats soon, brain activity is weaker than the first time: repetition suppression. Xue and colleagues showed faces several times. Spaced faces were recognised better and showed less suppression in the fusiform cortex, the face area. In Wagner's study, words were repeated after 2 minutes or 25 hours. After 25 hours, suppression was smaller and recognition two days later was better. But these links are correlational, and one replication did not find them.

Cells tell a matching story. Memories become stable through consolidation, at the level of synapses (minutes to hours) and of brain systems (days to years). In mouse brain tissue, spaced electrical stimulation produced stronger long-term potentiation than massed stimulation, and a drug that blocks protein synthesis reduced this benefit (Scharf et al.). But deficient processing has a gap: it cannot explain why memory gets worse when gaps become too long. The next step shows that pattern.

⚠ The trap✗ Deficient processing explains the whole spacing effect.✓ It cannot explain why memory declines when the gap is longer than optimal, or why the best gap depends on the retention interval.
📝 Exam-style questionLearners spend less self-paced study time on the second presentation of an immediately repeated item. Which account of the spacing effect does this support?

Model answer: Deficient processing: an immediate repetition feels familiar and receives less processing, so it is encoded less well.

Deficient processing suggests that longer gaps are always better. Is that true? The figure above lets you check. Pick each retention interval and watch where the curve peaks.

Cepeda and colleagues tested it over long time scales. People studied facts in two sessions separated by 0, 1, 7, 28, 84 or 168 days, and were tested 168 days later. Memory was best with a 28-day gap, and worse with both shorter and longer gaps. This shape is called an inverted U: memory rises with spacing, peaks, then falls again.

The best gap also moves. In a larger study, a 1-day gap was best when the test came after 7 days. A 21-day gap was best when the test came after 350 days. In plain words: the longer you need to remember something, the bigger your gaps should be. And spacing too little hurts more than spacing too much.

Why are gaps of a day or more special? After a day, there has been sleep, which helps consolidation, and the brain has started integrating the memory with existing knowledge. A repetition then strengthens the meaningful gist rather than surface details like the font. But none of this explains the decline at long gaps. That needs a different mechanism: retrieval.

⚠ The trap✗ The longest possible gap always gives the best memory.✓ Memory is an inverted U of the gap. Beyond the optimum it falls again; the optimum grows with the retention interval.

STEP7
Competing explanations

A repetition must remind you of the first time

❓ This step answers: Why does spacing work? The second answer: each repeat must remind you of the last

We need an account that explains the inverted U and the moving peak. The leading cognitive account is about retrieval.

The study-phase retrieval hypothesis says a repetition only helps if it reminds you of the earlier encounter. Retrieving a memory that is a bit harder to reach strengthens it more, as long as you succeed. So two forces compete. Longer gaps make the reminding harder, which helps. But longer gaps also make reminding more likely to fail, which hurts. At some point the failures win: that is the inverted U.

A rival account, encoding variability, says spaced repetitions are stored with different context cues, giving more routes to the memory later. The evidence goes against it. The spacing effect appears only for items people recognise when they are repeated. Repeating after a long gap in the same context works better than in a different one. And brain activity patterns were more similar across repetitions for items later remembered (Xue et al.).

Retrieval also explains the testing effect: practice tests after long gaps beat restudying, especially for long retention intervals. And it links to reconsolidation. Nader and colleagues reminded rats of a feared tone and immediately blocked protein synthesis; only the reminded memory was impaired. Reactivating a memory seems to make it changeable again, so it can be updated or strengthened. The authors stress that this needs much more research, especially in humans.

⚠ The trap✗ Encoding variability is the best explanation because spaced items are stored with more varied cues.✓ The review argues the evidence favours study-phase retrieval: same-context repetition works better, and remembered items show more similar brain patterns.

Model answer: It supports study-phase retrieval (similar encoding makes reminding easier) and contradicts encoding variability (which predicts that different contexts help).

STEP8
Implications for education

Three rules, two traps, one smart schedule

❓ This step answers: How can schools and students use spacing?

We now know that spacing works and why. The last part of the article turns this into advice. Try the planner game while you read.

Three rules follow from the research. First, avoid massed practice. Second, make the gaps between practice opportunities as long as possible. Third, include formal or informal practice tests, because effortful retrieval makes memories last. Two traps must be avoided: easy initial learning does not predict retention, and knowledge you have learned still needs review.

For teachers, this mostly means a different calendar, not new methods. Summarize the previous lesson at the start of the next class, and put a short quiz before the summary. Replace one big end-of-term review with several shorter ones. Use cumulative quizzes that mix older and newer material, which also discourages cramming. Put some older material into each homework assignment.

For learners: study the same material several times with long gaps, and test yourself after a delay, not right after studying, or you will overestimate what you remember. Spacing software can help: a correct answer lengthens the next gap, an error shortens it. When forgetting is fast, as in young learners or with low working memory, use an expanding schedule: short gaps first, then longer ones. Two exceptions: massing can help early with complex material, and cramming suits only a test that is very close.

⚠ The trap✗ Ending each lesson with a longer summary is the best way to apply spacing.✓ An immediate summary is massed repetition. A quiz on the previous lesson at the start of the next class spaces the repetition and adds retrieval.

GOAL1
Learning goal 1 · Chi & Wylie (2014)

What are the four modes of engagement in ICAP, and how do they impact learning outcomes?

↳ Why it matters: This is the core of ICAP and the most likely exam topic: name the modes, the processes and the predicted order.
← Builds on: Article 1, steps 3 to 6.
🧪 Try it: tap each mode and watch the chain change: what you see the student do → what happens in their head → what they can do afterwards.
✋ Answer out loud first. A full answer names: (1) the four modes with one example each, (2) the mental process behind each one, (3) the prediction I > C > A > P, and (4) one study that supports it.

ICAP (Chi & Wylie, 2014) sorts engagement by students' overt behaviour into four modes. Passive: receiving information without doing anything else (listening, reading). Active: physically manipulating the material in a way that focuses attention (underlining, copying, pausing a video). Constructive: generating outputs that go beyond the material (self-explaining, concept maps, notes in your own words). Interactive: a dialogue in which both partners are constructive and take frequent turns.

Each mode is linked to a knowledge-change process: storing (isolated, "inert" knowledge), integrating with activated prior knowledge, inferring new knowledge, and co-inferring with a partner. These lead to recall, apply, transfer and co-create, or minimal, shallow, deep and deepest understanding. Hence the ICAP hypothesis: I > C > A > P. Menekse et al. (2013) found gains of roughly 8 to 10% per mode in one study with all four modes.

💬 In plain words: the more you have to produce, and the more you build on a partner's ideas, the deeper you learn.
Receive → Manipulate → Generate → Dialogue: "listen, touch, make, talk". And the outcomes climb the same way: recall → apply → transfer → co-create.
✗ "Constructive means the student works hard."✓ Constructive means the student produces something new beyond the material. Copying a long text by hand is effortful but only active.
Exam stems often describe a behaviour and ask for the mode, or ask which process (store, integrate, infer, co-infer) or outcome belongs to a mode. Learn the four rows of Table 2 as one chain.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSMenekse et al. (2013)

Read / highlight / interpret a graph / interpret it with a peer. Only study with all four modes.

In plain words: each step up the ladder added about 8 to 10% on the posttest.
SUPPORTSColeman et al. (1997)

Listening, summarizing, explaining. Near and far transfer rose in that order.

In plain words: explaining beat summarizing, which beat listening.
SUPPORTSGobert & Clement (1999)

Drawing diagrams > writing summaries > only reading, on spatial and causal knowledge.

In plain words: making a picture of plate tectonics taught more than summarizing it.
SHOWSBauer & Koedinger (2007)

Highlighting vs copy-paste notes: no difference.

In plain words: two activities in the same mode give the same learning, as ICAP predicts.

Learning goal 2 · Chi & Wylie (2014)

How can you design activities to move students from passive to interactive engagement?

↳ Why it matters: It turns the theory into design: the skill your app case and the tutorial ask for.
← Builds on: Goal 1 (the modes) and the intended-versus-enacted rule.
🧪 Try it: slide the same lecture up the ladder and see what students must produce at each rung. Then test whether a pair activity is truly interactive.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name: (1) the key design lever (what students produce), (2) one upgrade for each step from passive to interactive, (3) the two checks that stop a design from slipping back down.

Change what students must produce, not the topic or the delivery (task and mode are independent). From a lecture: add a guided-notes sheet (active), then ask students to build a concept map or explain in their own words (constructive), then have pairs build or defend the map together, asking each other for justifications (interactive). In general: replace selecting with generating (generate link labels instead of choosing them; draw molecules instead of picking images), use prompts that need more than yes or no, and give incomplete worked examples.

Two design checks. (1) Intended vs enacted: a summary task can be done as copy-and-delete, so evaluate the products. (2) Group work is only interactive if the task is generative and both partners contribute with turn taking (joint, not individual, dialogue).

💬 In plain words: listen → fill in → build → build together and challenge each other.
You don't need a new lesson to raise engagement. Keep the content, change the job: from receiving it, to marking it, to making something with it, to making something with someone else.
✗ "Put them in groups and it becomes interactive."✓ Zhang & Linn: pairs who only selected images were active. Pairs who drew molecules were interactive. The task must be generative, and both must contribute.
Case questions: "As an instructional designer, which change raises engagement to the constructive level?" Find the option where students generate. Menus, copying, pausing and highlighting are all active.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSZhang & Linn (2013)

Pairs drawing molecules > pairs selecting images.

In plain words: generating together beats choosing together.
SUPPORTSKramarski & Dudai (2009)

Prompts to respond to and modify a friend's explanation > self-explain prompts only.

In plain words: reacting to each other's thinking adds to explaining alone.
SUPPORTSRussell et al. (1983)

Notes on a partial, scaffolded outline > a near-verbatim handout.

In plain words: a small active job beats being handed everything.
SHOWSTeacher workshops

Teachers could understand ICAP and apply it when designing new lessons.

In plain words: the framework is usable in practice (initial results).

Learning goal 3 · Chi & Wylie (2014)

What are the limitations of the ICAP framework?

↳ Why it matters: Exams love "which is NOT a limitation" and "why was there no difference" questions.
← Builds on: Goals 1 and 2: you can only see the limits once you know the predictions.
🧪 Try it: switch on a situation and watch what happens to the predicted staircase of results.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name the three overriding factors, each with its example study, and at least two built-in caveats.

Three factors can override the hypothesis. Assessment: shallow tests (recall, easy multiple-choice) cannot detect differences (Aleven & Koedinger, 2002; easy items in Menekse et al.). Domain: topics with arbitrary rules (English articles, Wylie, 2011) or so unfamiliar that learners have no schema to build on (emergence). Task differences within a mode: two interactive tasks can differ (inventing beat comparing and contrasting).

Built-in caveats: the knowledge-change processes are hypothetical and untested; overt behaviour is only a proxy (covert processing, intended vs enacted), so predictions hold with greater probability, not certainty; boundaries between modes are fuzzy (problem solving); ICAP covers only one aspect of learning (what students do).

💬 In plain words: ICAP is a good first cut, but the test, the topic and the exact task still matter, and what students do on the outside doesn't always match what happens inside.
T-T-T: the Test, the Topic, the Task. Plus two "inside" caveats: hidden processes and hidden thinking.
✗ "Within-mode differences prove ICAP wrong."✓ ICAP predicts differences across modes. Inventing and comparing both still beat lower modes.
"Which is NOT a limitation" questions: the false options often say ICAP was never tested in classrooms (it was), or that passive learning gives no learning at all (it gives recall).

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

LIMIT · TESTAleven & Koedinger (2002)

Explaining helped on hard-to-guess and transfer problems, not on easy-to-guess items.

In plain words: easy questions hide deep learning.
LIMIT · TOPICWylie (2011)

Choosing an article plus its rule = choosing the article alone.

In plain words: there is no "why" to reason out for a / an / the.
LIMIT · TOPICChi, Roscoe et al. (2012)

Prompts taught facts about molecules, not emergent between-level causality.

In plain words: you can't build on a schema you don't have.
LIMIT · TASKChi, Dohmen et al. (2012)

Invent > compare and contrast, both interactive. 100% vs 10% made triple-feature statements.

In plain words: the task's structure steers how students think.

Learning goal 4 · Brod (2021)

What are Generative Learning Strategies, and how do they differ from passive learning methods?

↳ Why it matters: It defines the six strategies the rest of Article 2 compares, and links them to ICAP.
← Builds on: Goal 1: generative = ICAP's constructive mode.
🧪 Try it: tap any activity on the line. Where does it sit between "take it in", "mark it" and "make something new"?
Tap a dot on the line.
✋ Answer out loud first. Give: (1) Brod's definition, (2) the mechanism (prior knowledge + integration), (3) the six strategies, (4) how they differ from passive and from merely active methods, (5) the link to ICAP.

GLSs are activities that prompt learners to produce something meaningful that goes beyond the information provided (Brod, 2021). Learners must activate prior knowledge and link it to the new information, which supports integration into existing knowledge. They come from the constructivist view of learning. The six reviewed: concept mapping, explaining, predicting, questioning, practice testing and drawing.

They differ from passive methods (listening, rereading), and also from merely active ones (highlighting, paraphrasing), because those reproduce the material without adding content. Brod equates GLSs with Chi's constructive mode. Practice testing counts as generative even when the answer is wrong.

💬 In plain words: you don't just take it in or mark it, you make something new with it.
If you can do it with a highlighter, it isn't generative. If you need a blank page, it probably is.
✗ "A practice test only counts as generative if you get the answer right."✓ It counts independent of correctness. Even a failed retrieval attempt helps structure knowledge.
Watch "summarizing": it is generative only if the learner enriches the text. A summary that condenses or paraphrases is not. Brod left summarizing out of the review for exactly this reason.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSSchroeder et al. (2018)

Self-generated concept maps: 0.72 SDs. Self-generating beats studying a provided map.

In plain words: making the map teaches more than reading one.
SUPPORTSRowland (2014)

Practice testing vs restudy: 0.50 SDs.

In plain words: pulling it out of memory beats reading it again.
SUPPORTSFiorella & Zhang (2018)

Drawing vs reading only: 0.46 SDs comprehension, 0.75 transfer.

In plain words: turning text into a picture helps, for adults especially.
SHOWSChi (2009) link

Brod: his distinction is "highly similar" to Chi's active vs constructive.

In plain words: generative = constructive.

Learning goal 5 · Brod (2021)

How do developmental processes like cognitive and metacognitive growth influence GLS effectiveness across age groups?

↳ Why it matters: It explains why the same strategy can work for a student and fail for a child.
← Builds on: Goal 4 (what GLSs are) plus the development step of Article 2.
🧪 Try it: move the age slider. Watch the three growing capacities, and see which strategies "unlock" at each school level.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name: (1) the three developmental processes, (2) the three deficiencies, (3) why strategies differ in when they start working (prerequisites), (4) one supporting study.

Strategy use grows with three processes. Knowledge: a richer semantic network to link to, and freed capacity. Cognitive capacity: executive functions (working memory, inhibition, shifting), tied to the maturing prefrontal cortex, which underlie reasoning and revising mental models. Metacognition: monitoring and control, which mature late. Children show production, mediation and utilization deficiencies.

So GLSs generally work better with age; around age 10 abstract self-reflection emerges. Strategies differ because their prerequisites differ. Predicting and testing mostly need effortful retrieval and basic feedback processing, so they work in lower elementary school. Questioning and drawing need prior knowledge and metacognitive monitoring to construct a useful question or drawing, so they are largely ineffective until secondary school (Table 1). Breitwieser & Brod (2020): children benefited more from predictions than examples, linked to reasoning ability.

💬 In plain words: a strategy only works once the brain skills it needs have grown. Guessing needs little, drawing and asking good questions need a lot.
"Test and guess work early. Draw and ask come late." And the three things that grow: know, control, reflect.
✗ "Age itself explains it."✓ Brod: age is not an explanatory variable. Children struggle because they typically lack the prerequisites. Age is only a rough proxy.
Know which capacity matters for which strategy: feedback processing (predicting, testing), reasoning (explaining, examples), metacognitive monitoring (questioning, drawing).

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSBreitwieser & Brod (2020)

Children: predictions > examples. Students: equal. The more adult-like, the higher the reasoning score.

In plain words: the strategy needing reasoning only works once reasoning has grown.
SUPPORTSVan Meter et al. (2006)

Sixth graders benefited from drawing; fourth graders did not, even with high support.

In plain words: drawing needs skills that 9-year-olds don't have yet.
SUPPORTSDenner & Rickards (1987)

Grades 5, 8, 11: self-generated questions improved with age; only 11th graders mostly targeted main ideas.

In plain words: good questions need metacognition.
SHOWSLipowski et al. (2014)

Testing beat restudy in 1st and 3rd graders.

In plain words: retrieval works early.

Learning goal 6 · Brod (2021)

How can GLS techniques (e.g., concept mapping) be adapted for different learners?

↳ Why it matters: It tells you what to do when a strategy is too hard for a learner: the practical half of Article 2.
← Builds on: Goal 5: support makes up for missing prerequisites.
🧪 Try it: pick a learner. The concept map changes to show what you give them (solid) and what they must generate (dashed, gold).
✋ Answer out loud first. Explain: (1) the principle (match support to prerequisites), (2) concept-map adaptations for two age groups with their studies, (3) one support for drawing or questioning, (4) the limit of support.

Match the amount of support to the learner's prerequisites. Younger or weaker learners get part of the material and metacognitive prompts. Concept mapping: high-school students profited more from a high-coherence map where they only label existing lines, university students from a low-coherence map where they create and label lines (Gurlitt & Renkl, 2008). Elementary children who filled in parts of a map did better than those who built the whole map, which produced poor maps and no benefit (Karpicke et al., 2014). Drawing: provide background parts (Schmeck et al., 2014) or prompt learners to compare their drawing with an illustration (Van Meter et al., 2006). Questioning: give question stems (King, 1994).

The limit: if learners are given everything and need no effortful retrieval, the task is no longer generative. In the long run, choose strategies by individual prerequisites, not just age.

💬 In plain words: give younger learners a scaffold, not the answer.
"Scaffold, not answer." Younger = more given, but always something left to generate.
Giving part of the work frees up a child's limited working memory for the thinking part. But hand over the whole thing and there's no thinking left to do.
Coherence is the trap word: high-coherence map = lines already drawn (more support, high school). Low-coherence = learners create the lines (less support, university).

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSGurlitt & Renkl (2008)

Map coherence × age group interaction.

In plain words: students got more from making the links, high-schoolers from labelling given ones.
SUPPORTSKarpicke et al. (2014)

Whole map alone: poor maps, no benefit. Filling in parts: better than both no-support and no-map groups.

In plain words: children need half a map, not a blank page.
SUPPORTSVan Meter (2001; 2006)

Comparing one's drawing with a provided illustration: more accurate drawings, more learning, better self-monitoring.

In plain words: a check-your-drawing prompt does the monitoring children can't yet do.
MIXEDKing (1994)

Question stems helped 4th–5th graders; unguided questioning did not.

In plain words: stems help, but Brod asks whether filling in stems is still real generation.

Learning goal 7 · Gerbier & Toppino (2015)

What is distributed practice, and why is it effective for long-term learning?

↳ Why it matters: It answers the "when" question of the theme and carries the mechanism questions.
← Builds on: Practice testing from goal 4, now combined with timing.
🧪 Try it: compare massed and spaced memory over time, then switch on each mechanism to see where it works.
✋ Answer out loud first. Give: (1) the definition with "same total time", (2) one number, (3) three mechanisms, (4) why the inverted U matters for choosing between them.

Distributed practice means repeating material in study sessions separated by time, instead of massing the same total time in immediate succession. Spacing improves long-term retention for almost all materials, ages and species (Gerbier & Toppino, 2015). Example: three 10-minute sessions over 3 days gave 35% better retention after four days than one 30-minute session (Bloom & Shuell).

Why it works: (1) Deficient processing: an immediate repetition gets less processing (smaller pupil dilation; repetition suppression in fMRI). (2) Synaptic plasticity: spaced stimulation produces more lasting LTP and consolidation, and over day-long gaps sleep and systems consolidation strengthen gist. (3) Study-phase retrieval: a repetition must remind you of the earlier encounter, and harder but successful retrieval strengthens memory and slows forgetting. This explains the inverted U and why the optimal lag grows with the retention interval. Reconsolidation may link retrieval to synaptic change.

💬 In plain words: a repeat that comes a bit later makes your brain work to pull the memory back, and that effort makes it last.
"Space for the date you need it." Three reasons, short to long: too-soon repeats get less effort, synapses need time to build, and each repeat must remind you.
✗ "Deficient processing explains the whole spacing effect."✓ It cannot explain why memory falls again when gaps are too long, or why the best gap depends on the retention interval. Study-phase retrieval can.
Match finding → mechanism: pupil dilation / repetition suppression → deficient processing; LTP with anisomycin → synaptic plasticity; inverted U, same-context advantage → study-phase retrieval; reminder + drug → reconsolidation.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSBloom & Shuell

One 30-min session vs three 10-min sessions over 3 days: 35% better after four days.

In plain words: same time, better spread, more remembered.
SUPPORTSXue et al.

Spaced faces: less repetition suppression in fusiform cortex, better recognition.

In plain words: the brain works harder on a later repeat.
SUPPORTSScharf et al.

Spaced (5 min) vs massed (20 s) stimulations: larger LTP, reduced by anisomycin.

In plain words: spacing builds stronger, protein-based synapse changes.
SUPPORTSCepeda et al. (Exp. 2)

Gaps of 0 to 168 days, test after 168 days: 28-day gap best.

In plain words: there is a best gap, and it depends on when you need it.

GOAL8
Learning goal 8 · Gerbier & Toppino (2015)

How can distributed practice be applied in educational settings?

↳ Why it matters: It is the applied half of Article 3 and feeds directly into the app case.
← Builds on: Goal 7: the rules follow from why spacing works.
🧪 Try it: you plan an 8-week course. Switch on the article's recommendations and watch the timeline and the retention meter.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name: (1) the three rules, (2) three teacher moves, (3) two learner moves, (4) the two traps and one exception.

Three rules: avoid massing, keep the intervals between practice opportunities as long as possible, and include practice tests. Teachers: summarize the previous day's lesson at the start of the next class, with a quiz before the summary; use several short reviews instead of one end-of-term review; give cumulative quizzes; put older material in homework; add follow-up reviews after compressed courses. Learners: plan several spaced sessions, self-test after a delay, use adaptive spacing software. Use an expanding schedule when forgetting is fast (young learners, low working memory, high interference).

Avoid two traps: easy initial learning ≠ good retention, and learned material still needs review. Exceptions: massing can help early with complex material; gaps can be too long; cramming suits only a very short retention interval.

💬 In plain words: spread it out, quiz yourself, keep coming back, and don't trust that "I've got it" feeling.
"Space it, test it, revisit it." And two traps: easy ≠ learned, learned ≠ done.
Teachers don't need new methods, just a new calendar: start class with a quiz on last time, keep tests cumulative, and slip old topics into homework.
Case options to reject: "one intensive review week before the exam" (massed), "longer summary at the end of the lesson" (immediate repetition), "homework only on today's lesson" (no spacing).

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSClassroom studies

Spaced practice and tests helped in vocabulary, statistics, history, intro psychology, phonics, middle-school science, a web course.

In plain words: it works outside the lab too.
SUPPORTSRetrieval attempts

Learning is better after a retrieval attempt followed by the correct answer than with no attempt.

In plain words: quiz first, even if they get it wrong.
CAUTIONStudents' beliefs

Students prefer massing, and may still predict massing works better after experiencing spacing.

In plain words: spacing feels worse while it works better, so build it into the schedule.
LIMITApplied research

Basic research identifies the variables, but not how to apply them in each specific context.

In plain words: teachers must use their own ingenuity for now.
