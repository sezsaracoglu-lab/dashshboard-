Meeting 1 · Learning strategies · Prepared for Sezen

How do you study so it actually sticks?

This theme reads three articles that each answer one part of that question. Your case: you are the designer of a learning tool, building an app that teaches students how to study. Each article gives you one design rule.

The thread that runs through all three: learning sticks when the learner does mental work. You make something new (ICAP, generative strategies) or pull it back out of memory after a gap (testing, spacing).

The articles are linked. Brod says outright that generative strategies are Chi's constructive mode. Practice testing shows up in Brod (a generative strategy) and in Gerbier & Toppino (the testing effect).

All three are review articles, not single experiments. So "the method" means: what evidence do they collect, and how do they judge it? Chi & Wylie re-classify conditions of old studies into ICAP modes. Brod sorts meta-analyses and age-comparison studies by grade level. Gerbier & Toppino bring together behavioural, brain-imaging and cellular studies. Each walk-through has a step called "How they test it".

Article 1 of 3 · The HOW

The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active Learning Outcomes

Chi, M. T. H., & Wylie, R. (2014). Educational Psychologist, 49(4), 219–243.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what kind of article is it?

Two learning scientists who wanted a usable definition

Authors
Michelene T. H. Chi and Ruth Wylie
Where
Mary Lou Fulton Teachers College, Arizona State University
Journal
Educational Psychologist (APA Division 15), 2014
Type
Theoretical framework, backed by a review of lab and classroom studies
Before
An earlier version (Chi, 2009) was called DOLA: Differentiated Overt Learning Activities
Two researchers noticed that everyone says "make students active", but nobody says what that means. They wrote a framework that sorts learning activities by what you can see students doing, and predicts how much they learn.
Know that ICAP is a framework + hypothesis built on a review. Exam options that say "Chi and Wylie ran an experiment showing..." are about the one study from their lab (Menekse et al., 2013). Menekse et al. is the only study run specifically to test all four modes; most other evidence is re-coded from earlier studies, some by the authors' own group.
ICAP spells the ranking top-down: Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive.
STEP2
What problem does the article try to solve?

"Be active" is good advice that tells a teacher nothing

Most research on engagement is about motivation (wanting to take part), behaviour (attending class, doing homework) or emotion (liking school). Chi and Wylie look at cognitive engagement instead: how much mental work you can see a student doing while learning.

The article's questions: Can we define engagement by overt behaviour (what you can see)? Can we sort it into a few modes? Does each mode predict a different level of learning?

"Although 'active learning' is a great idea for overcoming 'passive learning,' we have identified three concrete practical challenges that teachers may face when developing lessons that promote 'active learning.'"
Learning activity = the task the teacher chooses (reading, solving problems, watching a video). Engagement activity = how the student engages with it, seen in overt behaviour (for example summarizing after each paragraph). Teachers can design tasks that invite more engagement, for example a prompt to summarize.
✗ "ICAP is about motivation: engaged students are students who want to learn."✓ ICAP sets motivational, behavioural and emotional engagement aside. It is about cognitive engagement measured through small, visible behaviours.
STEP3
Key concepts and definitions · Learning goal 1

Four modes, four mental processes, four outcomes

👆 Tap a step of the staircase to see that mode's full row from the article's Table 2.
Tap a mode. The hypothesis: learning increases from Passive to Interactive because each higher mode involves more mental processing.
Two criteria, both needed: (a) both partners' utterances are mainly constructive, and (b) there is enough turn taking. If one partner dominates and the other just says "uh-huh", it is an individual dialogue: the speaker is constructive, the listener only active. Only a joint dialogue is truly interactive. Partners can be peers, teachers, parents or a computer agent that responds in a content-relevant way.
ICAP judges by overt (visible) behaviour because that is what a teacher can see and design for. A student who looks passive could be covertly self-explaining. Chi and Wylie admit this. They treat overt behaviour as the best non-invasive measure, which makes the prediction a matter of greater probability, not certainty.
✗ "Group work is interactive." / "A computer system that asks for a response is interactive."✓ Interactive means a constructive dialogue with turn taking. Two students copying each other's homework only interact actively. A student picking an answer from a menu in a so-called interactive system is only active.
Receive → Manipulate → Generate → Dialogue. In your head: "listen, touch, make, talk".

STEP5
Research method

They re-label other people's experiments

ICAP is conceptually derived, so it needs empirical support. Apart from one study from their own lab, Chi and Wylie reinterpret existing studies. They code every condition as P, A, C or I and check whether the outcomes fall in the predicted order.

The new move compared with Chi (2009): they compare the same activity done in different modes, for example note taking done actively versus constructively.

Ask one question about each condition: does the learner produce something that goes beyond the material given? No overt action → passive. Physical action that focuses attention, nothing new → active. New ideas beyond the text → constructive. Constructive dialogue with turn taking → interactive. Classroom interventions that mix activities are coded by their highest mode.
An exam can describe a study and ask for its ICAP modes. Watch the classic traps: a whole-class discussion counts as passive for most students (only a few talk). A summary that stays close to the text counts as active (Coleman et al. found many summaries did not go beyond it).
GAME · BE CHI & WYLIE

Code the conditions of real studies

These conditions all come from studies in the article. Give each one an ICAP mode, the way the authors coded it.

STEP6
Results · Learning goal 1

Learning climbs about 8 to 10% with each mode

PREDICT, THEN REVEAL

Menekse et al. (2013): materials science, all four modes

Passive: read a text. Active: read and highlight important sentences. Constructive: interpret a graph of the same information, without the text. Interactive: interpret the graph with a peer. Everyone had a pretest and a posttest. First guess: which condition had the highest posttest score?

Make your guess to reveal the graph.

Three modes: in Coleman et al., students took turns listening (P), summarizing (A) and explaining (C). Scores rose from listening to summarizing to explaining, on near and far transfer. Gobert & Clement found the same order: drawing diagrams > writing summaries > only reading.

Same mode, same result: highlighting versus copy-pasting notes made no difference (both active, Bauer & Koedinger). ICAP predicts exactly that.

"These results support the ICAP hypothesis and show that learning improves significantly at a rate of around 8% to 10% across each mode." Menekse et al.
SUPPORTS Underlining > reading (A>P). Free-form notes > cut-and-paste (C>A). Generating link labels in a concept map > selecting them (C>A, Yin et al.). Explaining sentences > repeating them (C>A, O'Reilly et al.). Collaboratively building concept maps > building them alone (I>C). Explaining with a partner > alone (I>C, Hausmann et al.).
SHOWS Same mode, no difference: two kinds of collaborative concept mapping (van Boxtel et al.); explaining own vs a puppet's misinterpretations, both constructive (Pillow et al.).
Zhang & Linn (2013): pairs drew molecules versus pairs selected static images. Both worked with a partner, but only drawing is generative. So this is interactive vs active, and drawing in pairs gave greater gains. In plain words: putting students together does not make an activity interactive. They must also create something.
STEP7
Caveats · Learning goal 3

When ICAP's prediction can fail

ICAP looks at one part of a learning situation: what students do. Other factors also shape learning, and three of them can override the hypothesis.

Even when two tasks in the same mode differ, ICAP still predicts both will beat a lower mode. Its claims are about differences across modes.

Chi, Dohmen et al. (2012): sixth graders learned projectile motion in pairs. Invent a method versus compare and contrast trajectories. Both interactive, yet inventing won. The worksheets show why: comparing focused students on single features ("they go at different speeds"). Inventing pushed them to connect features. Comparers made 3.0 single-feature statements on average, inventors none.
Wylie (2011): students chose the right English article, or the article plus the rule behind it. No difference. Either both tasks are just active selecting, or explaining doesn't help because the rules are human-made and can't be reasoned out.
✗ "A null result between a constructive and a passive condition disproves ICAP."✓ First check the test. If it only asks for recall, even passive learners can answer it. In plain words: a shallow test can't reveal deep learning.
"Which is NOT a limitation of ICAP?" Tempting wrong answer: "ICAP cannot be used by teachers." In fact the authors ran small teacher workshops, and teachers could understand and apply the framework.

STEP8
ICAP in practice · Learning goal 2

Explain, choose a control, evaluate, design

ICAP is also a tool, with four uses:

Constructivism: the same learner-centred idea, but ICAP sorts activities and outcomes more concretely. "Constructing" in ICAP means integrating and inferring, not discovering facts yourself.
Cognitive load theory: reduce the load of materials. ICAP often predicts the opposite: higher modes are more effortful and give more learning (McEldoon, 2014). The two agree when the extra effort is germane load.
Bloom (revised): Bloom is about ends (goals and assessment). ICAP is about means (how to get there). The two are complementary.
If you want students to go up the ladder, change what they have to produce. Listening becomes filling in, filling in becomes building, building becomes building together with real back-and-forth.

Starting point: students watch a 10-minute video on photosynthesis. At each level, pick the activity that really reaches the next mode. Watch for the traps.

STEP9
Conclusion and main take-aways

The article in one minute

"The ICAP hypothesis predicts that as activities move from passive to active to constructive to interactive, students undergo different knowledge-change processes and, as a result, learning will increase."
Take-awayIn plain words
Engagement can be defined by overt behaviour in 4 modes.Look at what the student visibly does, not at how motivated they seem.
Each mode has its own knowledge-change process: store, integrate, infer, co-infer.More mental work with the material changes your knowledge more deeply.
Predicted outcomes: recall, apply, transfer, co-create.Passive lets you repeat, interactive lets you invent.
Lab and classroom evidence supports I > C > A > P.The order holds for notes, concept maps and self-explaining.
It can fail with shallow tests, odd domains, task differences within a mode.Check the test and the topic before you blame the framework.
A tool to explain findings, pick controls, code outputs and design lessons.Useful for teachers and app designers.

Learning goals answered here:

Quick check

A student underlines key sentences but, in her head, explains each one to herself. What mode does ICAP assign, and why?

Active. ICAP codes the overt behaviour (underlining). The covert self-explaining is possible, which is why ICAP only claims "greater probability".

Two students talk about a text. One explains everything, the other mostly says "yes, ok". Who learns more according to ICAP?

The speaker. This is an individual dialogue: the speaker is constructive, the listener only active. Both would benefit only in a joint dialogue.

Why does ICAP make a prediction opposite to cognitive load theory?

Cognitive load theory says more load means less learning. ICAP says higher modes take more effort and give more learning. They agree when the effort is germane, as in self-explaining.

Author
Garvin Brod

Journal
Educational Psychology Review, 2021 (open access)
Type

Most "best study strategies" research was done with university students. Brod asks the obvious question: does what works for a 20-year-old also work for an 8-year-old? He reviews six strategies age group by age group.
Remember the exclusions. An option like "Brod found collaborative concept mapping most effective for children" is wrong twice: collaboration was excluded, and the review compares strategies by age, not by group work.
STEP2
What question does the article ask?

Are generative strategies equally effective at every age?

The opening line: "Teaching an 8-year-old is different from teaching a 16-year-old." Yet big reviews of learning strategies mostly rely on university students.

Two goals: (1) for what ages has each strategy been shown to work? (2) Are there age-related differences in how well it works?

"Its main purpose is to review for what ages the effectiveness of these strategies has been demonstrated and whether there are indications of age-related differences in their effectiveness."
✗ "The review asks which generative strategy is the best one."✓ It asks for whom each strategy works, by age. Earlier reviews (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Fiorella & Mayer) looked for the best strategy for everyone.
STEP3
Key concepts · Learning goal 4

Generative = you produce something beyond what you were given

Generative learning strategies (GLSs) are activities that make learners produce something meaningful that goes beyond the information provided. To do that you activate prior knowledge and link it to the new information. That is thought to help integrate the new information into what you already know.

GLSs come from the constructivist view: learning is active construction built on prior knowledge (Wittrock, 1985).

Passive methods (listening, rereading) and merely active ones (highlighting, paraphrasing) reproduce the material. GLSs make you add to it: a link, a reason, a guess, a picture. Brod says this is "highly similar" to Chi's active vs constructive distinction, so GLSs are instances of constructive activities.
The content does not have to be invented by you, only produced beyond what was given. So practice testing is a GLS, whether or not your answer is correct. A favourite exam detail.
If you can do it with a highlighter, it is not generative. If you need a blank page, it probably is.

STEP4
Development · Learning goal 5

Why children struggle with strategies: three deficiencies, three growth processes

Children use more strategies as they grow, and this drives much of their improvement in learning and memory. Strategies that rely on activating prior knowledge are especially hard for young children.

Three developmental processes explain the growth: more knowledge, more cognitive capacity (executive functions, linked to the maturing prefrontal cortex), and better metacognition.

Knowledge: age and world knowledge go together, at least in the first two decades. More knowledge gives a richer semantic network, so new material links more easily. It also frees capacity for elaboration (Bjorklund). But knowledge is not the only source: studies controlling for it still find age effects.
Executive functions: working memory and inhibition (plus shifting) underlie reasoning and testing and revising mental models. All GLSs need at least basic reasoning.
Metacognition: monitoring and control mature late. Using a strategy well means monitoring it and adapting it when needed.
Because children use strategies so poorly on their own, any GLS that does work for them could help them even more than it helps adults, who already use strategies spontaneously.
✗ "Older children learn better simply because they know more."✓ Knowledge matters a lot, but studies that controlled for prior knowledge still found age differences. Cognitive capacity plays a role too.

STEP5
Research method

Three questions asked of every strategy

A narrative review. For balance, Brod relies on meta-analyses whenever possible. Effect sizes are reported in SDs (standard deviations, Cohen's d: how many standard deviations better the strategy group did).

The strongest evidence for age differences comes from age-comparative studies: the same task given to different age groups.

0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large (a common rule of thumb). Watch the control condition: concept mapping is 1.05 SDs better than a passive control but only 0.48 SDs better than an active control such as summarizing. The same strategy looks stronger against a weaker comparison. Chi and Wylie make the same point about control conditions.
Many studies were run in real classrooms, so grade is what they report. Grades 4–5 get their own column because so many studies used them.
A meta-analysis that includes only published studies is likely to overestimate effects (the file-drawer problem). Brod says Table 1 likely overestimates effectiveness for exactly this reason.

STEP7
Discussion · Learning goal 5

Different strategies need different prerequisites

General development (knowledge, capacity, metacognition) explains why all GLSs work better with age. It does not explain why strategies differ from each other.

Brod's answer: each strategy has its own prerequisites. A strategy only works once its prerequisites have developed.

Breitwieser & Brod (2020): grade 4–5 children and university students learned numerical facts by generating either a prediction or an example first. Students did equally well with both. Children learned more from predicting. The more adult-like a child's pattern, the higher their reasoning score, and reasoning is a prerequisite for making useful examples.
SUPPORTS the idea that differences between strategies depend on prerequisites.
A guess only needs you to reach into memory. A good question or drawing needs you to know enough to see what matters, and to keep checking whether your picture matches the text. Children's monitoring is still growing.
Brod argues that age is not an explanatory variable. Eight-year-olds struggle because they typically lack certain prerequisites, not because they are eight. Age is only a rough proxy. The long-term goal: choose the best strategy for each individual learner.

How to adapt a GLS for younger or weaker learners: give them part of the material (a partly built map, background parts for a drawing) and metacognitive prompts ("compare your drawing with this illustration"). This frees capacity for the generative work.

The limit: if learners get everything and don't need effortful retrieval, the strategy is no longer generative.

1. Few age-comparative studies, so no strong conclusions about age differences.
2. Even fewer studies test the mechanisms (for example by manipulating prior knowledge).
3. Mostly published studies, so Table 1 likely overestimates effectiveness (file drawer).
4. Very few studies below grade 4: written tests are hard at that age, and GLSs need self-regulation that matures slowly.
5. Many verdicts rest on very few studies, so the table is tentative.
Gurlitt & Renkl (2008): university students profited more from a low-coherence map (create and label lines). High-school students profited more from a high-coherence map (only label existing lines). Karpicke et al. (2014): children who built whole maps made poor maps and gained nothing. Children who filled in parts did better than both no-support and no-map groups. Van Meter et al. (2006): sixth graders gained most when prompted to compare their drawing with a provided illustration.

Article 3 of 3 · The WHEN

The effect of distributed practice: Neuroscience, cognition, and education

Gerbier, E., & Toppino, T. C. (2015). Trends in Neuroscience and Education, 4, 49–59.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what kind of article is it?

A memory review that links three levels of evidence

Authors
Emilie Gerbier and Thomas C. Toppino
Where
Université Lumière Lyon 2 (France) and Villanova University (USA)
Journal
Trends in Neuroscience and Education, 2015
Type

More than 100 years of research say spreading out your study works. These authors collect the evidence from behaviour, brain scans and cells, explain why it works, and tell teachers and students what to do with it.
This review carries the mechanism questions: deficient processing, study-phase retrieval, encoding variability, consolidation, reconsolidation. Expect "Which account explains finding X?"
STEP2
What question does the article ask?

One week until your history exam. How do you schedule it?

YOUR FIRST INSTINCT

The article opens with this exact dilemma. What would you do?

You must learn a lesson on the French Revolution. The exam is in seven days, and you have a fixed amount of study time.

Pick one. We come back to it at the end of the article.

The article's questions: How should study time be scheduled to maximise learning and long-term memory? Why does distributed practice work (behaviour, brain, cells)? And why is something so well established still under-used? The authors suggest it is counter-intuitive.

"It is best to study something by scheduling relatively short study sessions that can be repeated after an appropriate period of time rather than by devoting the same total amount of time to a single study session or to a number of repeated study sessions that occur in immediate succession."
Massed study feels easier and more successful, and a long gap makes you feel you have forgotten a lot. Students prefer massing, and even after experiencing the spacing benefit some still predict massing will work better.
STEP3
Key concepts · Learning goal 7

Same total time, spread out: the spacing effect

Distributed (spaced) practice: repetitions of the same material separated by time. Massed practice: the repetitions come right after each other. The advantage of spacing is the spacing effect. Two spaced presentations can be twice as effective as two massed ones.

It works for words, word pairs, faces, pictures, texts, motor skills, grammar and maths problems. It works from 5-month-old infants to the elderly, and in fruit flies, sea slugs (aplysia), bees and rodents.

Lag (inter-study interval) = the time between study sessions. Retention interval = the time between the last study session and the test. The best lag depends on the retention interval (step 6).
An effect found in almost every species suggests a fundamental principle of memory, shaped by evolution. It calls for a very general mechanism. Example: fruit flies taught to avoid an odour (paired with shocks) kept avoiding it longer after 15-minute spaced exposures than after massed ones.
The testing effect: retrieving information (a practice test) strengthens memory more than restudying. It is the second tool the article recommends, and the same practice testing Brod calls a generative strategy.
✗ "Spaced practice works because you spend more time studying."✓ Total study time is held equal. Only the scheduling changes. In plain words: same hours, better spread, longer memory.

STEP5
Mechanisms · Learning goal 7

A repetition that comes too soon gets less processing

Deficient processing hypothesis: an immediately repeated item gets less processing the second time, so it is encoded less well.

In the brain this shows up as repetition suppression: the response to a repetition is weaker than to the first presentation, strongest after a short lag, fading as the lag grows. Xue et al.: spaced faces were recognised better and showed less suppression in the fusiform cortex.

SUPPORTS Pupil dilation (a sign of effort) is smaller for the second massed presentation. Reaction times to a tone are shorter during massed repetitions (less attention). Learners who set their own study time spend less on the second massed presentation. Part of it is voluntary (it feels familiar), but part is involuntary: the effect survives incentives, incidental learning and very young children.
Words seen twice, 2 minutes or 25 hours apart, with a surprise recognition test 48 hours later. Second presentations were faster (priming) and showed less activation in left inferior prefrontal and left fusiform cortex, more so after 2 minutes. Recognition was better in the 25-hour condition. More priming and suppression went with poorer recognition. But correlation is not causation, and Stark et al. replicated the overall results without the correlations.
Learning needs synaptic plasticity, which becomes stable through consolidation: synaptic (minutes to hours) and systems (days to years, e.g. hippocampus → neocortex). LTP (long-term potentiation) has an early phase without protein synthesis and a late phase with protein synthesis and gene expression. Spaced stimulation gives more LTP (Scharf et al.: 5 min vs 20 s apart, reduced by the protein-synthesis inhibitor anisomycin). Spacing also grows lasting dendrites (Wu et al.) and helps new hippocampal neurons survive (Sisti et al.).
Deficient processing cannot explain why memory gets worse when the lag is too long, or why the best lag depends on the retention interval. It predicts performance that keeps rising (or levels off) as lag increases. So it is only part of the story (step 7).

Inverted U: increasing the lag helps up to an optimum, then memory declines. Cepeda et al. (Exp. 2) separated two sessions by 0, 1, 7, 28, 84 or 168 days and tested 168 days later: 28 days was best. With a 10-day retention interval the best lag was 1 day (Exp. 1).

"Thus, the general picture is that if you want to retain your history lesson longer, you will have to space the repetitions more. However, if you target short-term success and not long-term retention, short repetition intervals may be more effective."
After 24 hours: deficient processing barely matters, more is forgotten in between, synaptic consolidation is complete, and there is sleep, which helps consolidation. Systems consolidation integrates new information with prior knowledge. So a day-later repetition re-encodes the durable gist rather than shallow cues such as the font or where it was on the page.
"Space for the date you need it." The longer you need to remember, the bigger the gaps.

STEP7
Competing explanations

A repetition must remind you of the first time

Study-phase retrieval hypothesis (the leading cognitive account): a repetition only helps if it reminds you of the earlier encounter. A harder but successful reminding helps more and may slow forgetting. Too long a gap and the reminding fails, which gives the inverted U.

Rival: encoding variability (Glenberg's component-levels theory): spaced repetitions are encoded in different contexts, which gives more retrieval routes.

AGAINST encoding variability (1) The spacing effect only appears for items recognised when repeated. (2) Repeating after a long lag in the same context beats a different context. (3) Recall after spacing exceeds the maximum possible if repetitions were encoded independently. (4) Xue et al.: brain activity patterns were more similar across repetitions for items later remembered.
Practice tests (without feedback) after long lags beat restudy, mainly at long retention intervals, which suggests retrieval slows forgetting. Retrieval is more beneficial when it is harder: after a longer interval, by recall rather than recognition, with fewer cues. Modigliani: final recall was an inverted-U function of spacing. Items failed on the practice test stayed poor, while recall of successfully retrieved items rose with spacing.
Reactivating a consolidated memory makes it labile (changeable) again, and it must be reconsolidated. Nader et al.: rats reminded of a feared tone and then given anisomycin immediately lost the memory. Those given it hours later, or with no reminder, did not. In humans: Hupbach et al. found that a reminder of list 1 before learning list 2 led to list-2 intrusions. Reconsolidation may update and strengthen memories, a possible link between retrieval and synaptic plasticity. Caution: not all studies find it, and there is little human research.

STEP8
Implications for education · Learning goal 8

Three rules, two traps, one smart schedule

Three recommendations: (1) avoid massed practice; (2) make the gaps between practice opportunities as long as possible; (3) include formal or informal practice tests, which require effortful retrieval.

Two traps: easy initial learning is not a good predictor of retention, and knowledge you have acquired still needs review.

Summarize the previous day's lesson at the start of the next class, ideally with a quiz before the summary. Use several shorter review sessions instead of one end-of-semester review. Give cumulative quizzes and tests (recent and older material). Put some older material in each homework assignment. Infrequent tests encourage cramming. Compressed courses (summer classes, immersion) need follow-up reviews weeks or months later.
Study the same material several times with long gaps, and add self-testing. Test yourself after a delay, not straight after studying, or you will overestimate what you'll remember. Spacing software can help: a correct answer lengthens the next interval, an error shortens it. Retrieval attempts help even when the answer is wrong, if you then study the correct answer.
Massed practice can help early in learning complex material, to get the initial encoding right. Gaps can be too long: if the item is forgotten when repeated, the repetition helps little. And if a student only cares about tomorrow's test, cramming can be reasonable, because massing works best for very short retention intervals.

Take-awayIn plain words

Quick check

Why can't deficient processing explain the whole spacing effect?

It predicts that memory keeps improving (or levels off) as lag increases. It cannot explain the decline at long lags, or why the optimal lag depends on the retention interval. Study-phase retrieval can.

A student self-tests right after studying, gets 95% and stops. What is the problem?

Testing soon after studying inflates performance, so she overestimates what she will remember. Self-tests should come after an extended delay.

GOAL1
Learning goal 1 · Chi & Wylie (2014)

What are the four modes of engagement in ICAP, and how do they impact learning outcomes?

🧪 Try it: tap each mode and watch the chain change: what you see the student do → what happens in their head → what they can do afterwards.
✋ Answer out loud first. A full answer names: (1) the four modes with one example each, (2) the mental process behind each one, (3) the prediction I > C > A > P, and (4) one study that supports it.

ICAP (Chi & Wylie, 2014) sorts engagement by students' overt behaviour into four modes. Passive: receiving information without doing anything else (listening, reading). Active: physically manipulating the material in a way that focuses attention (underlining, copying, pausing a video). Constructive: generating outputs that go beyond the material (self-explaining, concept maps, notes in your own words). Interactive: a dialogue in which both partners are constructive and take frequent turns.

Each mode is linked to a knowledge-change process: storing (isolated, "inert" knowledge), integrating with activated prior knowledge, inferring new knowledge, and co-inferring with a partner. These lead to recall, apply, transfer and co-create, or minimal, shallow, deep and deepest understanding. Hence the ICAP hypothesis: I > C > A > P. Menekse et al. (2013) found gains of roughly 8 to 10% per mode in one study with all four modes.

💬 In plain words: the more you have to produce, and the more you build on a partner's ideas, the deeper you learn.
Receive → Manipulate → Generate → Dialogue: "listen, touch, make, talk". And the outcomes climb the same way: recall → apply → transfer → co-create.
✗ "Constructive means the student works hard."✓ Constructive means the student produces something new beyond the material. Copying a long text by hand is effortful but only active.
Exam stems often describe a behaviour and ask for the mode, or ask which process (store, integrate, infer, co-infer) or outcome belongs to a mode. Learn the four rows of Table 2 as one chain.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSMenekse et al. (2013)

Read / highlight / interpret a graph / interpret it with a peer. Only study with all four modes.

In plain words: each step up the ladder added about 8 to 10% on the posttest.
SUPPORTSColeman et al. (1997)

Listening, summarizing, explaining. Near and far transfer rose in that order.

In plain words: explaining beat summarizing, which beat listening.
SUPPORTSGobert & Clement (1999)

Drawing diagrams > writing summaries > only reading, on spatial and causal knowledge.

In plain words: making a picture of plate tectonics taught more than summarizing it.
SHOWSBauer & Koedinger (2007)

Highlighting vs copy-paste notes: no difference.

In plain words: two activities in the same mode give the same learning, as ICAP predicts.

GOAL2
Learning goal 2 · Chi & Wylie (2014)

How can you design activities to move students from passive to interactive engagement?

🧪 Try it: slide the same lecture up the ladder and see what students must produce at each rung. Then test whether a pair activity is truly interactive.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name: (1) the key design lever (what students produce), (2) one upgrade for each step from passive to interactive, (3) the two checks that stop a design from slipping back down.

Change what students must produce, not the topic or the delivery (task and mode are independent). From a lecture: add a guided-notes sheet (active), then ask students to build a concept map or explain in their own words (constructive), then have pairs build or defend the map together, asking each other for justifications (interactive). In general: replace selecting with generating (generate link labels instead of choosing them; draw molecules instead of picking images), use prompts that need more than yes or no, and give incomplete worked examples.

Two design checks. (1) Intended vs enacted: a summary task can be done as copy-and-delete, so evaluate the products. (2) Group work is only interactive if the task is generative and both partners contribute with turn taking (joint, not individual, dialogue).

💬 In plain words: listen → fill in → build → build together and challenge each other.
You don't need a new lesson to raise engagement. Keep the content, change the job: from receiving it, to marking it, to making something with it, to making something with someone else.
✗ "Put them in groups and it becomes interactive."✓ Zhang & Linn: pairs who only selected images were active. Pairs who drew molecules were interactive. The task must be generative, and both must contribute.
Case questions: "As an instructional designer, which change raises engagement to the constructive level?" Find the option where students generate. Menus, copying, pausing and highlighting are all active.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSZhang & Linn (2013)

Pairs drawing molecules > pairs selecting images.

In plain words: generating together beats choosing together.
SUPPORTSKramarski & Dudai (2009)

Prompts to respond to and modify a friend's explanation > self-explain prompts only.

In plain words: reacting to each other's thinking adds to explaining alone.
SUPPORTSRussell et al. (1983)

Notes on a partial, scaffolded outline > a near-verbatim handout.

In plain words: a small active job beats being handed everything.

Learning goal 3 · Chi & Wylie (2014)

What are the limitations of the ICAP framework?

🧪 Try it: switch on a situation and watch what happens to the predicted staircase of results.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name the three overriding factors, each with its example study, and at least two built-in caveats.

Three factors can override the hypothesis. Assessment: shallow tests (recall, easy multiple-choice) cannot detect differences (Aleven & Koedinger, 2002; easy items in Menekse et al.). Domain: topics with arbitrary rules (English articles, Wylie, 2011) or so unfamiliar that learners have no schema to build on (emergence). Task differences within a mode: two interactive tasks can differ (inventing beat comparing and contrasting).

Built-in caveats: the knowledge-change processes are hypothetical and untested; overt behaviour is only a proxy (covert processing, intended vs enacted), so predictions hold with greater probability, not certainty; boundaries between modes are fuzzy (problem solving); ICAP covers only one aspect of learning (what students do).

💬 In plain words: ICAP is a good first cut, but the test, the topic and the exact task still matter, and what students do on the outside doesn't always match what happens inside.
T-T-T: the Test, the Topic, the Task. Plus two "inside" caveats: hidden processes and hidden thinking.
✗ "Within-mode differences prove ICAP wrong."✓ ICAP predicts differences across modes. Inventing and comparing both still beat lower modes.
"Which is NOT a limitation" questions: the false options often say ICAP was never tested in classrooms (it was), or that passive learning gives no learning at all (it gives recall).

Learning goal 4 · Brod (2021)

What are Generative Learning Strategies, and how do they differ from passive learning methods?

🧪 Try it: tap any activity on the line. Where does it sit between "take it in", "mark it" and "make something new"?
Tap a dot on the line.
✋ Answer out loud first. Give: (1) Brod's definition, (2) the mechanism (prior knowledge + integration), (3) the six strategies, (4) how they differ from passive and from merely active methods, (5) the link to ICAP.

GLSs are activities that prompt learners to produce something meaningful that goes beyond the information provided (Brod, 2021). Learners must activate prior knowledge and link it to the new information, which supports integration into existing knowledge. They come from the constructivist view of learning. The six reviewed: concept mapping, explaining, predicting, questioning, practice testing and drawing.

They differ from passive methods (listening, rereading), and also from merely active ones (highlighting, paraphrasing), because those reproduce the material without adding content. Brod equates GLSs with Chi's constructive mode. Practice testing counts as generative even when the answer is wrong.

💬 In plain words: you don't just take it in or mark it, you make something new with it.
If you can do it with a highlighter, it isn't generative. If you need a blank page, it probably is.
✗ "A practice test only counts as generative if you get the answer right."✓ It counts independent of correctness. Even a failed retrieval attempt helps structure knowledge.
Watch "summarizing": it is generative only if the learner enriches the text. A summary that condenses or paraphrases is not. Brod left summarizing out of the review for exactly this reason.

Learning goal 5 · Brod (2021)

How do developmental processes like cognitive and metacognitive growth influence GLS effectiveness across age groups?

🧪 Try it: move the age slider. Watch the three growing capacities, and see which strategies "unlock" at each school level.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name: (1) the three developmental processes, (2) the three deficiencies, (3) why strategies differ in when they start working (prerequisites), (4) one supporting study.

Strategy use grows with three processes. Knowledge: a richer semantic network to link to, and freed capacity. Cognitive capacity: executive functions (working memory, inhibition, shifting), tied to the maturing prefrontal cortex, which underlie reasoning and revising mental models. Metacognition: monitoring and control, which mature late. Children show production, mediation and utilization deficiencies.

So GLSs generally work better with age; around age 10 abstract self-reflection emerges. Strategies differ because their prerequisites differ. Predicting and testing mostly need effortful retrieval and basic feedback processing, so they work in lower elementary school. Questioning and drawing need prior knowledge and metacognitive monitoring to construct a useful question or drawing, so they are largely ineffective until secondary school (Table 1). Breitwieser & Brod (2020): children benefited more from predictions than examples, linked to reasoning ability.

💬 In plain words: a strategy only works once the brain skills it needs have grown. Guessing needs little, drawing and asking good questions need a lot.
"Test and guess work early. Draw and ask come late." And the three things that grow: know, control, reflect.
✗ "Age itself explains it."✓ Brod: age is not an explanatory variable. Children struggle because they typically lack the prerequisites. Age is only a rough proxy.
Know which capacity matters for which strategy: feedback processing (predicting, testing), reasoning (explaining, examples), metacognitive monitoring (questioning, drawing).

Children: predictions > examples. Students: equal. The more adult-like, the higher the reasoning score.

Sixth graders benefited from drawing; fourth graders did not, even with high support.

GOAL6
Learning goal 6 · Brod (2021)

How can GLS techniques (e.g., concept mapping) be adapted for different learners?

🧪 Try it: pick a learner. The concept map changes to show what you give them (solid) and what they must generate (dashed, gold).
✋ Answer out loud first. Explain: (1) the principle (match support to prerequisites), (2) concept-map adaptations for two age groups with their studies, (3) one support for drawing or questioning, (4) the limit of support.

Match the amount of support to the learner's prerequisites. Younger or weaker learners get part of the material and metacognitive prompts. Concept mapping: high-school students profited more from a high-coherence map where they only label existing lines, university students from a low-coherence map where they create and label lines (Gurlitt & Renkl, 2008). Elementary children who filled in parts of a map did better than those who built the whole map, which produced poor maps and no benefit (Karpicke et al., 2014). Drawing: provide background parts (Schmeck et al., 2014) or prompt learners to compare their drawing with an illustration (Van Meter et al., 2006). Questioning: give question stems (King, 1994).

The limit: if learners are given everything and need no effortful retrieval, the task is no longer generative. In the long run, choose strategies by individual prerequisites, not just age.

💬 In plain words: give younger learners a scaffold, not the answer.
"Scaffold, not answer." Younger = more given, but always something left to generate.
Giving part of the work frees up a child's limited working memory for the thinking part. But hand over the whole thing and there's no thinking left to do.
Coherence is the trap word: high-coherence map = lines already drawn (more support, high school). Low-coherence = learners create the lines (less support, university).

Learning goal 7 · Gerbier & Toppino (2015)

What is distributed practice, and why is it effective for long-term learning?

🧪 Try it: compare massed and spaced memory over time, then switch on each mechanism to see where it works.
✋ Answer out loud first. Give: (1) the definition with "same total time", (2) one number, (3) three mechanisms, (4) why the inverted U matters for choosing between them.

Distributed practice means repeating material in study sessions separated by time, instead of massing the same total time in immediate succession. Spacing improves long-term retention for almost all materials, ages and species (Gerbier & Toppino, 2015). Example: three 10-minute sessions over 3 days gave 35% better retention after four days than one 30-minute session (Bloom & Shuell).

Why it works: (1) Deficient processing: an immediate repetition gets less processing (smaller pupil dilation; repetition suppression in fMRI). (2) Synaptic plasticity: spaced stimulation produces more lasting LTP and consolidation, and over day-long gaps sleep and systems consolidation strengthen gist. (3) Study-phase retrieval: a repetition must remind you of the earlier encounter, and harder but successful retrieval strengthens memory and slows forgetting. This explains the inverted U and why the optimal lag grows with the retention interval. Reconsolidation may link retrieval to synaptic change.

💬 In plain words: a repeat that comes a bit later makes your brain work to pull the memory back, and that effort makes it last.
"Space for the date you need it." Three reasons, short to long: too-soon repeats get less effort, synapses need time to build, and each repeat must remind you.
✗ "Deficient processing explains the whole spacing effect."✓ It cannot explain why memory falls again when gaps are too long, or why the best gap depends on the retention interval. Study-phase retrieval can.
Match finding → mechanism: pupil dilation / repetition suppression → deficient processing; LTP with anisomycin → synaptic plasticity; inverted U, same-context advantage → study-phase retrieval; reminder + drug → reconsolidation.

🔬 The evidence behind the answer

SUPPORTSBloom & Shuell

One 30-min session vs three 10-min sessions over 3 days: 35% better after four days.

In plain words: same time, better spread, more remembered.
SUPPORTSXue et al.

Spaced faces: less repetition suppression in fusiform cortex, better recognition.

In plain words: the brain works harder on a later repeat.
SUPPORTSScharf et al.

Spaced (5 min) vs massed (20 s) stimulations: larger LTP, reduced by anisomycin.

In plain words: spacing builds stronger, protein-based synapse changes.

Learning goal 8 · Gerbier & Toppino (2015)

How can distributed practice be applied in educational settings?

🧪 Try it: you plan an 8-week course. Switch on the article's recommendations and watch the timeline and the retention meter.
✋ Answer out loud first. Name: (1) the three rules, (2) three teacher moves, (3) two learner moves, (4) the two traps and one exception.

Three rules: avoid massing, keep the intervals between practice opportunities as long as possible, and include practice tests. Teachers: summarize the previous day's lesson at the start of the next class, with a quiz before the summary; use several short reviews instead of one end-of-term review; give cumulative quizzes; put older material in homework; add follow-up reviews after compressed courses. Avoid two traps: easy initial learning ≠ good retention, and learned material still needs review. Exceptions: massing can help early with complex material; gaps can be too long; cramming suits only a very short retention interval.

💬 In plain words: spread it out, quiz yourself, keep coming back, and don't trust that "I've got it" feeling.
"Space it, test it, revisit it." And two traps: easy ≠ learned, learned ≠ done.
Teachers don't need new methods, just a new calendar: start class with a quiz on last time, keep tests cumulative, and slip old topics into homework.
