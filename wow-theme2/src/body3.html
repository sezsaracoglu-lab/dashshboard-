Engaging aging: a model of proactive work behavior and engagement with increasing age

Bakker, A. B., & Hakanen, J. J. (2019). Engaging aging: A model of proactive work behavior and engagement with increasing age. Chapter 12 in The fun and frustration of modern working life, pp. 153–163.

STEP1
Work engagement, and the question of this chapter

Can employees stay engaged as they grow older?

❓ This step answers: What is work engagement, why does it matter, and what does the chapter want to find out about age?
🔥 Listen to five employees: tap the tile that fits what each one says. Four tiles: three parts of engagement, and one that is something else.
Authors
Arnold B. Bakker and Jari J. Hakanen
Type
A short book chapter (2019), written as Wilmar Schaufeli's retirement approaches; his definition and questionnaire set off work engagement research
Built for
Three questions: does work engagement change with age, which HR practices foster "engaging aging", and what can employees do to stay engaged?
Main message
Aging brings loss and gain of personal resources; with energizing HR practices, employees can use both to act proactively and stay engaged and productive

Some words first. A resource is anything that helps you reach your goals or cope: energy, skills, health, support. Personal resources sit in the person; job resources sit in the job. HR practices are what an organization does to manage its people (HR = human resources). Proactive means acting on your own initiative, ahead of problems. In the picture: four tiles with a plain description. Five employees speak; tap the tile that fits.

Work engagement is defined as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication and absorption (Schaufeli and colleagues, 2002). Vigor: high levels of energy and the willingness to invest effort in the job. Dedication: being strongly involved in the work and experiencing significance, enthusiasm and inspiration. Absorption: being fully immersed in work activities, so that time passes quickly. For two decades most research has measured it with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale.

Engagement differs from other positive states such as happiness and job satisfaction because it is an activated state. A satisfied person can feel calm and content; an engaged person feels excited and enthusiastic, and is therefore prepared to put thinking power and energy into the work. That is also why it matters to organizations: open "Why it is so popular" at the end of the scene for what it has been related to.

Research on what leads to engagement produced Job Demands-Resources theory, which you know from theme 1. Engagement is most likely when job challenges are combined with personal or job resources: interesting, challenging tasks together with social support, autonomy and skill variety; or work pressure and deadlines on days when people feel optimistic and self-efficacious. One principle of the theory carries this chapter: employees need to be proactive to stay engaged.

Now the question. The average age of the workforce in industrialized countries is clearly rising. Findings so far point to a positive link between age and engagement: older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones (Hakanen and colleagues, 2019). The authors want to explain how engagement can be kept up with increasing age. Their plan: look at what people lose and gain as they age, combine that with what is known about proactivity, and build a model they call "engaging aging".

⚠ The trap✗ An employee who is satisfied with the job is engaged.✓ Satisfaction can be calm and passive. Engagement is an activated state: energy (vigor), meaning (dedication) and immersion (absorption).
📝 Exam-style questionA nurse says: "My shifts fly by, I feel strong and energetic, and my work really means something to me." Name the three dimensions of work engagement in her words.

Model answer: "My shifts fly by" is absorption: being fully immersed, so that time passes quickly. "I feel strong and energetic" is vigor: high levels of energy and willingness to invest effort. "My work really means something to me" is dedication: strong involvement with a sense of significance, enthusiasm and inspiration.

The model of engaging aging

❓ This step answers: What is the model of engaging aging, and how do loss, gain and HR practices lead to proactive behavior?
🗺️ Follow Karin, 58, through the model: six questions. Each answer lights up one part of the figure.

A model is a drawing of how the authors think things hang together; this one is a proposal. A motivational state is a condition that makes you want and able to act. A moderator is something that makes a link stronger or weaker. A plus sign on an arrow means: more of the first goes with more of the second. In the picture: age on the left; loss of resources above, gain below; HR practices and proactive behavior in the middle; work outcomes on the right.

The authors use proactivity theory to argue that all employees have to keep fine-tuning what they do at work and in their free time if they want to stay engaged and perform well, and that this is more needed with increasing age. Proactive behavior at work is self-initiated, anticipatory action, such as taking charge, voicing concerns and seeking feedback (Parker & Bindl, 2017). There is considerable evidence that it goes with favorable performance outcomes: person and job come to fit better, people get more chances to use their resources and strengths, and work may become more meaningful (Kooij and colleagues, 2017).

According to Parker, Bindl and Strauss (2010), three motivational states prompt and sustain proactivity. "Can do": do I believe I can, is it feasible, and what will it cost me (self-efficacy, feasibility, costs)? "Reason to": why would I? Feeling personally responsible, and wanting from within to take initiative. "Energized to": do I have the energy? A state of feeling and motivation that supplies the energy for proactive action.

The chapter connects each state to aging. Gain of personal resources facilitates "can do". Loss of personal resources provides the "reason to": as physical abilities slowly decline and the body recovers its balance less easily, the need to be proactive grows. And because age itself is only weakly and unsystematically related to proactive behavior, something has to supply the energy: age-focused HR practices initiated by the organization. They also act as moderators, and loss and gain may have a combined impact, as conservation of resources theory suggests.

⚠ The trap✗ In the model, losing resources makes older employees less proactive.✓ The model proposes the opposite: loss provides the reason to be proactive. What may be missing is the energy, which is why HR practices are in the model.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain with the model why two 60-year-olds with the same health complaints may differ in how proactively they adapt their work.

Model answer: Both have a "reason to" be proactive, because loss of personal resources (health, physical abilities) provides that reason. They may differ in "can do": the one who has gained more personal resources (job knowledge, judgment, wisdom, socio-emotional abilities) is better able to adjust. And they may differ in "energized to": age-focused HR practices in the organization (a health behavior climate, job crafting training, idiosyncratic deals) energize employees and strengthen the effect of loss and gain on proactive work behaviors.

STEP3
Resource loss and gain with increasing age

What goes down, what goes up, and what stays level

❓ This step answers: Which resources do employees lose and gain with increasing age?
⚖️ Predict eight changes: for each characteristic, decide whether it goes down, goes up, or shows no clear link with age.

The demographic trend toward an older workforce matters for organizations because individuals lose and gain resources as they age. The chapter reviews both sides, largely from the review that is reading 4 of this theme (Truxillo, Cadiz & Hammer, 2015).

Lost. Physical ability is the obvious one, with possible health problems and reduced work ability as a result. Cognitive ability changes too: fluid intelligence declines. And people lose social resources: fewer close friends, possibly less support from grown-up children, and a moderate decrease in social vitality, meaning that people approach life with less anticipation, enthusiasm and excitement.

Gained. Crystallized intelligence increases: knowledge, skills and wisdom. Personality develops in a favorable direction: more conscientiousness and agreeableness, less neuroticism. Although personality traits are usually defined as relatively stable, research shows these changes over the life span (Soto and colleagues, 2011). And emotional abilities grow: older people are better at perceiving and regulating emotions.

Hold on to two things for the rest of the chapter. First, the two lists return, in part, as the two boxes in Figure 1: loss (information processing capabilities, physical abilities, fluid cognitive abilities, health) and gain (job knowledge, judgment, wisdom, socio-emotional abilities). Second, proactivity does not rise with age by itself, even though older employees have both a reason and the means. That is the puzzle the model answers with HR practices.

⚠ The trap✗ Because cognitive abilities decline, older employees perform worse.✓ It is particularly fluid intelligence that declines; crystallized abilities increase. And research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance.
📝 Exam-style questionA manager wants to move all employees over 55 away from customer contact "because they are worn out". Give two findings from the chapter that argue against this.

Model answer (any two): Older people are better at perceiving and regulating emotions, which is very useful in work that involves interacting with clients. Agreeableness and emotional stability increase with age: older workers are more caring, less irritable and less anxious, and cope better. Social stressors may have a smaller impact on older people, who appraise stressful events less negatively. Research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance. Older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones.

STEP4
Age-focused HR practices

Three ways an organization can supply the energy

❓ This step answers: Which HR practices foster engaging aging, according to the chapter?
🏢 Six situations in a company: tap the practice each one shows. The tile then gets its name.

Because employees' resources change so much with age, the authors find it crucial that organizations adjust their usual HR practices, so that people stay healthy, engaged and productive (Truxillo and colleagues, 2015). In the model, age-focused HR practices are the third pillar: they energize employees to be proactive, and they strengthen the translation of "reason to" and "can do" into behavior. The chapter discusses three examples.

1. A health behavior climate. Management communicates, through strategy and vision, that it cares for its employees' health, and it arranges the environment so that healthy behavior is the easy option: a nudge. In this way organizations may indirectly improve employees' health, work engagement and performance.

2. Job crafting training. Employees learn to redefine their job in personally meaningful ways: they learn to identify their most important job demands and job resources, and to set and carry out goals to optimize their job design. This can take the form of a one-day training or workshop that ends in a personal job crafting plan carried out over several weeks. There is evidence that such interventions work, and the authors find them particularly suited to an aging workforce because they are tailored to individual needs and abilities.

3. Idiosyncratic deals (i-deals). Personalized arrangements negotiated between one employee and the organization. They vary in content and differ from employee to employee; the most common are about schedule flexibility and about development, and they can also be about tasks. They may protect against age-related loss (flexible hours allow recovery) and help with learning demands (development), and they show the employee that the organization is willing to deal with individual needs.

⚠ The trap✗ Job crafting training and idiosyncratic deals are the same thing: both fit the job to the person.✓ An i-deal is negotiated with the organization and results in an agreed arrangement. Job crafting is done on the employee's own initiative; the training only teaches and encourages it.
📝 Exam-style questionA hospital wants to keep its nurses over 50 engaged. Propose one measure for each of the three HR practices in the chapter.

Model answer (examples): Health behavior climate: include health behavior in the hospital's strategy and vision and arrange the environment so that healthy choices are the easy ones (for example healthy food and opportunities to be active during shifts). Job crafting training: a one-day workshop in which nurses make a personal job crafting plan and carry it out in the following weeks. Idiosyncratic deals: allow individual nurses to negotiate personalized arrangements, such as flexible working hours that allow recovery from physical strain, or development opportunities to keep up with new technology.

STEP5
Proactive work behaviors

Three behaviors that keep employees engaged

❓ This step answers: What strategies can employees use to stay engaged with increasing age?
🙋 Seven actions, in order: first follow an older manager through three steps, then four more actions. Tap the tile each action belongs to.

The model proposes that aging employees have the reason and the ability to be proactive, that HR practices energize them, and that employees can be encouraged to handle age-related changes in their resources with several proactive behaviors. The chapter discusses three with high potential for positive outcomes.

SOC strategies come from the theory of selective optimization with compensation (Baltes, 1997). Its central proposition: selection, optimization and compensation help individuals to allocate their limited resources optimally. Selection is choosing and prioritizing goals. Optimization is investing additional resources in the selected goals. Compensation is replacing means that no longer work with more effective ones.

Job crafting you know from theme 1. It was originally defined as the changes people make, in what they do and in how they see it, to the tasks and the relationships of their work (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). In the Job Demands-Resources approach it means changing your job demands and job resources on your own initiative, so that the work fits your abilities and preferences better (Tims & Bakker, 2010).

Proactive vitality management is the third behavior. Where job crafting changes aspects of the job or the work environment, this behavior directly manages one's own physical and mental energy, with various small strategies that help to stay vital or regain energy.

⚠ The trap✗ Compensation means lowering your goals when you get older.✓ Choosing fewer or different goals is selection. Compensation keeps the goal and changes the means: using other, more effective ways to reach it.
📝 Exam-style questionA 62-year-old teacher can no longer stand all day. She decides to concentrate on her exam classes, prepares those more thoroughly, and uses a stool and recorded instructions. Label the three SOC strategies.

Model answer: Concentrating on the exam classes is selection: choosing and prioritizing the goals to pursue in order to deal with resource loss. Preparing them more thoroughly is optimization: investing additional resources to achieve the selected goals. Using a stool and recorded instructions is compensation: replacing means that no longer contribute to reaching the goal with more effective means.

The authors' conclusion (p. 161), in our words. By combining the literature on aging with the literature on proactivity, the chapter arrives at a model of "engaging aging". Losing personal resources with age is a reason to be proactive; gaining personal resources makes proactivity possible. Because age is only weakly related to personal initiative, age-focused HR practices are needed to give employees the energy and to encourage proactive work behaviors: selection, optimization and compensation strategies, job crafting, and proactive vitality management. Employees who use these behaviors can be expected to stay engaged and productive as they grow older.

🧠 Why this matters

The chapter changes the usual story about older workers. A common picture of aging at work is decline (that contrast is ours). Here aging is loss and gain, and the response is activation: give people the energy and the room to reshape their own work. It is theme 1 (Job Demands-Resources theory, job crafting) applied to a whole working life.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

What are sustainable employability and engaging aging? The chapter does not use the term "sustainable employability"; in the words of this chapter it would be staying healthy, engaged and productive with increasing age (our link; a definition of sustainable employability has to come from another source, so tell me if your tutor gives one). Engaging aging is the chapter's own term for staying engaged with increasing age through proactive work behavior, supported by age-focused HR practices. Should interventions be offered only to older employees, or also to younger ones? This chapter points to both: it argues that all employees need to keep optimizing their work and leisure activities and that this becomes more needed with age; its nudging example speaks of younger and older employees; and job crafting training is tailored to individual needs and abilities.
