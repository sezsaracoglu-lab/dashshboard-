What is work engagement, why does it matter, and what does the chapter want to find out about age?Why this matters. Workforces are getting older, so organizations need people to stay engaged for longer. This short chapter combines research on aging with research on proactivity, and builds on theme 1 (engagement, JD-R, job crafting). A resource is anything that helps you reach goals or cope; personal resources sit in the person, job resources in the job.In this step you learn:1What work engagement is2How it differs from satisfaction3What leads to it4The chapter's question about age

⭐ A positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind characterized by vigor, dedication and absorption (Schaufeli and colleagues, 2002).VVigor: high energy and the willingness to invest effort.DDedication: strong involvement, with significance, enthusiasm and inspiration.AAbsorption: being fully immersed, so time passes quickly.William Kahn first introduced engagement in 1990. For two decades most research has measured it with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale. The chapter was written as Wilmar Schaufeli's retirement approached; his definition and questionnaire set off this research.So this means: engagement is energy, meaning and immersion together.→ Next: how it differs from feeling satisfied.

⭐ Engagement is an activated state; satisfaction can be calm and passive.A satisfied person can feel calm and content; an engaged person feels excited and enthusiastic, and is prepared to put thinking power and energy into the work. In an early qualitative study, Schaufeli and colleagues (2001) described an engaged bus driver who loved driving smoothly so passengers were not startled. Engagement has been related to higher task performance, creativity and financial results, fewer medical errors and less counterproductive behavior (Christian and colleagues, 2011), and to fewer depressive symptoms and higher life satisfaction.So this means: an exam option saying a satisfied employee is engaged is wrong.→ Next: where engagement comes from.

⭐ Job challenges combined with personal or job resources, as Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) theory predicts.Engagement is most likely with interesting, challenging tasks plus social support, autonomy and skill variety; or with work pressure and deadlines on days when people feel optimistic and self-efficacious (confident in their own ability). One JD-R principle carries this chapter: employees need to be proactive (acting on their own initiative, ahead of problems) to stay engaged.So this means: challenge plus resources, and your own initiative, keep engagement up.→ Next: the question about age.

⭐ Three questions: does work engagement change with age, which HR practices foster "engaging aging", and what can employees do to stay engaged?The average age of the workforce in industrialized countries is clearly rising. Findings so far: older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones (Hakanen and colleagues, 2019, a large-scale study in 30 European countries). The plan: look at what people lose and gain with age, combine that with proactivity research, and build a model of "engaging aging".So this means: the chapter asks how engagement can be kept up with age, not only whether it changes.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Work engagement is an activated state of vigor, dedication and absorption, fed by challenges plus resources; the chapter asks how to keep it up with age.1Engagement? Vigor, dedication, absorption (Schaufeli et al., 2002); UWES.2Vs satisfaction? Activated vs calm.3What leads to it? Challenges plus resources; proactivity.4Question? How to stay engaged with age; older workers slightly more engaged.Typical exam questions on this step:?A case: an employee is calm and content but not energized. Engaged? (no: satisfied)?Name the three components of work engagement

⚠ The trap✗ An employee who is satisfied with the job is engaged.✓ Satisfaction can be calm and passive. Engagement is an activated state: energy (vigor), meaning (dedication) and immersion (absorption).

The model of engaging aging

❓ This step answers: What is the model of engaging aging, and how do loss, gain and HR practices lead to proactive behavior?

What is the model of engaging aging, and how do loss, gain and HR practices lead to proactive behavior?Why this matters. This is the chapter's Figure 1, the core of the reading. It explains, step by step, how aging employees can stay engaged. The model is a proposal, not a tested result. Figure 1 (p. 157) reads left to right: employee age; loss above and gain below; age-focused HR practices; proactive work behaviors; work outcomes (engagement, creativity, performance).In this step you learn:1Why proactive behavior is needed2The three motivational states behind it3How loss, gain and HR practices fit

⭐ Because everyone must keep adjusting their work and free time to stay engaged and perform well, and this need grows with age.Proactive behavior at work is self-initiated, anticipatory action, such as taking charge, voicing concerns and seeking feedback (Parker & Bindl, 2017). There is considerable evidence that it goes with good performance: person and job fit better, people use more of their resources and strengths, and work may become more meaningful (Kooij and colleagues, 2017).So this means: proactivity is the engine that keeps engagement going over a working life.→ Next: what drives proactivity.

⭐ "Can do", "reason to" and "energized to" (Parker, Bindl & Strauss, 2010).A motivational state makes you want and able to act.1Can do: do I believe I can, is it feasible, what will it cost me?2Reason to: why would I? Feeling responsible, wanting from within to take initiative.3Energized to: do I have the energy for it?So this means: proactivity needs ability, a reason and energy together.→ Next: where age comes in.

⭐ Gain of personal resources gives the "can do"; loss gives the "reason to".As physical abilities slowly decline and the body recovers less easily, the need to be proactive grows: loss is a reason. Gained personal resources, such as emotional stability, self-discipline and emotion regulation skills, make it possible. Loss and gain may also work together: conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll and colleagues, 2018) says people protect their resources, and investing them matters more when others are lost, so older employees may use gained resources such as emotional stability and wisdom to compensate for loss.So this means: an exam option saying loss makes older employees less proactive is wrong: in the model, loss is a reason to act.→ Next: the missing piece.

⭐ To supply the energy: research on age and proactive behavior is inconclusive (weak positive links or none; Zacher & Kooij, 2017), so something has to energize it.Age-focused HR practices, initiated by the organization, give the "energized to". HR practices are what an organization does to manage its people. They also act as moderators (factors that change how strong a link is; here they make it stronger): they help turn "reason to" and "can do" into behavior. Proactive behavior then leads to engagement, creativity and performance.So this means: the organization's job is to energize what age already gives reason and means for.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Engaging aging: loss gives a reason, gain gives the means, HR practices give the energy; together they lead to proactive behavior and engagement.1Why proactivity? Fine-tuning work is more needed with age.2Three states? Can do, reason to, energized to.3Loss and gain? Loss = reason to; gain = can do.4HR practices? Energize, and strengthen the translation into behavior.Typical exam questions on this step:?In the model, what does resource loss provide? (a reason to be proactive)?Why are HR practices needed in the model? (age is only weakly related to proactivity)

⚠ The trap✗ In the model, losing resources makes older employees less proactive.✓ The model proposes the opposite: loss provides the reason to be proactive. What may be missing is the energy, which is why HR practices are in the model.
STEP3
Resource loss and gain with increasing age

What goes down, what goes up, and what stays level

❓ This step answers: Which resources do employees lose and gain with increasing age?

Which resources do employees lose and gain with increasing age?Why this matters. The model's two boxes, loss and gain, need content. The chapter reviews both sides, largely from reading 4 (Truxillo, Cadiz & Hammer, 2015).In this step you learn:1What declines2What grows3What this means for the model

⭐ Physical ability, fluid intelligence and some social resources.Physical ability declines, with possible health problems and lower work ability. Fluid intelligence, the ability to reason and handle new information (working memory, abstract reasoning, attention), declines too, most likely starting already around the early twenties (Kanfer & Ackerman, 2004). Physically: poorer vision and hearing, less strength, power and balance, higher blood pressure, slower recovery from stress (Hedge & Borman, 2012). People lose social resources: fewer close friends, possibly less support from grown-up children, and a moderate decrease in social vitality (approaching life with less anticipation and excitement). Our example: a long-distance runner in her fifties: slower, but running smarter races.So this means: age takes some speed, fluid reasoning and social energy, not knowledge.→ Next: what grows.

⭐ Crystallized intelligence, a more favorable personality and better emotional abilities.Crystallized intelligence (knowledge, skills and wisdom built up over the years) increases. Personality shifts favorably: more conscientiousness (organized, careful) and agreeableness (caring), less neuroticism (irritable, anxious) (Soto and colleagues, 2011). Older people are also better at perceiving and regulating emotions, in line with a positive link between age and emotional intelligence (Mayer and colleagues, 1999).So this means: an exam option saying older employees perform worse because cognition declines is wrong: mainly fluid intelligence declines, and research generally shows no significant link between age and job performance, although older workers most likely need to invest more cognitive and time resources to reach the same level.→ Next: the link with the model.

⭐ Loss gives a reason to act, gain gives the means; yet proactivity does not rise with age by itself.In Figure 1, loss covers information processing, physical and fluid cognitive abilities and health; gain covers job knowledge, judgment, wisdom and socio-emotional abilities. Older employees have both a reason and the means, but age and proactive behavior are only weakly linked.So this means: that puzzle is why the model needs HR practices.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ With age, physical ability, fluid intelligence and some social resources decline; crystallized intelligence, personality and emotional skills improve.1Lost? Physical ability, fluid intelligence, social resources.2Gained? Crystallized intelligence, conscientiousness, agreeableness, emotional stability, emotion skills.3For the model? Loss = reason, gain = means; proactivity still needs energy.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which kind of intelligence declines with age, and which grows? (fluid; crystallized)?Is age related to job performance? (generally no significant relationship)

⚠ The trap✗ Because cognitive abilities decline, older employees perform worse.✓ It is particularly fluid intelligence that declines; crystallized abilities increase. And research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance.
STEP4
Age-focused HR practices

Three ways an organization can supply the energy

❓ This step answers: Which HR practices foster engaging aging, according to the chapter?

Which HR practices foster engaging aging, according to the chapter?Why this matters. In the model, age-focused HR practices supply the energy for proactive behavior. The chapter gives three examples it expects to be most likely to be effective; many more are possible, such as age management programs, mentoring and flexible work hours.In this step you learn:1Why HR practices must adjust2A health behavior climate3Job crafting training4Idiosyncratic deals

⭐ Because aging comes with important changes in employees' resources, the authors find it seems crucial to adapt the usual HR practices to keep people healthy, engaged and productive (Truxillo and colleagues, 2015).Examples include age-focused job design and age-focused training and development (Dikkers and colleagues, 2017). In the model they energize employees to be proactive and strengthen the translation of "reason to" and "can do" into behavior.So this means: HR practices are the organization's part of engaging aging.→ Next: the first example.

⭐ Employees' perceptions of the organization's policies, practices and procedures for health behavior, including expectations and rewards that encourage health promotion.Management communicates its care through strategy and vision; this care becomes more important with increasing age. Organizations can also design the "choice architecture" so that younger and older employees are nudged to be physically active and eat healthy food (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). A nudge is a small change in the environment that steers behavior without forcing it. In this way organizations may indirectly improve health, engagement and performance. Our example: stairs placed more visibly than the lift.So this means: make the healthy choice the default.→ Next: the second example.

⭐ A training in which employees learn to identify their key demands and resources and to set goals to optimize their own job.It can be a one-day workshop ending in a personal job crafting plan carried out over several weeks. There is evidence such interventions work (for example Gordon and colleagues, 2018), and the authors find them particularly suited to an aging workforce because they are tailored to individual needs and abilities.So this means: the organization teaches employees to reshape their own jobs.→ Next: the third example.

⭐ Personalized arrangements negotiated between one employee and the organization, most often about schedule flexibility and development.(Rousseau, Ho & Greenberg, 2006.) I-deals vary per employee and can also be about tasks. They may protect against age-related loss (flexible hours allow recovery), help with learning demands (development), and show that the organization is willing to meet individual needs.So this means: an exam option saying i-deals and job crafting training are the same is wrong: an i-deal is negotiated with the organization; job crafting is done on the employee's own initiative, and the training only teaches it.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Age-focused HR practices energize proactivity: a health behavior climate, job crafting training and idiosyncratic deals are the chapter's examples.1Why adjust? Resources change with age (Truxillo et al., 2015).2Health climate? Care plus nudges toward healthy choices.3Crafting training? Learn to optimize your own job; tailored.4I-deals? Negotiated personal arrangements: flexibility, development.Typical exam questions on this step:?What is the difference between an i-deal and job crafting??Give an example of a nudge in a health behavior climate

⚠ The trap✗ Job crafting training and idiosyncratic deals are the same thing: both fit the job to the person.✓ An i-deal is negotiated with the organization and results in an agreed arrangement. Job crafting is done on the employee's own initiative; the training only teaches and encourages it.
STEP5
Proactive work behaviors

Three behaviors that keep employees engaged

❓ This step answers: What strategies can employees use to stay engaged with increasing age?

What strategies can employees use to stay engaged with increasing age?Why this matters. The model ends in proactive behavior. The chapter names three behaviors with high potential for positive outcomes, the employee's own tools for engaging aging.In this step you learn:1Selection, optimization and compensation2Job crafting3Proactive vitality management

⭐ Selection, optimization and compensation: ways to use limited resources as well as possible (Baltes, 1997).SSelection: choosing and prioritizing goals.OOptimization: investing extra resources in the chosen goals.CCompensation: replacing means that no longer work with more effective ones.Our example: a 60-year-old pianist plays fewer pieces (selection), practises those more (optimization), and plays slow passages before fast ones so they sound faster (compensation). The chapter's example: an older front-line manager chooses to become a leader who motivates his team rather than stay the expert (selection), takes leadership training and reads books (optimization), and after setbacks invests more time, shows self-compassion and models other leaders (compensation). Using SOC predicts work ability, engagement and job performance (Zacher and colleagues, 2015).So this means: an exam option saying compensation means lowering your goals is wrong: choosing fewer or different goals is selection; compensation keeps the goal and finds other means to reach it.→ Next: the second behavior.

⭐ Changing your job demands and resources on your own initiative so the work fits your abilities and preferences better (Tims & Bakker, 2010).You know this from theme 1. Originally: "the physical and cognitive changes individuals make in the task or relational boundaries of their work" (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001, p. 179). Four strategies: increasing resources (asking for feedback), increasing challenges (new tasks), decreasing hindering demands (less bureaucracy) and optimizing demands (Demerouti & Peeters, 2018). The chapter's aging examples: seeking support to learn new IT applications, volunteering for the mentoring program, or limiting time spent on details. Survey, diary and intervention studies link crafting to resources, engagement, meaningfulness and performance.So this means: crafting lets an aging employee reshape the job around changing resources.→ Next: the third behavior.

⭐ Managing your own physical and mental energy directly, with small strategies to stay vital or regain energy.Like job crafting it is self-initiated and goal-oriented, but it manages your own physical and mental energy with small strategies (Op den Kamp and colleagues, 2018). For aging employees: regular exercise, a walk in the park, a gallery visit, or regular micro-breaks. It has been positively related to relaxation, cognitive liveliness, work engagement, creativity and task performance.So this means: crafting changes the job; vitality management changes your energy.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Employees stay engaged with age through SOC strategies, job crafting and proactive vitality management.1SOC? Select goals, optimize effort, compensate with new means (Baltes, 1997).2Job crafting? Change demands and resources to fit (Tims & Bakker, 2010).3Vitality management? Manage your own energy directly.Typical exam questions on this step:?Classify: an older nurse uses a checklist for details she now forgets (compensation)?Which statement is wrong? "Compensation means lowering your goals."

⚠ The trap✗ Compensation means lowering your goals when you get older.✓ Choosing fewer or different goals is selection. Compensation keeps the goal and changes the means: using other, more effective ways to reach it.

The authors' conclusion (p. 161), in our words. By combining the literature on aging with the literature on proactivity, the chapter arrives at a model of "engaging aging". Losing personal resources with age is a reason to be proactive; gaining personal resources makes proactivity possible. Because age is only weakly related to personal initiative, age-focused HR practices are needed to give employees the energy and to encourage proactive work behaviors: selection, optimization and compensation strategies, job crafting, and proactive vitality management. Employees who use these behaviors can be expected to stay engaged and productive as they grow older.

🧠 Why this matters

The chapter changes the usual story about older workers. A common picture of aging at work is decline (that contrast is ours). Here aging is loss and gain, and the response is activation: give people the energy and the room to reshape their own work. It is theme 1 (Job Demands-Resources theory, job crafting) applied to a whole working life.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

What are sustainable employability and engaging aging? The chapter does not use the term "sustainable employability"; in the words of this chapter it would be staying healthy, engaged and productive with increasing age (our link; the chapter itself gives no definition). Engaging aging is the chapter's own term for staying engaged with increasing age through proactive work behavior, supported by age-focused HR practices. Should interventions be offered only to older employees, or also to younger ones? This chapter points to both: it argues that all employees need to keep optimizing their work and leisure activities and that this becomes more needed with age; its nudging example speaks of younger and older employees; and job crafting training is tailored to individual needs and abilities. (Reading "age-focused" as "designed with age in mind" is our interpretation.) Reading 4 adds more on this question.
