Reading 6 of 8 · Meeting 4 · Conceptual article · Employees as change agents

Sustainability champions

Pekaar, K. A., Demerouti, E., & van Gool, P. J. R. (2024). Sustainability champions: A proactive perspective on the inter-organizational job design dynamics of sustainability implementation. Organizational Psychology Review, 14(4), 594–617.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what they want to solve

Why is sustainability in supply chains so hard, and who can move it?

❓ This step answers: What problem does the article start from, what is its idea, and what does it add to earlier research?
🔎 Think along with the authors: five short decisions. Each one fills in a part of the picture.
Authors
Keri Pekaar (Tilburg University and Eindhoven University of Technology), Evangelia Demerouti and Piet van Gool (Eindhoven University of Technology), the Netherlands
Type
Conceptual article in Organizational Psychology Review (2024): it builds a framework with ten propositions. It collects no data
Question
How can individual employees who work at the border between organizations get sustainability implemented in a supply chain?
Main message
Motivated employees at the border can act as sustainability champions: by proactively regulating their own and their partner's sustainability demands and resources, they may steer sustainability from the bottom up (proposed, not yet tested)

Some words first. A supply chain is the row of organizations a product passes through: from raw materials to production, storage and delivery. A supplier sells to a buying firm; the large firm that leads a chain is called the focal firm. Inter-organizational means between organizations. A conceptual article develops ideas and predictions from existing research; a proposition is such a prediction, offered for others to test. In the picture: a supplier and a buying firm, and between them a place that is still empty.

The authors use the best-known definition of sustainable development, from the Brundtland Report (1987): development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Elkington (1997) turned this into the triple bottom line, also called the triple P framework: people, planet and profit. Its idea is that an organization's success should be measured in money and also in social and ethical standards and environmental impact. Supply chain sustainability then means organizational efforts to consider people, planet and profit during a product's whole journey through the chain. Most research looked at only one of the three; the authors use all three, because for them implementing sustainability is about conserving resources in a broad sense.

The United Nations set 17 goals for sustainable development by 2030, and these cannot be reached without changing how businesses work with their supply chain partners. That has proven particularly hard. In their plain language summary the authors give three reasons: several players have to change their behavior, it is generally costly, and people lack the resources to make a difference. Organizations depend on partners that may lack the expertise, know-how, means or motivation to change. And unlike a regular economic exchange, supply chain sustainability is an ongoing change process between organizations. Management of it is typically demand-focused (regulations, policies, managerial decisions), which alone is often ineffective because employees lack the resources to act.

Change of this kind does not happen automatically through pressure from the top. It needs change agents who start and carry it at the place where critical sourcing decisions are made: the interface between organizations. People who work there are called boundary spanners. The authors call the motivated ones sustainability champions. Their central claim: a champion should work on the partner's balance of sustainability demands and resources, and in that way indirectly improve the own balance, because the demands and resources on both sides of the border are linked. The authors do not discard top-down models; systematic and contextual factors often override individual influence in large change processes, but employee behavior and motivation remain crucial.

The authors aim for three contributions. To the supply chain literature: a bottom-up, behavioral solution, a possible "missing behavioral link" in a literature with little work on the micro level. To Job Demands-Resources theory: the idea that one employee's crafting can activate an employee in another organization to become proactive, and that this can build up into gain or loss spirals. To the organizational change literature: a concrete description of how individual employees act as change agents together, with different roles (the initiator, the resource-providing partner). The influence that travels up and down the chain is expected to pass through social exchange and social learning.

The field of supply chain sustainability management goes back to the early 2000s. Much of it defined and measured concepts, or reviewed enablers and barriers at the level of society or the organization: external pressures, supply and reputation risks, how focal firms can check suppliers' compliance. More recently researchers turned to the micro level: social and psychological factors. The article's Table 1 gives the overview; open it below.

⚠ The trap✗ In this article "actor" and "partner" are the two companies in the supply chain.✓ They are individuals who work across organizational boundaries, for example a purchasing professional at the focal firm and someone at a first-tier supplier. The authors chose "actor (initiator)" and "partner" instead of "buyer" and "supplier" on purpose: implementation can start on either side and travel up or down the chain, depending on the industry, market power or the individual motivation of the people involved.
📝 Exam-style questionA retailer sends all its suppliers a new sustainability policy and a schedule of audits. Two years later little has changed. Explain this with the article's analysis.

Model answer: The retailer used demand-focused management: policies and compliance checks. According to the authors this is, in isolation, often ineffective, because the employees who have to act have insufficient resources to adopt sustainable behavior. Supply chain sustainability is an ongoing inter-organizational change process, and such processes do not occur automatically through top-down pressure. It needs motivated change agents at the interface who balance sustainability demands with sustainability resources, for themselves and for their partner.

STEP2
The building blocks · propositions 2 and 3

Sustainability demands and resources, on both sides

❓ This step answers: What are sustainability demands and resources, and why are those of actor and partner tied together?
⚖️ Sort six situations: a demand or a resource? In the last three, watch for whom.

Job Demands-Resources theory (Demerouti and colleagues, 2001) is the theory from theme 1. Job demands are aspects of work that require sustained physical or psychological effort and therefore have costs. Job resources are aspects of work that help to achieve work goals, reduce demands and their costs, or stimulate learning, growth and development. Demands drain energy; resources can generate motivation; people thrive when the two are in balance. In the picture: two baskets, one for demands and one for resources.

The theory's core, as the authors use it: a work environment can be divided into demands and resources, and what matters is their balance. The framework starts from the observation that implementing sustainability disturbs that balance for the individual employee. It adds sustainability criteria to the economic and operational demands that are already there, and these often do not fit the traditional job, so the employee has to combine them with regular duties such as deadlines and quality.

Sustainability demands named in the article: sourcing new products, requiring additional information, searching for new ways of storage and transport, cultural differences, hazardous working conditions in the manufacturing process, and compliance checks; also time and financial pressure, costs and conflicting priorities. Sustainability resources: capital, sustainability training, autonomy and know-how; also a network, knowledge and managerial support. Proposition 2 says that implementing supply chain sustainability changes the balance of both actors and partners, by imposing additional sustainability demands and requiring specific sustainability resources.

Actor and partner are bound by the exchange between their organizations. When the actor starts, the status quo is challenged and the partner has to update the own resource pool. An extra demand in the exchange makes the partner look for extra resources. And because sustainability expertise is spread over organizations, one organization's pool is not enough: knowledge has to be integrated and resources shared. So demands and resources cross the border, and when they do they may change in nature: a resource for one may be an additional demand for the other. Proposition 3: the partners' sustainability demands and resources are (inversely) related, so that changes on one side prompt corresponding updates on the other.

The theory has mostly been applied to one individual at a time. Team and organizational factors are included as things that shape one employee's demands and resources, but the influence is usually seen as one-way, with little attention for what my demands and resources mean for someone else. Researchers have begun to study this among colleagues (Tims and colleagues, 2015; Tims & Parker, 2020). The authors propose a similar mechanism for actor and partner. Like those colleagues, they have to collaborate closely; unlike them, they work at different organizations that negotiate about the "costs" of their shared responsibility.

⚠ The trap✗ A resource is a resource: when a buyer provides resources, everybody's balance improves.✓ Across a boundary, demands and resources may change in nature. What the partner receives as a resource (specialized knowledge, a higher price) may be an additional demand for the actor who has to provide it. This is why proposition 3 says "(inversely) related", and why proposition 7 (step 4) says that helping the partner is costly at first.
📝 Exam-style questionA purchaser asks her supplier for a fully traceable product. Use propositions 2 and 3 to describe what this does to the work of the supplier's account manager, and what it may do to the purchaser's own work.

Model answer: For the account manager the request is an additional sustainability demand on top of economic and operational demands (proposition 2): extra information has to be collected and delivered, and specific resources are needed, such as know-how, time or capital. Because the demands and resources of the two are interrelated (proposition 3), the supplier's side has to update its resource pool, and may need resources from the purchaser's side, such as knowledge or a higher price. Providing those is a resource for the account manager and may be a new demand for the purchaser. (The traceable product is our example.)

STEP3
The right-hand side of the framework · propositions 4 and 5

The same demand: motivation or strain?

❓ This step answers: What happens in the partner when an initiator brings sustainability demands, and what decides whether the result is promoting or undermining behavior?
🤝 Predict first: six cases. Two suppliers get the same demand; watch which route lights up.

Motivation is the wish and energy to do something. Strain is the wear that follows from demands that keep draining a person. An audit is a formal check, here of whether a supplier meets sustainability standards. A code of conduct is a set of rules a buying firm asks its suppliers to follow. A first-tier supplier delivers directly to the focal firm; a second-tier supplier delivers to that supplier. In the picture: on the left what arrives at the partner, in the middle what the partner feels, on the right what the partner does.

Job Demands-Resources theory describes two processes that start when the mix of demands and resources changes. In the motivational process, resources foster motivation, through chances to grow, learn and reach work goals, and that leads to better performance. In the health-impairment process, chronic demands deplete a person's cognitive and physical resources, which leads to strain and then to impaired performance. The theory looks at the balance, with two ideas: the buffer hypothesis (resources weaken the draining effect of demands) and the boosting hypothesis (demands bring out the benefit of resources). The authors propose that an initiator triggers these processes in the partner.

When the partner is challenged with additional sustainability demands, this costs resources. With ample access to sustainability resources in the own work environment, the draining effect is buffered: the partner experiences more sustainability motivation and responds with more sustainability-promoting behavior than in a resource-constrained environment. That is proposition 4. Support comes from two directions. Among colleagues, an employee who proactively increased challenging demands together with job resources raised co-workers' work enjoyment and their willingness to collaborate (Fong and colleagues, 2022). And at firm level, suppliers reduced their sustainability violations when focal firms offered resources (training, price premiums) along with demands (enforced codes of conduct, audit systems). The framework offers a behavioral explanation for such firm-level findings.

The other route. In the theory, the impairment process can in the end lead to self-undermining: behavior that creates obstacles and makes one's own situation worse. The authors apply it: a partner who faces sustainability demands with low access to sustainability resources may experience sustainability strain and respond with sustainability-undermining behavior. That is proposition 5. Fong and colleagues (2022) again give the parallel among colleagues: unbalanced demands and resources in a collaboration lowered the work enjoyment of both and raised relationship conflict.

Many focal firms manage supplier sustainability in exactly this unbalanced way: they impose demands such as codes of conduct, monitor compliance with audits, and punish violations with fines or by ending the contract. The article calls this relying on "sticks" without "carrots", and it often proves ineffective for improving sustainability in the long term. It can even backfire: suppliers with few resources may be compelled to hide information from auditors (Plambeck & Taylor, 2016). The authors hedge these propositions with "may": the routes are expected, not demonstrated at the level of individual employees in two organizations.

⚠ The trap✗ Sustainability demands on suppliers are bad: they cause strain and cheating.✓ The demand is not the problem; the balance is. With high access to resources the same demand is a challenge that raises motivation and promoting behavior (proposition 4). Only with low access to resources is it expected to lead to strain and undermining behavior (proposition 5). The framework does not advise firms to stop asking; it advises them to give as well.
📝 Exam-style questionA newspaper reveals that a factory kept two sets of records: one for the auditors of its Western buyers and one real. Explain with the framework how this may have come about, and what the buyers could have done differently.

Model answer: The framework would read this as sustainability-undermining behavior, the end of the impairment route (proposition 5): the people at the factory were challenged with sustainability demands (codes of conduct, audits, the threat of fines or losing the contract) while they had low access to sustainability resources, which may lead to strain and to hiding practices or audit fraud. Buyers could have provided resources along with the demands, such as training, knowledge or price premiums, so that the resources buffer the demands and the motivational route (proposition 4) becomes more likely. (The newspaper case is our example; the article mentions hiding information from auditors and audit fraud.)

STEP4
The left-hand side of the framework · propositions 1, 6, 7 and 8

Regulating my own balance, and my partner's

❓ This step answers: Which proactive strategies can a sustainability champion use, why do they cost something, and what has to be in place first?
🏅 Follow Noor, a buyer (our example): match each thing she does to a tile, then answer two questions about what it takes.

Proactive means acting on your own initiative, before you are told to. Job crafting, from theme 1, is the proactive changing of your own job demands and job resources. The authors use the word regulation for the same kind of behavior, so that it can also cover changing someone else's demands and resources. In the picture: four tiles, two for what a champion does for herself and two for what she does toward the partner.

The framework begins with the sustainability motivation of one individual, the initiator. Just as organizations can want sustainability for business, legitimacy or development reasons, individuals can have an internal desire for it, which shapes what information they seek, what they decide and what they do. Research cited by the authors shows that individual sustainability motivation drives sustainability decisions at work, especially when organizational objectives are unclear, and that it is influenced by personal attitudes, perceived social norms and perceived control (Swaim and colleagues, 2016). The authors call this motivation the "spark". They do not study where it comes from; they study what happens next. For the spark to spread, the initiator needs empowerment and tools: a resourceful work environment with autonomy, social support and opportunities for growth. Proposition 1: implementation requires a motivated and proactive initiator who can mobilize sufficient sustainability resources in the own work environment.

Partner-focused sustainability regulation is the new concept. Job crafting is normally self-directed, with effects on others as a by-product. Here the employee intentionally changes the demands and resources of a person in another organization: imposing sustainability demands and providing sustainability resources. At firm level the supply chain literature knows this as supplier development, with direct strategies (the buyer invests: money, training) and indirect ones (certification, auditing). Proposition 6: actors can use proactive partner-focused regulation to positively influence the partner's balance, and so trigger the partner's sustainability motivation and promoting behavior. To do it well, the actor needs meta-knowledge: insight into what the partner can handle and what the partner lacks.

Helping the partner is not free. The resources an actor provides come from the actor's own pool, and in an interdependent chain a resource given is a resource no longer held. Crafting also takes energy and can bring role conflict, disappointment and regret in the short term, and the long-term benefit of crafting someone else's environment is even less certain. Proposition 7: actors who use partner-focused regulation may need to draw from their own sustainability resource pool, risking an initial disbalance in their own demands and resources.

That is why the champion's own base matters. The work environment of supply chain actors often does not support sustainability: they typically lack autonomy, managerial support, training, capital, collaboration across functions and latitude to decide. Sustainability behavior is usually voluntary, so what they invest is not automatically replaced by the organization. Self-focused sustainability regulation (optimizing own demands, seeking own resources) repairs this. Proposition 8: actors can use proactive self-focused regulation to optimize their own balance, such that it facilitates partner-focused regulation. An earlier study by two of the authors found that optimizing demands and seeking resources went with more proactive sustainability behavior from day to day (Pekaar & Demerouti, 2023).

⚠ The trap✗ Partner-focused regulation is just job crafting with a side effect on others.✓ Earlier research showed that self-directed crafting can affect colleagues as a side effect. Partner-focused regulation is different in two ways: the change in the other person's demands and resources is intended, and the other person works in a different organization. The authors present this as an extension of Job Demands-Resources theory to pairs of people across organizations.
📝 Exam-style questionAn enthusiastic buyer spends months coaching a supplier on cleaner production. Her own targets suffer and her manager is displeased. Which propositions describe her situation, and what would the framework have advised her to do first?

Model answer: She used partner-focused regulation by providing resources (proposition 6). Those resources came from her own pool, which may create an initial disbalance in her own demands and resources (proposition 7): time and energy went to the supplier while her regular demands stayed. The framework advises self-focused regulation first or alongside (proposition 8): seek resources in her own organization, such as managerial support, autonomy, time or budget, and optimize her own demands, so that her own balance can carry the investment. Proposition 1 makes the same point: an initiator needs sufficient resources in the own work environment.

STEP5
The framework over time · propositions 9 and 10

A journey, not a destination

❓ This step answers: What does the framework expect over many exchanges, how does it spread through a longer chain, and how could it be tested?
🌀 Play it forward: six rounds. Watch which spiral grows.

A spiral is a loop that strengthens itself: each round makes the next one more likely. Lagged means with a delay: the effect comes later. A dyad is a pair. Longitudinal research measures the same people several times. In the picture: two empty frames, one for what grows when exchange is balanced and one for what grows when it is not.

Implementing sustainability in a supply chain is, in the article's words, a journey and not a destination. One response from the partner is not enough; it needs a lasting stream of sustainability resources through the chain. Job Demands-Resources theory already describes loss and gain cycles within one person: loops between demands and resources, proactive behavior and outcomes that strengthen over time. The article moves these loops between two people in different organizations.

The gain side rests on the boosting hypothesis. A raised sustainability demand makes the partner's resources more valuable and prompts a proactive, motivated search for more. The proactive behavior thus passes from initiator to partner, and it may come back to the initiator as resources that the partner found and shares. This needs a balanced exchange of organizational resources. Proposition 9: a balanced organizational resource exchange is related to a gain spiral, in which high sharing of sustainability resources (initiator) and sustainability-promoting behavior (partner) influence each other over time, through successful regulation of the sustainability resources of both. The authors point to reading 5 (Luu, 2019) as an example of resource gain spirals inside organizations, and add that under the present conditions in most supply chains gain spirals are not likely to develop.

The loss side. People under strain tend to use maladaptive strategies, such as poor communication, careless mistakes and conflicts, and these add to their demands. Between organizations: demands without resources lead the partner toward undermining behavior, and the initiator toward sharing less. Suppliers who are audited by many buyers can develop audit fatigue, which draws them away from real improvement and may end in audit fraud; when exposed, that can severely damage the brand. Proposition 10: an unbalanced organizational demands exchange (high sustainability demands, low resources) is related to a loss spiral, in which low sharing of resources (initiator) and sustainability-undermining behavior (partner) influence each other over time, through unsuccessful regulation of the sustainability demands of both.

The framework describes one pair, but it is reversible. A partner who becomes motivated can be the initiator toward a sub-supplier. Actors and partners therefore have a dual role: meeting the lead firm's demands by mobilizing resources, and acting as a change agent who passes demands and resources on. The result can be a ripple effect up and down the chain. One finding fits: the resources available at first-tier suppliers determine their ability to drive implementation to second-tier suppliers (Wilhelm and colleagues, 2016).

The propositions are meant to be tested. The authors recommend collecting data from both sides of the pair, employees in different, connected organizations. Because the partner needs time to respond, the time frame matters: event sampling or diary studies for quick responses, a longitudinal panel for slower ones. The spiral propositions require several measurements, possibly at several levels. For the analysis they suggest Actor-Partner Interdependence Models, which separate what a person's behavior does for that person from what it does for the partner, and simulation approaches (agent-based modeling, system dynamics) for the dynamics over time.

⚠ The trap✗ The article shows that balanced exchange produces gain spirals in supply chains.✓ It proposes this. The article reports no data of its own. The spirals are derived from Job Demands-Resources theory, from studies of colleagues inside organizations and from firm-level supply chain research. Whether they occur between individual employees of two organizations still has to be tested, with repeated measurements on both sides.
📝 Exam-style questionYou want to test proposition 9. Describe a suitable design: who you measure, how often, and how you analyze it.

Model answer: Measure both members of the pair: employees at the border in two connected organizations, for example buyers and their contact persons at suppliers (multi-source data). Measure several times, because a spiral is a process over time and partners need time to respond: a longitudinal panel, or diary or event-sampling waves. Measure the initiator's sharing of sustainability resources and the partner's sustainability-promoting behavior at each wave, and ideally the organizational exchange too. Analyze with an Actor-Partner Interdependence Model to separate actor and partner effects and to test influence in both directions; a gain spiral predicts that each one's earlier behavior is related to the other's later behavior, with levels rising over time.

The authors' conclusion (p. 611), in our words. The article brings a proactive job design perspective to the change process of implementing sustainability between organizations. Behavioral regulation strategies at the micro level, between organizations, might steer implementation from the bottom up and in the end add up to gain or loss spirals of sustainability resources through the supply chain. The framework is meant to offer new explanations for why sustainability development between organizations is stimulated or held back.

🧰 What to do with it, according to the authors

Employees at the border (actors and partners): recognize your own sustainability demands and resources and craft your job to create room for sustainability; invest in understanding your partner's demands and resources, through more effective and transparent communication, so that partner-focused regulation fits and the response is more collaborative. Leaders: model proactive and sustainable behavior and promote it through resource-providing and empowering leadership. Organizations: select team leads of buyers and salespeople on these criteria or train them; give buyers and salespeople extra resources for sustainability initiatives and reduce the trade-off with other performance outcomes, for instance by including sustainability performance in the key performance indicators (the measures on which people are judged); offer job crafting training. Supply chain managers: encourage resource-sharing across all organizational levels.

🌍 The place of context

The bottom-up focus does not replace top-down models. Local work designs are shaped by context at several levels: global and industrial (government regulation, technology), organizational (strategy, initiatives, climate) and team (social dynamics, leadership). According to the authors, context works mainly through social norms, which communicate what sustainability behavior is expected and whether proactivity is valued, and which shape both self-focused and partner-focused regulation. Context can also disturb the balance directly, through crises such as the pandemic, technological disruptions or new regulations.

⚠️ How sure can we be?

This is a conceptual article. Its propositions are derived from Job Demands-Resources theory, from studies of colleagues inside organizations, and from supply chain research at the level of firms. The step to individual employees in two different organizations is the authors' proposal, and they present the propositions as a research agenda. The note under Figure 1 adds two limits: influence between actor and partner rarely is a direct exchange between two individuals, because it is part of a broader exchange between their organizations, which both can influence only to some degree; and the macro-level process of that exchange (negotiations, market forces) is outside the model. The authors do believe the framework reaches beyond sustainability: in a study of 225 manufacturer-distributor pairs, resource-providing behavior between salespeople and individual buyers was more strongly related to beneficial exchange between the organizations than the same behavior between top executives (Huang and colleagues, 2016).

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

How can employees proactively drive green initiatives within their organizations? This article's answer, and it reaches across the organization's border: by being a motivated initiator with enough resources (proposition 1); by self-focused regulation, that is optimizing the own sustainability demands and seeking sustainability resources (proposition 8); and by partner-focused regulation, that is imposing sustainability demands on the partner together with providing sustainability resources (proposition 6), guided by knowledge of what the partner can handle. What obstacles do sustainability champions face? Sustainability demands on top of regular duties and trade-offs with cost; a work environment that does not support it (little autonomy, managerial support, training, capital, latitude); voluntary investments that the organization does not replace; dependence on a partner who may lack means or motivation; the short-term cost of helping the partner (proposition 7); and the risk of a loss spiral when exchange is unbalanced. How can they deal with them? Secure the own balance first, learn the partner's situation, give resources together with demands, and ask the organization for support: leadership that provides resources, sustainability in the performance measures, and job crafting training.
