Increasing pro-environmental behaviors by increasing self-concordance

Unsworth, K. L., & McNeill, I. M. (2017). Increasing pro-environmental behaviors by increasing self-concordance: Testing an intervention. Journal of Applied Psychology, 102(1), 88–103.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what they want to solve

Not for the planet, for your own goals

❓ This step answers: Why do the usual ways of promoting green behavior often fail, and what third approach do the authors propose?
Authors
K. L. Unsworth and I. M. McNeill; page numbers on this page refer to the accepted manuscript you have (38 pages of text, tables and figure at the end)
Type
Empirical article in the Journal of Applied Psychology (2017): one time-lagged correlational study and two experiments
Question
Can people be motivated to act more sustainably by linking green behaviors to their own personal goals, even goals that have nothing to do with the environment?
Main message
Yes: helping people connect a green behavior to goals that matter to them raised its self-concordance and their intentions to do it, beyond their environmental values (small effects, mostly intentions)

Why do the usual ways of promoting green behavior often fail, and what third approach do the authors propose?Why this matters. This is the last reading and one of the intervention studies reading 1 cited. Pro-environmental behavior helps the environment, such as saving energy or taking the bus. An intention is what you plan to do. The article is by Unsworth and McNeill in the Journal of Applied Psychology (2017): one time-lagged correlational study and two experiments. Page numbers refer to the accepted manuscript.In this step you learn:1Why green strategies at work disappoint2Why the two usual approaches fail3The third approach: self-concordance

⭐ Because their success depends on whether employees go along, and employees do not always do so.Organization-wide green strategies are sometimes successful, but their effects are underwhelming. Reasons employees do not go along: a mismatch with their values, low priority of sustainability causing conflicting goals, and cynicism when the organization says one thing and does another.So this means: the bottleneck is employees' own motivation.→ Next: the first usual approach.

⭐ Because people tend to brush aside, or push back against, messages that clash with what they already believe and want.Approach 1 tries to make people care about the environment: changing values, norms, knowledge and attitudes. It fails with those who deny climate change, find it unimportant, or whose political identity opposes it, a large group: in a study of 1001 Americans, 28% did not believe in global warming or that humans could reduce it; in a 2014 poll, 38% of Australians and 36% of Americans said they were tired of all the attention for the environment. Environmental goals can also conflict with other goals, such as privacy or the organization's priorities. A value is a broad principle you find important.So this means: "do it for the planet" does not reach people who do not care about the planet.→ Next: the second approach.

⭐ Because the effect of rewards does not last, and compliance can backfire and cause rebound effects.Approach 2, from economics and behavioral finance, uses rewards or compliance. Extrinsic motivation means acting for an outside reward or to avoid punishment. Compliance strategies are rules people must follow. A rebound effect means savings in one place lead to more use elsewhere (our example: someone with an energy-efficient car drives more). The authors' verdict on both approaches: mixed effects at best. Our example: a teenager tidies the room for a reward, until the reward stops.So this means: outside pressure tends to fade or backfire.→ Next: the alternative.

⭐ Raise the self-concordance of the green behavior: show how it serves the person's own values, identities and long-term goals (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999).It builds on goal hierarchy and goal systems theories. People act by their own choice, avoiding the downside of extrinsic motivation, and they pick their own goals, so a lack of interest in the environment does not block it. The authors predict this works even after controlling for environmental concern and political ideology. Our example: "a tidy room means you find your football boots before the match."So this means: an exam option saying the approach works by making people care more about the environment is wrong: it links the behavior to their own, possibly non-green goals.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Environmental appeals and rewards often fail; the self-concordance approach links green behavior to people's own goals.1Why disappoint? Employees do not go along: values, priorities, cynicism.2Appeals? Rejected by those who do not share them (28% non-believers).3Rewards and compliance? Do not last; can backfire.4Third approach? Self-concordance through own goals.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which statement is wrong? "Self-concordance works by making people care about the planet."?Why should self-concordance work for climate skeptics? (it uses their own goals)

⚠ The trap✗ The self-concordance approach works by making people care more about the environment.✓ It works by linking the green behavior to the person's own goals, which may have nothing to do with the environment (saving money, health, an easy life). That is why it should also work for people who do not hold environmental goals.
STEP2
The theory · Figure 1 · the hypotheses

One behavior, many goals

❓ This step answers: What is a goal hierarchy, what exactly is self-concordance, why can it be changed, and what did the authors predict?

What is a goal hierarchy, what exactly is self-concordance, why can it be changed, and what did the authors predict?Why this matters. Figure 1 shows part of an academic's goals. Understanding it is the key to the whole reading. Our analogy: a train station is more worth visiting the more of the lines you want to travel stop there.In this step you learn:1The goal hierarchy and its links2Self-concordance and why it motivates3Why it can be changed, and earlier evidence4The three hypotheses

⭐ A map of everything you strive for, in four layers: values, identities, projects and tasks.Your goal hierarchy holds everything you strive for, at work and at home, in four layers: values (for example creativity), roles you identify with (researcher), longer projects (publishing a paper) and everyday tasks (answering emails). Higher-order goals are all the goals above the behavior you are looking at. An identity is a role you see yourself in. In Figure 1: values such as respect, helpfulness and family; identities such as researcher, teacher and father; projects such as conducting research and teaching; tasks such as statistics and turning off office lights.So this means: every behavior sits under goals that give it meaning.→ Next: the links.

⭐ By facilitative links (the behavior helps a goal) and inhibitive links (it blocks a goal).A facilitative link: activating one activates the other; the academic sees statistics as helping him publish, so wanting to publish brings statistics to mind. To activate a goal is to make it active in your mind. An inhibitive link: activating one suppresses the other; statistics takes time from teaching, so while doing statistics his teaching goal is pushed aside.So this means: a behavior can help some goals and hurt others at the same time.→ Next: self-concordance.

⭐ The motivational pull a behavior gets from its links: more helping and fewer hindering links to higher-order goals, weighted by how important those goals are to the person.Self-determination theory (SDT) says everyone needs autonomy, competence and relatedness. Unlike SDT, which names goals that matter for everyone, self-concordance leaves it to each person to decide which goals count. Our example: video meetings help family time (very important) and hinder personal contact (slightly important), so most weight lands on the helping side.So this means: an exam option saying self-concordance means fitting the three basic needs of self-determination theory is wrong.→ Next: why it motivates.

⭐ Two reasons: one action pays off for many goals (utilitarian), and it helps your goals fit together (self-consistency).Utilitarian: more value for the same effort. Self-consistency: an action that serves many goals is easier to keep up and helps the parts of your personality fit together, which goes with well-being.So this means: behaviors that serve many of your goals are worth more and last longer.→ Next: can it be changed?

⭐ In theory yes: the links are thought to be malleable, so new helping links could be created through goals people already have; Studies 2 and 3 test this.Situations and learning can activate, strengthen or weaken links. In Figure 1, turning off office lights helps only one goal (teaching students about green behavior) and no hindering links: weak to moderate self-concordance. If the academic realizes he can use it as a measure in his research, a new helping link appears and self-concordance rises, without any environmental goal.So this means: the lever is to add links to existing goals, not to install new values.→ Next: earlier evidence.

⭐ Two studies that framed climate action in terms of other goals got more supportive emotions (Myers) or intentions (Bain), though neither measured self-concordance.Testing is needed because earlier self-concordance research covered behaviors with direct personal payoffs (work performance, coping, hazard planning); green behaviors mainly benefit society and pay off much later. Framing climate change as a public health risk raised more supportive emotions than framing it as an environmental or national security risk (Myers and colleagues, 2012). Asking climate skeptics to link green citizenship to interpersonal warmth or societal development raised intentions more than linking it to fewer climate hazards (Bain and colleagues, 2012). The authors' explanation: most people hold goals such as health, warmth and progress, while a climate goal clashes with many people's own goals. Both prescribed one goal; the authors want people to choose their own.So this means: non-green framings can work better than climate framings.→ Next: the hypotheses.

⭐ One correlational test of the link (H1), then two experiments testing whether raising self-concordance raises intentions (H2a) and whether it works through self-concordance (H2b).H2b is a mediation hypothesis: the intervention works through raising self-concordance. Study 1 tests H1; Studies 2 and 3 test H2a and H2b.So this means: one correlational test, then two experiments.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Self-concordance is how much a behavior helps (and does not hinder) a person's own important goals; it motivates and can be raised by creating new links.1Goal hierarchy? Values, identities, projects, tasks.2Links? Facilitative and inhibitive.3Self-concordance? More helping, fewer hindering links, weighted by importance; own goals.4Why motivating? Utilitarian and self-consistency.5Changeable? Yes, links are malleable.6Earlier evidence? Myers et al. (2012); Bain et al. (2012).7Hypotheses? H1, H2a, H2b (mediation).Typical exam questions on this step:?Which statement is wrong? "Self-concordance means fitting SDT's basic needs."?What is an inhibitive link? (activating the behavior suppresses a goal)

⚠ The trap✗ Self-concordance means that a behavior fits the three basic needs of self-determination theory.✓ Self-concordance here does not prescribe which goals matter. It is calculated from the person's own goals: how helpful the behavior is for each, weighted by how important each goal is to that person.

A time-lagged correlational study · Tables 1 and 2

Does self-concordance go with green action?

❓ This step answers: How was self-concordance measured, how was real behavior captured, and what did Study 1 find?

How was self-concordance measured, how was real behavior captured, and what did Study 1 find?Why this matters. Study 1 tests H1. A correlational study measures things as they are, without changing anything: it shows whether they go together, not what causes what. Time-lagged means measured at different moments. Our example: checking whether students who see more reasons to study visit the library a month later.In this step you learn:1The sample2The self-concordance score3The behavior measure4The result and its limit

⭐ Australian working adults from a survey panel, surveyed twice, one month apart: 528 completed the first survey, 305 the second, and 303 could be matched.Spreading the measures over a month reduces response biases (answers colouring each other when everything is asked at once). Only 181 of the 305 answered the petition question.So this means: a month's gap separates the predictor from the behavior.→ Next: the score.

⭐ By multiplying, for 12 goals, how important each goal is by how helpful green energy use is for it, and adding up: a score from 12 to 420.Importance 1 to 5; helpfulness 1 (very unhelpful) to 7 (very helpful). Goals included financial goals, frugality, being helpful to others, protecting the environment, our children's future, an easy life, reducing climate change. The score correlated with climate change beliefs (.22) and environmental identity (.49), as a related measure should (convergent validity), but not with political orientation (.02) (divergent validity).So this means: the score is personal: your goals, your weights.→ Next: the behavior.

⭐ By a petition to the government for 100% renewable energy at the end of the second survey, with a decoy petition.A second petition to scrap the carbon tax guarded against social desirability (answering to look good). People could decline, sign the standard text, change it, or write their own. Of 181 who answered, 29.3% were not interested; the rest signed, so the outcome was signed versus not. Signing the renewable petition was unrelated to signing the decoy (r = −.06).So this means: a real action, not just a stated plan.→ Next: the result.

⭐ Self-concordance predicted signing, beyond green beliefs, identity and politics: H1 supported.B shows the direction and size of a predictor's link with the outcome; above zero means more of it goes with a higher chance of signing. The odds compare the chance of signing with the chance of not signing. Logistic regression predicts a yes/no outcome from several variables at once. B = .012, p = .007: each extra point raised the odds of signing by about 1% (the score runs from 12 to 420). The p-value is the chance of a result this strong if there were no real effect; below .05 is significant. Control variables, included to rule them out: free-market ideology, environmental identity, climate change beliefs, age, gender, political orientation (Table 2). The controls cover green goals from broad to specific, to rule out that green goals, not self-concordance, drive signing. Political orientation also mattered. Environmental identity and climate change beliefs did not predict signing, probably because they overlap with self-concordance (r = .49 and .22); free-market ideology just missed significance (p = .055).

Robustness: counting the 124 who skipped the question as non-signers, and four other model variants, gave the same result.So this means: people who see green action as serving their goals act green.→ Next: the limit.

⭐ That raising self-concordance causes green action: the study is correlational, so it cannot show cause, or that self-concordance can be raised at all.That is what the two experiments test.So this means: an exam option saying Study 1 shows raising self-concordance makes people sign is wrong.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Study 1: a personal self-concordance score predicted signing a renewable-energy petition a month later, beyond green beliefs and politics; correlational only.1Sample? 305 Australian working adults, two surveys a month apart.2Score? Importance × helpfulness over 12 goals (12–420).3Behavior? Petition; decoy petition.4Result? B = .012, p = .007; H1 supported.5Limit? No causal claim.Typical exam questions on this step:?How was self-concordance scored in Study 1??Which statement is wrong? "Study 1 shows raising self-concordance causes signing."

An experiment · Tables 3 to 5 · mediation

Tell and ask: can self-concordance be raised?

❓ This step answers: What was the intervention, did it raise intentions to save energy, and did it work through self-concordance?

What was the intervention, did it raise intentions to save energy, and did it work through self-concordance?Why this matters. Study 2 is an experiment: researchers change one thing and use random assignment, so groups differ only by chance. A control condition gets no intervention. Our example: a coach who says: "running also helps you sleep and meet friends; how could it help you?"In this step you learn:1The sample and conditions2The tell-and-ask intervention3What people wrote4The effect on intentions, and mediation

⭐ 507 Australian adults from a survey panel, none from Study 1: 256 randomly assigned to the self-concordance condition, 251 to control.After removing 53 blank or "don't know" answers and 42 people who finished in under 8 minutes, 412 remained (172 and 240). People who did the writing task were more often female and valued the environment more than those who dropped it, but did not differ from the control group, so this is unlikely to bias the result.So this means: random assignment lets differences be read as caused by the intervention.→ Next: the intervention.

⭐ Two parts: tell people green behaviors have many other benefits, then ask them to write how such behaviors could help their own goals.Tell: energy saving saves money, commuting gives time to read, careful waste means cleaner beaches for your holidays, socially responsible products mean less exploitation, walking and cycling mean fewer accidents. Ask: participants wrote how such behaviors could help their goals. The written answer was the manipulation check (a test that the intervention was really taken in). Most answers were about energy, so the study focused on energy behavior.So this means: the person makes the links, not the researcher.→ Next: what they wrote.

⭐ Money came up most often (51 answers), the environment or climate next (37); the authors say these counts are only indicative.Money was not always selfish, for example saving money to treat one's children. Table 3: compared with control, the experimental group saw saving energy as more helpful for green goals (environment, children's future, climate) and for non-green goals (financial goals, an easy life, being a good citizen, changing the world).So this means: if people had only linked to the planet, this would be approach 1 in disguise; it was not.→ Next: the effect.

⭐ Yes, a little: 3.46 versus 3.23 on a 1 to 5 scale (p = .021, d = .23), and self-concordance rose too: H2a supported.Effect size d shows how large a difference is (by a common rule of thumb, not from the article, around .2 is small). Self-concordance: 262 versus 247 (p = .045). With controls (age, gender, education, political orientation, social desirability, environmental values), self-concordance still added explained variance (Table 5: R², the share of differences explained, from .25 to .39), and the strong effect of environmental values (.46) shrank to non-significant (.11).So this means: a short writing exercise caused a small rise in green intentions.→ Next: the route.

⭐ Yes, fully: the whole effect ran through self-concordance (H2b supported).Total effect: the tell-and-ask group intended more (B = .23). Step 1: the intervention raised self-concordance (B = 15.17, p = .046). Step 2: higher self-concordance went with higher intentions (B = .01, p < .001). Step 3: at equal self-concordance, the intervention had no significant extra effect (direct effect B = .11, p = .17). A bootstrap test (re-running the analysis on 5000 resamples) put the indirect effect between .03 and .24 with 95% confidence; that range excludes 0. It was compared with doing nothing, not with a climate message.So this means: an exam option saying full mediation means the intervention had no effect is wrong: it worked, entirely through self-concordance.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

An experiment with three conditions

Your goals versus carbon facts

❓ This step answers: Does the self-concordance intervention also work for commuting, and does it beat the usual climate change message?

Does the self-concordance intervention also work for commuting, and does it beat the usual climate change message?Why this matters. Study 2 compared tell-and-ask with doing nothing. Study 3 adds the real competitor: a climate message. Our example: two posters at a bus stop, one with carbon numbers, one asking "what would you do with 40 free minutes a day?"In this step you learn:1The design with three conditions2What people wrote3The results4How solid the climate comparison is

⭐ A self-concordance message, a climate change message and a control condition, for commuting by public transport.300 full-time employees from the UK and US (Prolific; students screened out, home workers removed; most drove to work) read a scenario (an imagined situation): your company removes most parking spaces but will heavily subsidize public transport. Self-concordance: seven advantages (saving money, time to read, time to catch up on work, exercise, helping others by reducing cars, no status differences, better for the environment), a reminder of one's own most important value and goals, then writing how public transport would help them. Climate: carbon facts, then writing how it would help reduce climate change. Control: writing thoughts about public transport.

Intentions: four items (1 to 7), two reversed; self-concordance with 13 goals ("being successful" added). 174 were analysed (49, 60, 65 per condition; the article does not detail every exclusion). The inconvenience of public transport for each person was also measured.So this means: the study tests self-concordance against the usual approach, for a disliked behavior.→ Next: what people wrote.

⭐ In the self-concordance condition only 7 people wrote about the environment; most wrote about money (24) or leisure.Leisure: relaxing (7), reading (7), talking (3).So this means: people again linked green behavior to non-green goals.→ Next: the results.

The authors' conclusion (manuscript pp. 37–38). Their key sentence: "motivation to perform environmentally sustainable behaviors can be increased by letting people connect these behaviors to higher-order goals of their own personal choice". In our words: this route works without people holding green goals; it is not meant to replace environmental values, but to reach people who would otherwise ignore green behavior.

🆕 What the article adds

The first tests that self-concordance can be changed and that changing it has a causal effect: earlier tests were all correlational. Self-concordance also works for behaviors on the edge of people's self-serving goals, such as green behavior. And it explains earlier findings (Myers; Bain): those interventions prescribed one goal (public health, warmth, or the needs of self-determination theory) and so only work for people who value that goal; letting people choose their own goals reaches more people. Self-determination-based campaigns belong to approach 1.

🧰 What organizations can do with it

Design campaigns that show how a green behavior serves many common, non-environmental goals, and then let employees think through how it serves their goals. At work, link the behavior to workplace goals such as being a good employee or colleague, getting a promotion or building skills. This may be especially useful in organizations with mixed motives about the environment (like the fictional firm of Study 3, which removed parking to build offices but paid for public transport) or in industries that seem to contradict green values, such as oil companies: there, environmental appeals may backfire because people see a mismatch between words and deeds.

⚠️ How sure can we be? The limits the authors name

Small to medium effects, though the authors argue the tests were conservative: the petition is partly an activist act; Study 2 targeted a broad range of green behaviors; commuting by public transport is widely disliked. Not tested on skeptics as a group: samples came from the general population, although environmental goals were controlled for. Intentions, not behavior, in the experiments: intentions predict behavior, but not perfectly; adding action plans (when and where you will do it) could close the gap. A closed list of goals, limited to avoid fatigue; an open-ended approach should be tested. Unclear mechanism over time: did people form new links or become aware of existing ones? So it is unclear how long the effect lasts without reminders, although research in other domains (Carr & Walton, 2014) suggests such links can last.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

Why does self-concordance help to increase environmentally sustainable behaviors? A behavior that is connected to many goals that matter to a person, through helping links and few hindering ones, has more motivational pull: it serves several goals at once ("bang for the buck"), is easier to regulate and fits the person as a whole. Because the links are malleable, an intervention can create or highlight them, for any goals people hold, so it does not depend on environmental values and avoids the backlash and the fading effects of the other two approaches. How can organizations increase pro-environmental behaviors? By telling employees how green behaviors serve common goals and asking them how the behaviors serve their own, linked to workplace goals.
