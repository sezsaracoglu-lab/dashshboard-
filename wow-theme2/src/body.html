Theme 2 · The psychology of sustainability in organizations · Prepared for Sezen

What makes people at work act green, and stay able to work?

A company can promise to be sustainable. Whether it happens depends on what its people do every day, and on whether those people can keep working well for a whole career. This theme gives you the psychology of both.

The story of this theme · 4 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Theme 1 gave you one engine for the whole course: job demands, job resources, and what employees themselves do with them (job crafting, playful work design). Theme 2 uses that engine for a new question: sustainability. The word has two meanings here. One is sustainability for the planet: how do employees, leaders and human resource practices make an organization greener? The other is sustainability for the people: how do employees stay engaged and employable as they grow older?

Two meetings, eight readings. Meeting 3, Sustainability models, gives the frameworks: employee green behavior (reading 1), the green behavior of leaders (reading 2), staying engaged with increasing age (reading 3) and supporting an aging workforce (reading 4). Meeting 4, Sustainability crafting and interventions, asks what can be done: green human resource practices and collective green crafting (reading 5), sustainability champions (reading 6), comparative feedback (reading 7) and self-concordance (reading 8).

Why a psychologist learns this: technology and laws set the frame, but, as Stern (2000) noted, what organizations do is the biggest direct source of many environmental problems, and what organizations do is the behavior of people. Changing behavior at work is the psychologist's field.

Reading 1 of 8 · Meeting 3 · Review article · The map of the theme

Employee green behavior as the core of environmentally sustainable organizations

Zacher, H., Rudolph, C. W., & Katz, I. M. (2023). Employee green behavior as the core of environmentally sustainable organizations. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 10, 465–494.

STEP1
What employee green behavior is, and what this review does

Not good intentions: countable behavior at work, for better or worse

❓ This step answers: How is employee green behavior defined, and why do the authors call it the core of a sustainable organization?
Authors
Hannes Zacher (Leipzig University), Cort W. Rudolph (Saint Louis University) and Ian M. Katz (Old Dominion University)
Type
Review article in the Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior (2023): it summarizes a whole research field and proposes a model
Built for
Three goals: review the research, build one integrative model, and give recommendations for research and practice
Main message
Organizations become sustainable through what employees do; that behavior is work performance, shaped at five levels

How is employee green behavior defined, and why do the authors call it the core of a sustainable organization?Why this matters. This is the map reading for the whole theme: every later reading builds on its definition. An organization becomes sustainable (able to go on without using up what the future needs) only through what people in it do, so you need a precise idea of that behavior first.In this step you learn:1The definition, read in parts2Why the behavior is the core of a sustainable organization3Why it counts as work performance, and the forms it takes4What this review sets out to do

⭐ "Scalable actions and behaviors that employees engage in that are linked with and contribute to or detract from environmental sustainability" (Ones & Dilchert, 2012).The review shortens employee green behavior to EGB (quoted on p. 467). Read it in parts:1Scalable: it can be measured, by how often it happens or how well it is done.2Behaviors: what the employee does and controls, not the outcomes of behavior.3Contribute to or detract from: it can help or harm, judged by its impact on the natural environment.So this means: green behavior is countable behavior at work, for better or worse, not good intentions.→ Next: why it matters so much.

⭐ Because organizations cannot reach their environmental goals unless employees at different levels actually perform green behavior.Many organizations now put environmental sustainability goals in their strategy, next to economic and social goals: the triple bottom line. Once that is so, an employee's behavior can be judged by what it adds to or takes from those goals. Our example: a company pledges to halve its waste; that pledge only becomes real when people at the printer, in purchasing and in the board room act on it.So this means: the strategy is on paper; the behavior makes it real.→ Next: how the authors classify this behavior.

⭐ Because green behavior cuts across the established dimensions of work performance, held together by one goal: environmental sustainability.Work performance is goal-directed behavior that contributes to the organization's goals. Its established dimensions include task performance, citizenship (helping beyond your task), counterproductive behavior, team member performance and leadership performance. A compound performance domain is not one more box next to these; it runs through them, and the list is not comprehensive. Adaptive, proactive and innovative performance can also have an environmental impact.So this means: if green behavior is performance, everything known about the causes of performance applies to it (step 4).→ Next: the forms it takes.

⭐ Prescribed or discretionary, helpful or harmful, more relevant to some jobs, and present at every level of the hierarchy.Prescribed: creating sustainable products, delivering services in sustainable ways. Discretionary (left to your own choice): switching the computer off, or wasting paper. It can ask for initiative or creativity, such as voicing suggestions. It matters more for a sustainability officer than for a supermarket cashier, and runs from top managers' purchasing to team members' advocacy among coworkers. It differs from pro-environmental behavior outside work (behavior that harms the environment as little as possible, or even benefits it; Steg & Vlek, 2009), because work brings its own motives, opportunities, constraints, norms, leaders and climate; but the two may share causes such as green attitudes, knowledge and habits.So this means: an exam option saying green behavior is only the good, voluntary things employees do is wrong.→ Next: what the review adds.

⭐ It reviews the field, builds one integrative model, and gives recommendations, looking at causes and consequences at several levels.A review article summarizes what a whole field has found. In 2012, Ones and Dilchert urged organizational psychologists to study the microfoundations of organizational sustainability: the individual behaviors on which the organization's results rest. Since then the field grew: four edited books, six systematic reviews and two meta-analyses. But most studies focused on individual antecedents (things that come before a behavior and may lead to it), such as attitudes, values, perceived norms and intentions. The authors argue for causes and consequences at five levels: individual, team, work context, organization, and society and culture.So this means: the review widens the lens from the person to everything around the person.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Employee green behavior is measurable behavior at work that helps or harms the environment; it is a performance domain and the core of a sustainable organization.1Definition? Scalable behaviors that contribute to or detract from environmental sustainability (Ones & Dilchert, 2012).2Why the core? Environmental goals are reached only through employees' behavior.3Performance domain? It cuts across all performance dimensions.4Forms? Prescribed or discretionary, helpful or harmful, at every level.5What the review does? Review, model, recommendations; causes and consequences at five levels.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which statement about employee green behavior is wrong? ("only voluntary, helpful acts")?Why do the authors call green behavior a compound performance domain??How does employee green behavior differ from pro-environmental behavior outside work?

⚠ The trap✗ Employee green behavior is the list of good, voluntary things employees do for the environment.✓ It covers behavior that helps and behavior that harms, and behavior that is required and behavior that is voluntary. What makes it green behavior is that it is countable behavior at work with an impact on the environment.

Five levels in, the core, five levels out

❓ This step answers: What is the authors' model of employee green behavior, and at which levels do its causes and consequences sit?

What is the authors' model of employee green behavior, and at which levels do its causes and consequences sit?Why this matters. Step 1 defined the behavior. Figure 1 places it in a map of causes and consequences. Every later step of this reading, and readings 2, 5, 7 and 8, fill in a part of this map. A conceptual model is a proposal built on research, not a tested result.In this step you learn:1The core of the model: green behavior described three ways2How causes reach the behavior: direct and indirect paths3What the behavior brings, bottom-up

⭐ Green behavior, described three ways: as a compound performance domain, as self-regulated behavior, and as something studied at several levels.1A compound performance domain with distinct dimensions (task-related, citizenship, proactive, innovative, counterproductive) that may have shared and unique causes; knowledge and skills may matter more for task-related forms, motivation more for discretionary ones.2Self-regulated: employees steer it in a goal-directed way (step 4).3Multilevel: differences between persons, changes within a person over time, and a whole team's behavior added together.So this means: the middle of the map is richer than one behavior; it is a family of behaviors that people steer.→ Next: how the causes reach it.

⭐ Through three direct determinants (knowledge, skills, motivation) and indirect ones that work through them.Direct determinants: knowledge, skills and motivation. Indirect ones: stable individual differences (such as personality) and cognitive and affective states, which act through the direct ones. Our example: a green attitude only turns into recycling if it raises motivation, and the person also knows how and can do it.So this means: every individual cause has to pass through knowing, being able, or wanting.→ Next: and the levels around the person?

⭐ Directly, by giving opportunities or putting up constraints, or indirectly, through the levels below them.The levels are the team, the work context (technology, equipment, job roles such as a sustainability officer, work design such as autonomy), the organization (strategies, initiatives, culture and climate), and society and culture (laws, technology, external stakeholders, cultural values). Our example: a law can work through an organization's strategy, which works through a team's norms, which work through one person's motivation. Leadership sits at the team level, next to coworker support, advocacy, norms and team climate.So this means: an exam option saying the organization influences employees only through its leaders is wrong.→ Next: the other side of the map.

⭐ Benefits that may build bottom-up from the individual to the team, the organization and society, possibly giving a competitive advantage and feeding back into more green behavior.For the individual: need satisfaction, identity development, self-efficacy, well-being, job performance, future intentions. Team: norms, climate, team environmental performance. Work context: green tasks and jobs, through job creation and green job crafting. Organization: environmental performance, attractiveness. Society: national and international sustainability goals. Outcomes can build step by step: members act green → green team climate → better team environmental performance → organizational goals met. Some individual outcomes may raise later green behavior: a virtuous cycle.So this means: the model mirrors itself: five levels in, five levels out.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Figure 1: five levels of causes → green behavior (a self-regulated, multilevel performance domain) → outcomes at five levels.1Middle? Compound performance domain; self-regulated; multilevel.2Individual causes? Direct: knowledge, skills, motivation; indirect: traits and states.3Surrounding levels? Direct (opportunities, constraints) or indirect through lower levels.4Outcomes? Bottom-up at five levels; possible competitive advantage and virtuous cycle.Typical exam questions on this step:?Name the three direct determinants of green behavior?Where does the model place leadership? (team level)?Give one example of a bottom-up chain of outcomes

⚠ The trap✗ In the model, the organization influences employees only through its leaders.✓ Every contextual level can work directly (opportunities and constraints) or indirectly through the levels below. And leadership is placed at the team level, next to coworker support, advocacy, norms and team climate.
STEP3
The kinds of green behavior

The Green Five, a sixth, and three lines to place any behavior on

❓ This step answers: Which five dimensions of employee green behavior does the taxonomy distinguish, how do they differ, and what is the sixth?

Which five dimensions of employee green behavior does the taxonomy distinguish, how do they differ, and what is the sixth?Why this matters. A taxonomy is an ordered system of categories. With it, you can name any green behavior in a case, and see which kinds research has neglected.In this step you learn:1Why a taxonomy was built2The Green Five3The sixth: counterproductive sustainability behaviors4Why the required/voluntary split is criticized

⭐ Because early research focused mostly on single green acts, such as recycling paper; an overview of everything employees do was missing.Ones and Dilchert (2012) built the Green Five from thousands of critical incidents (real examples of good or bad behavior) collected in the United States and Europe. It is hierarchical: one overall pro-environmental performance factor, five broad dimensions, and 17 narrow subdimensions. Many constructs in the literature fit into one of the five.So this means: the taxonomy turns a pile of single acts into an ordered map.→ Next: the five.

⭐ Transforming, conserving, avoiding harm, influencing others and taking initiative.🔁Transforming: adapting work products and processes to make them more sustainable; doing required tasks in sustainable ways belongs here.💡Conserving: preserving resources and avoiding waste (reducing use, recycling).🛡️Avoiding harm: reducing negative impact and mitigating damage, including environmental management activities.📣Influencing others: promoting other people's green behavior (educating, training).🚀Taking initiative: proactive, entrepreneurial behaviors (initiating programs, lobbying, activism) involving personal risk and sacrifice.Each has a positive and a negative end (saving energy, wasting energy). Table 1 below gives subcategories and measures.So this means: to classify a behavior, ask what it is about: the process, resources, damage, other people, or initiative.→ Next: what the five leave out.

⭐ Counterproductive sustainability behaviors: actively harmful behaviors that risk or directly result in environmental harm (Dilchert, 2018).Dilchert noted that the taxonomies left out the more severe, actively harmful behaviors. Because sustainability is a strategic goal of many organizations, harming the environment detracts from that goal and violates the organization's legitimate interests: that makes it counterproductive. A related construct is "nongreen behaviors". The five plus this one are the "green six".So this means: wasting energy is the low end of conserving; the sixth is for more severe harm.→ Next: a split the review rejects.

⭐ Because it confounds the motive for a behavior with its content.Ones and colleagues criticized the split: it confounds (mixes up) why a behavior happens with what it is. Not printing e-mails is the same behavior whether a rule demands it or you choose it. So transforming, whose example measure is task-related, can also be discretionary.So this means: classify a green behavior by what it does, not by why the person does it.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ The Green Five (transforming, conserving, avoiding harm, influencing others, taking initiative) plus counterproductive sustainability behaviors make the green six.1Why a taxonomy? Research looked at single acts; Ones & Dilchert (2012) built an overview.2Green Five? Transforming, conserving, avoiding harm, influencing others, taking initiative.3Sixth? Counterproductive sustainability behaviors (Dilchert, 2018).4Required vs voluntary? Confounds motive with content.Typical exam questions on this step:?Classify: an engineer redesigns a process to use less water (transforming)?Which statement is wrong? "Wasting energy is a counterproductive sustainability behavior, outside the Green Five." (it is the low end of conserving)

❓ This step answers: Along which three lines can any green behavior be classified?

Along which three lines can any green behavior be classified?Why this matters. The Green Five say what a behavior is about. The three lines (Francoeur and colleagues, 2021) say how it is carried out. Continuous means a line with two ends and everything in between, not two boxes.In this step you learn:1The three lines2What they reveal about the research

⭐ In-role or extra-role, direct or indirect, and low or high intensity.1In-role (part of your core tasks) or extra-role (what you do as an organizational citizen).2Direct (you act on the environment yourself) or indirect (you encourage others: coworkers, customers, supervisors, other departments).3Low intensity (less effort and risk, incremental) or high intensity (more effort and risk, radical). A related distinction: local versus wide impact.Our example: switching off the lights is extra-role and low intensity; redesigning a production process can be in-role and high intensity.So this means: an exam option saying extra-role and high intensity are the same is wrong: they are different lines.→ Next: what the lines reveal.

⭐ They show that questionnaires cover the lines unevenly, so what we know is mostly about voluntary, individual behavior.In the review of scales: 60% of items were about direct and 40% about indirect behavior; 41% were explicitly about voluntary behavior and only 5% about in-role behavior (55% did not say); 72% were about individual employees, 13% about leaders, 7% about pairs and 8% about collective behavior such as a team.So this means: the research picture is skewed; be careful generalizing to in-role or team behavior. Most scales were also built for office behavior (paper, printing, lights); production and customer service are hardly studied.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Three lines classify how a behavior is carried out; the measures lean heavily toward direct, voluntary, individual behavior.1Three lines? In-role/extra-role; direct/indirect; low/high intensity.2Why they matter? Measures are uneven: mostly voluntary (41% vs 5% in-role) and individual (72%).Typical exam questions on this step:?Place: a manager persuades purchasing to buy recycled paper (indirect)?What share of scale items was about individual employees? (72%)

⚠ The trap✗ Extra-role green behavior is the same as high-intensity green behavior.✓ They are different lines. Switching off the lights can be extra-role and low in intensity. Redesigning a production process can be in-role and high in intensity.
STEP4
The psychological theories behind green behavior

Why people act green, and what it takes to do it

❓ This step answers: Which theories from social and environmental psychology are used to explain employee green behavior?

Which theories from social and environmental psychology are used to explain employee green behavior?Why this matters. Researchers used many theories to understand the web of relations around green behavior (the review calls it the nomological network). Most effort went into the causes. Each theory suggests a different way to change behavior (our reading; the review itself only says that combining the theories could help design interventions).In this step you learn:1Two theories that are easy to mix up2Three more: value-belief-norm, social identity, social exchange3Three models built by organizational scholars

⭐ A theory of rational decision-making: attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control predict intentions, which result in behavior (Ajzen, 1991).An attitude is how positively or negatively you judge something. A subjective norm is what you think important others expect. Perceived behavioral control is how able you feel to do it. An intention is a plan to act. Our example: "I think cycling is good, my team expects it, and I can do it, so I plan to cycle tomorrow."So this means: in this theory, green behavior follows a reasoned plan.→ Next: a theory about moral obligation.

⭐ A theory of moral obligation: personal norms, switched on by awareness of consequences and feeling responsible, drive behavior (Schwartz, 1977).A personal norm is what you feel morally obliged to do. Our example: "If I leave the machine running, the river gets polluted, and that is on me."So this means: an exam option saying planned behavior and norm activation are the same because both use norms is wrong: subjective norm (others) versus personal norm (my own obligation).→ Next: three more theories.

⭐ Each adds a different source of motivation: personal values (value-belief-norm), belonging to a group (social identity), and giving back what you receive (social exchange).1Value-belief-norm theory extends norm activation: personal values predict environmental beliefs, then personal norms and behavior.2A social identity approach: identification with the in-group, its norms and goals, and collective efficacy beliefs predict environmental action.3Social exchange theory: people feel obliged to reciprocate when they receive rewards or bear costs in a relationship.The studies, in one line each: planned behavior: intentions to commute sustainably and to voice suggestions (Yuriev and colleagues, 2020); norm activation: awareness and responsibility led to personal norms, which led to energy saving (Zhang and colleagues, 2013); value-belief-norm: personal norms mediated the link between environmental worldviews and energy conservation (Scherbaum and colleagues, 2008); social identity: units that got comparative feedback (how they did against another unit) saved more energy than units that saw only their own results (Siero and colleagues, 1996; reading 7); social exchange: organizational support went with green behavior among employees with a strong exchange ideology (Paillé & Mejía-Morelos, 2019).So this means: each theory names a different engine: values, group belonging, or giving back.→ Next: models made for organizations.

⭐ Three: the comprehensive action determination model, a multilevel model of pro-environmental motivation, and a process framework of macrodeterminants.Lülfs and Hahn (2014) add situational influences and habits. Norton and colleagues (2015) add controlled ("I have to") and autonomous ("I want to") motivation and outcomes at four levels. Young and colleagues (2015) add group, organizational and external factors. Our reading: each theory points to a lever: change attitudes and control (plan), make consequences visible (moral), use group norms (identity), give support first (exchange). The review only says integrating the theories could help design interventions.So this means: organizational models add the work context that general theories leave out.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Five theories explain green behavior through plans, moral norms, values, group identity or exchange; three organizational models add situation, motivation type and wider factors.1Planned behavior? Attitudes, subjective norms, control → intention → behavior.2Norm activation? Awareness + responsibility → personal norm → behavior.3Other three? Value-belief-norm; social identity; social exchange.4Organizational models? Lülfs & Hahn; Norton et al.; Young et al.Typical exam questions on this step:?What is the difference between the theory of planned behavior and the norm-activation model??Which theory fits: "My team does it, so I do it too"? (social identity)

⚠ The trap✗ The theory of planned behavior and the norm-activation model both say that norms cause green behavior, so they are the same.✓ Different norms, different engines. Planned behavior: the subjective norm (what others expect) is one input into a rational intention. Norm activation: the personal norm (my own moral obligation) is the engine, switched on by awareness of consequences and responsibility.
❓ This step answers: What do theories of work performance add to the explanation of green behavior?

What do theories of work performance add to the explanation of green behavior?Why this matters. If green behavior is work performance, what we know about the causes of performance should apply to it. The authors find it surprising that research neglected these well-established frameworks.In this step you learn:1Campbell's three direct determinants2Opportunity to perform3A simple check for any intervention

⭐ Declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge or skills, and motivation (Campbell's theory of work performance).Declarative knowledge: knowing what. Procedural knowledge or skills: knowing how. Motivation: choice behavior about the direction, intensity and duration of effort. These three mediate (carry) the effects of more distant determinants such as abilities, personality, attitudes, feelings and context. A green attitude may work through motivation; a training through knowledge and skills.So this means: an exam option saying a green attitude by itself leads to green behavior is wrong.→ Next: what the person cannot supply.

⭐ Because, besides ability and motivation, employees must have the opportunity to perform (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982).So contextual factors that facilitate or constrain green behavior must be considered next to individual ones. In the review, opportunities to perform and barriers to perform become the two faces of the context. Our example: a motivated, skilled employee cannot recycle if there are no bins.So this means: know, can, want, and a chance: all four are needed.→ Next: a practical check.

⭐ Ask which of the four parts it addresses, and whether that part was the problem.An information campaign addresses knowledge; a training, skills; feedback and incentives, motivation; recycling bins and job autonomy, opportunity. An intervention aimed at a part that was not the problem will not help. (This check is our way of using the theory.)So this means: diagnose first, then choose the lever.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Green behavior needs knowledge, skills and motivation (Campbell) plus the opportunity to perform; interventions should target the missing part.1Direct determinants? Declarative knowledge, skills, motivation.2Opportunity? Context gives opportunities or barriers (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982).3Using it? Match the intervention to the missing part.Typical exam questions on this step:?Diagnose: employees know how and want to, but there are no bins (opportunity)?Which statement is wrong? "Green attitudes are enough for green behavior."

⚠ The trap✗ If employees have green attitudes, green behavior will follow.✓ An attitude is an indirect determinant. It has to pass through motivation, and the behavior still needs knowledge, skills and the opportunity to perform.
❓ This step answers: How does action regulation theory describe the way employees steer their own green behavior?

How does action regulation theory describe the way employees steer their own green behavior?Why this matters. The authors see green behavior as active and dynamic: employees do not only react to rules, they set goals and act on them. Action regulation theory describes how.In this step you learn:1The sequence of steering an action2The levels of regulation, and habits3What this suggests for research and practice

⭐ A meta-theory of how people regulate goal-directed behavior at work, through a sequence of steps.A meta-theory is a broad theory that frames others. The sequence: developing and selecting goals, orienting yourself in the environment, planning, monitoring the execution, and processing feedback. Our example: Noor sets a goal to cut paper use, checks where paper is used, plans to go digital, watches her progress, and adjusts after a month.So this means: green behavior is steered like any goal at work.→ Next: the levels.

⭐ Through practice, regulation moves down to a lower level (routinization); awareness can move it back up (reintellectualization).The structure: regulation happens at several levels, from the sensorimotor or skills level, through flexible action patterns (routines), and the conscious intellectual level, to meta-cognitive heuristics. Routinization: developing energy conservation habits. Reintellectualization: becoming aware of wasteful behavior again.So this means: an exam option calling a habit the opposite of regulated behavior is wrong: it is behavior regulated at a lower level.→ Next: what follows.

⭐ The authors call for research on how employees set green goals and what helps or hinders their self-regulation; for practice, our reading is: build good habits and make bad ones conscious again.The authors recommend research on how employees develop and select green goals, and how regulation opportunities (autonomy, support) and regulation problems (obstacles, uncertainty, overload) affect them. Our reading for practice: to build a good habit, let people practise until it runs by itself; to break a bad one, first make it conscious again.So this means: changing a habit starts by bringing it back into awareness.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Action regulation theory: people steer green behavior through goals, planning, monitoring and feedback, at levels from habits to conscious thought.1What is it? A meta-theory of goal-directed regulation: goal, orient, plan, monitor, feedback.2Habits? Routinization moves behavior down; reintellectualization brings it back up.3Implications? Research on green goals and regulation; build or break habits.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which phase: Noor checks her printing counter weekly? (monitoring)?Which statement is wrong? "A habit is the opposite of regulated behavior."

⚠ The trap✗ A habit is the opposite of regulated behavior.✓ In action regulation theory a habit is behavior regulated at a lower level (flexible action patterns). It got there through practice, and it can be brought back to the conscious level.
STEP5
The empirical findings

What leads to green behavior, and what it brings

❓ This step answers: What are the individual antecedents of employee green behavior, and how strong are they?

What are the individual antecedents of employee green behavior, and how strong are they?Why this matters. This is the person side of the model, with numbers from a meta-analysis. A correlation says how strongly two things go together (0 not at all, 1 perfectly). A meta-analysis combines many studies into one estimate. The review reports ρ (in our words, the estimated average correlation across studies) and k (the number of studies).In this step you learn:1A warning about cause and effect2Demographics, attitudes, norms and intentions3Job attitudes and personality4The gaps: knowledge, skills and feelings

⭐ As associations, not proven causes: most studies are cross-sectional.A cross-sectional study measures everything at one moment, so it cannot show what causes what. The review warns that causality still has to be shown with experiments and longitudinal studies (measuring the same people several times). ❓ This step answers: How has employee green behavior been studied and measured, and what does that mean for how far we can trust the findings?

How has employee green behavior been studied and measured, and how far can we trust the findings?Why this matters. A finding is only as good as the study behind it. This tab tells you how to read every "effect" in this review. A research design is the plan of a study.In this step you learn:1The designs used so far2The few experiments3Problems with measures: jingle and jangle4What theory and methods still need

⭐ Mostly with cross-sectional self-report surveys, more recently with diary and experience-sampling studies.Self-report: a person's own answer about themselves. Diary and experience sampling studies ask the same people short questions many times, so they show how behavior changes within a person. Our analogy: judging a new medicine, you first notice who takes it and who feels better; later you run trials. Studies with several sources, levels and waves are still very rare.So this means: read most findings in this review as relations, not proven causes.→ Next: the exceptions.

⭐ Emotions, self-concordant goals and feedback can change green behavior, in a handful of studies.Emotions: in three quasi-experimental studies (groups compared without random assignment) with fictional news videos, anger and fear seemed to raise green behavior and sadness to lower it (Russell & Ashkanasy, 2021). Self-concordance: in two intervention studies, linking energy use and commuting to personally important goals raised intentions compared with control groups (Unsworth & McNeill, 2017; reading 8). Feedback: more feedback went with more later green behavior; employees with weak autonomous motivation gained most when they also had high goal commitment (Davis and colleagues, 2020). Two lab experiments: green behavior (for example printing on recycled paper) was highest when both environment-specific transformational leadership and green HR practices were high (Peng and colleagues, 2020). A qualitative (interview-based) study found obstacles to biodiversity involvement: a complex issue, little corporate commitment, initiatives handed to outsiders, no training (Boiral and colleagues, 2019).So this means: the few experiments are the kind of studies meeting 4 turns to.→ Next: the measures.

⭐ Many are self-report scales, often newly written, and scales with the same label can measure different things.The jingle fallacy: same label, so we wrongly assume the same thing. The jangle fallacy: different labels, so we wrongly assume different things. In Francoeur and colleagues' (2021) review of 53 studies, 42% wrote new items and 45% reused published ones. Example of jingle: four scales all called "organizational citizenship behavior for the environment" measure three, one, six and three different dimensions. Citizenship behavior for the environment is studied most. The fix: theory-based, non-redundant, more objective measures, asking about the last day rather than the last year.So this means: an exam option calling jingle and jangle two words for the same mistake is wrong: they mirror each other.→ Next: theory's to-do list.

⭐ Designs that fit causal questions, more than one source, competing explanations, and better measures.Cross-sectional surveys mostly cannot test mediation, so use experiments, interventions with one component at a time, diaries and studies with three or more waves, with people nested in teams or organizations. The authors' conclusion (pp. 486–487), in our words. Employee green behavior is an emerging and important performance construct. Most published studies used single-source data from cross-sectional surveys and looked at possible antecedents. To grow up as a field, it has four jobs: (a) see and measure green behavior as a family of distinct performance dimensions that people steer themselves; (b) test models in which it counts as performance that helps or hurts the organization's environmental aims; (c) use stronger designs: experiments, studies over time and experience sampling; and (d) look at causes and results at every level, from the individual to society and culture.

🧠 Why this matters

For the planet, the argument is simple: Stern (2000) noted that what organizations do is the biggest direct source of many environmental problems, and what organizations do is carried out by employees. For a psychologist, the review turns a moral topic into a workable one: green behavior can be defined, measured, explained with known theories, and supported with known tools (selection, training, feedback, leadership, work design). It also keeps you honest: much of what we "know" rests on one-moment self-report surveys.

🧩 The three in-depth questions of the meeting, from this reading

What are possible obstacles for organizational sustainability? In the person: unfavorable attitudes, no perceived norm, low self-efficacy, low awareness, lack of time and knowledge, unwillingness to change habits. In the context: a non-green culture, job constraints, lack of resources, lack of support from supervisors and colleagues. And possibly the costs of green behavior itself: fatigue, conflict, resistance, boycott. Could employees become activists at work? The taxonomy says yes: taking initiative includes lobbying and activism, environmental voice and putting environmental interests first, and it involves personal risk; the review also raises that colleagues and supervisors who care less may react negatively. The other readings add to both questions; the third in-depth question (interventions for older employees only, or younger too) belongs to readings 3 and 4.
