Supporting the aging workforce

Truxillo, D. M., Cadiz, D. M., & Hammer, L. B. (2015). Supporting the aging workforce: A review and recommendations for workplace intervention research. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 2, 351–381.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and why it matters

An older workforce, and almost no tested interventions

❓ This step answers: Why does the aging workforce matter, what gap does the review address, and who counts as an older worker?
📈 Four quick decisions: each one fills a box of the review's starting point.
Authors
Donald Truxillo and Leslie Hammer (Portland State University) and David Cadiz (Oregon Nurses Foundation), United States
Type
Review article in the Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior (2015): summarizes research on aging at work and proposes interventions to be tested
Built for
Challenging work psychology to develop and test research-based interventions that support older workers and an age-diverse workforce
Main message
Aging brings losses, gains and shifts; theory suggests many useful interventions, but almost none have been tested with age in focus

Some words first. An intervention is a planned change meant to improve something, such as a training or a new policy. A review summarizes the research on a topic. Age-diverse means mixing people of different ages. IO/OB is short for industrial-organizational psychology and organizational behavior: the research fields of work psychology. In the picture: four boxes: the trend, the gap, the definition and the outcomes.

Think of a library full of books on why a bridge wears out, with hardly a page on how to repair it (our analogy). People are aging more slowly and will need to work longer to fund a longer retirement and keep pension systems going; many countries are raising the retirement age, and the economic downturn left many with too little savings. So workforces get older, and older and younger people work together as never before. As many as 60% of workers aged 45 to 74 said they worked mainly for financial reasons. Organizations and societies need to keep these workers healthy and well, and keep relations between generations good.

Research on age at work has boomed: motives, stereotypes, age climate, caregiving, retirement. Age has moved from a control variable (something researchers merely correct for) to a main topic. But intervention research at work has almost never focused on age, and there is almost no research on which actual interventions or objective policies help workers of different ages. The authors want to fill that gap with theory-based proposals.

Who is "older" depends on job, culture and law; in the US legal age protections start at 40, and the idea shifts as people age more slowly. The authors use older worker for people approaching retirement age or working a bit beyond it, in the US people in their late 50s and 60s, and consider changes across the normal working life (early 20s to 70s). Because individuals age differently, changes within one person over time may matter more than comparing age groups.

The review aims at better proximal outcomes (work-life balance, job satisfaction, engagement, burnout, strain) and distal ones (safety, performance, health, work ability, retirement, turnover, life satisfaction). Its route: what changes with age inside the person (step 2), how people adapt (step 3), the social context (step 4), interventions (step 5) and research needs (step 6).

⚠ The trap✗ The review evaluates which workplace interventions for older workers work best.✓ It finds that almost no such interventions have been tested. It reviews what is known about aging at work and proposes interventions for future research.
📝 Exam-style questionGive two reasons why the workforce in industrialized countries is getting older, and explain what the authors mean when they say age should move beyond being a control variable.

Model answer: People are aging more slowly and must work longer to support themselves in a longer retirement and to keep retirement systems going; many countries raise the retirement age; and many workers have too little retirement savings after the economic downturn. "Beyond a control variable": in earlier research age was only statistically corrected for; the authors want it treated as a focus of study and of interventions, asking what employers and societies can do to support workers as they age.

STEP2
Within-person changes · Table 1

Losses, gains and shifts

❓ This step answers: What changes physically, cognitively, emotionally, in personality and in motivation as people age?
🔽🔼 Sort six changes: does it decline, increase, or is it mixed?

Within-person changes happen inside one person over time. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. A trait is a stable characteristic, such as conscientiousness (careful, reliable) or neuroticism (prone to worry). In the picture: three columns: declines, increases, and mixed.

Think of a car that gets slower off the line but has a better navigation system and a calmer driver (our analogy). Aging is not one long decline. Table 1 lists changes in five areas: physical, cognitive, affective (emotions), personality and motivation. Some go down, some go up, some shift. And people differ a lot in how they age, more so in later years.

Sensory, muscular, aerobic and immune functions decline, and the body is slower to restore balance (homeostasis), so heat, cold, humidity or night shifts take longer to recover from. Clinical health problems rise with age (disabilities especially after 50), but self-reported physical problems do not. Older workers have fewer injuries but recover more slowly. There is little research linking these declines to lower job performance, which suggests people adapt.

Fluid intelligence (processing speed, working memory, attention) declines; perceptual speed starts to fall at 25 and declines significantly after 60 (Schaie, 1994). Crystallized intelligence (knowledge, skills, wisdom) may peak around 60 and declines only gradually. This may explain why age and job performance show no consistent negative relation: Gc gains may offset Gf losses. Jobs needing peak Gf (air traffic control) fit older workers less; jobs using accumulated knowledge (senior managers) fit well. Education, good health and complex jobs make cognitive aging more resilient.

Emotional well-being and emotion regulation improve with age (the positivity effect): older adults appraise stress less negatively and focus on positive cues, so social stressors may hit them less, though extreme or chronic stressors may hit harder because recovery takes longer. Conscientiousness and agreeableness rise and neuroticism falls; within extraversion social dominance rises and social vitality falls. A 15-year study found these personality changes went together with rising job satisfaction (Wille and colleagues, 2014).

A meta-analysis found age related positively to intrinsic motives (accomplishment, connection, autonomy), helping others and contributing to society (generativity) and job security, and negatively to growth (achievement, mastery) and extrinsic motives (pay, advancement) (Kooij and colleagues, 2011). Interest in training falls too. Motivation does not simply decline; it changes.

Interventions should compensate for losses, use the gains, and fit work to changing motives, while treating older workers as individuals rather than one group. Next: the theories that explain how people adapt.

📝 Exam-style questionA manager says older employees are less motivated. Use the review to correct this statement.

Model answer: Motivation does not simply decline with age; it shifts. A meta-analysis (Kooij and colleagues, 2011) found that age relates positively to intrinsic motives (accomplishment, connection with others, autonomy), to generativity (helping people and contributing to society) and to job security, and negatively to growth motives (achievement, mastery) and extrinsic motives (pay, advancement). Training interest does decline. So older employees may be less driven by pay and promotion but more by meaningful, autonomous work and helping others.

STEP3
Life-span development theories

Choose, invest, work around

❓ This step answers: Which theories explain how people adapt to aging, and what do they predict at work?
🧭 Follow Fatima, a 61-year-old nurse (our example): match what she does to a strategy, then two questions.

Life-span development theories describe how people change and adapt across their whole life. To adapt is to adjust so that you keep functioning well. In the picture: four tiles: three strategies of one theory and one other theory.

Think of a footballer in his thirties (our example): he plays fewer minutes, trains smarter, and relies on positioning instead of speed. The leading theory, selection, optimization and compensation (SOC) (Baltes & Baltes, 1990), says people use three strategies all through life to match their resources to the demands. Selection: choose and prioritize a few goals instead of spreading resources thin. Optimization: focus effort and resources on reaching them. Compensation: find strategies to offset declines and keep performing.

Support at work is robust. Using SOC strategies goes with better work ability, job satisfaction, perceived career success and well-being, goal attainment, supervisor-rated performance and a focus on future opportunities, and with less job and family stress and work-family conflict. SOC also buffers poor conditions: weak family-friendly policies or supervisor support, low-complexity work, high problem-solving demands.

Socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensen and colleagues, 1999): how much time you feel you have left steers your social goals. With open-ended time (younger people) knowledge goals come first; with limited time (older people) emotional goals come first. It explains why older workers tend to have more positive job attitudes (they focus on positive experiences and avoid negative ones), the positivity effect of step 2, and why job features such as task and skill variety affect burnout and turnover differently with age.

Kanfer and Ackerman (2004) name four patterns that change work motivation: loss (declining resources such as fluid intelligence), growth (gains such as crystallized intelligence), reorganization (changes in emotions and other non-ability traits) and exchange (shifts in which motives matter, through personality, self-concept, interests and values). Kanfer and colleagues (2013) separate motivation to work, at work and to retire, each shaped by the person, finances and health, work conditions such as age climate, and social norms such as the usual retirement age.

Some adults keep their abilities into very old age; others decline earlier. The successful aging literature studies what helps people maintain abilities, from health and longevity to adaptation, control and mastery. Its key contribution at work: bringing in the life-span view (SOC, SST), which says development depends on age and non-age factors and that positive adaptation is possible. Measures of successful workplace aging exist, but what leads to it still needs research.

These theories are the backbone of the interventions in step 5: teach SOC strategies, redesign work to fit shifting motives, and match HR practices to life stage. Next: the social context that older workers face.

⚠ The trap✗ Selection in SOC theory means that organizations select which older workers to keep.✓ In SOC theory the person selects: choosing and prioritizing a few goals to focus resources on. Optimization and compensation are also strategies of the individual.
📝 Exam-style questionUse socioemotional selectivity theory to explain why older workers tend to report more positive job attitudes than younger workers.

Model answer: SST says that perceived time left steers goal selection. Older people perceive their time as more limited, so they prioritize emotion-related goals over knowledge-related goals. At work this means older workers focus on positive, emotionally meaningful experiences and tend to ignore or avoid negative aspects of the job and work environment. That focus is expected to show up as more positive job attitudes, which is in line with meta-analytic findings (Ng & Feldman, 2010).

STEP4
Stereotypes, discrimination, climate, teams, leaders, work-life

How others see and treat older workers

❓ This step answers: Which age stereotypes hold, how age shapes decisions, teams and leadership, and what we know about work-life support across ages?
🗣️ Fact or myth? Six findings; each answer fills a tile.

A stereotype is a generalized belief about a group. Explicit stereotypes are ones people know they hold; implicit ones work without awareness. Discrimination is treating people worse because of group membership. A climate is what people in an organization share as their view of its policies and practices. In the picture: six tiles, one per area.

Think of a new team (our example) where everyone first notices each other's age, then learns who is funny, careful or creative, and age stops mattering. Older workers are shaped not only by their own changes but by how others see and treat them: stereotypes, discrimination, climate, team composition, leadership and work-life policies.

Older people are generally seen as warm but not very competent (Cuddy & Fiske, 2002). Stereotypes of older workers: lower performance and learning ability, resistance to change, shorter tenure, higher cost (Posthuma & Campion, 2009). Meta-analyses: apart from less interest in training, most are not supported; older workers have more positive job attitudes and more citizenship behavior. Some stereotypes are positive (more reliable, more conscientious, less neurotic), and replications show older and younger workers rated similarly for hiring, training and promotion.

Still, higher age goes with worse decisions on advancement, selection and evaluation (Bal and colleagues, 2011). One possible reason: implicit stereotypes. Bias is stronger for jobs seen as "young" and when raters are cognitively busy, and weaker when raters have job-relevant information. Also studied: meta-stereotypes (only one workplace study) and age diversity climate. In 128 companies more age diversity went with a stronger age discrimination climate and lower performance (Kunze and colleagues, 2011); in 93 German firms inclusive HR practices went with a more positive age climate, which related to performance and lower collective turnover intentions (Böhm and colleagues, 2014).

Being a different age from your group goes with less integration and more turnover, and subgroups can form (faultlines, weaker for age than for sex or ethnicity). But surface differences such as age fade as people learn each other's deep traits, and intergenerational contact reduces ageism. Age diversity hurt performance on routine tasks and helped on complex decisions (Wegge and colleagues, 2008). Leaders: older leaders seen as low in generativity were rated less effective, those high in it as effective as younger leaders (Zacher and colleagues, 2011); leader age mattered only for followers with negative age stereotypes (Zacher & Bal, 2012).

Many work-life policies exist (unpaid leave, flexible times, part-time work, telecommuting, compressed weeks, job sharing, child care and eldercare support, employee assistance), but few studies go beyond correlations and none the authors knew of focused on older workers. The one study comparing caregiving situations found supports related most strongly to satisfaction for employees with eldercare duties in an unsupportive work-family culture (Sahibzada and colleagues, 2005); they were on average older (46).

The evidence points to levers: give raters real information, build an inclusive age climate, create positive contact between ages, develop leaders' generativity, and tailor work-life support. Next: what has been tried, and what the authors propose.

⚠ The trap✗ Age stereotypes do not matter, because meta-analyses show most of them are false.✓ The stereotypes are mostly not supported, but decisions about older people are still more negative (Bal and colleagues, 2011), possibly through implicit stereotypes, and especially for jobs stereotyped as young.
📝 Exam-style questionA company forms new mixed-age project teams and worries about conflict between generations. What does the review predict, and which conditions help?

Model answer: At first, surface differences such as age may stand out, and being a different age from the group can reduce integration; subgroups (faultlines) could form, although age faultlines are weaker than those for sex or ethnicity. Over time, as members learn about each other's deep characteristics (personality, ability), surface differences become less important (Harrison and colleagues). Positive intergenerational contact reduces ageism. Age diversity helps more on complex decision tasks than on routine ones (Wegge and colleagues, 2008). An inclusive age climate, supported by inclusive HR practices and supervisors, also helps.

STEP5
Existing studies · Table 2

What could support an aging workforce?

❓ This step answers: What do the few existing intervention studies show, and which interventions do the authors propose, based on which theory?
🧰 Match six cases to the type of intervention in Table 2.

A randomized controlled trial assigns people by chance to an intervention or a control group, so differences can be read as effects. Atheoretical means without a theory explaining why it should work. A bundle is a set of HR practices that go together. In the picture: six tiles for six intervention types.

Think of a toolbox where every tool has a note saying which problem it is for and why it should work (our analogy). That is Table 2: ten intervention categories, each with examples and the theory or findings behind it. The authors stress that older workers are not one group, and that interventions must take coworkers, supervisors and change over time into account.

Only two examples were found, both health programmes. Hughes and colleagues (2011) randomly assigned 423 university employees over 40 to printed materials, an online health risk check with personal coaching, or an online check with training modules. Coaching improved fruit and vegetable intake and physical activity; the training modules improved waist size. Strijk and colleagues (2013) offered 730 Dutch hospital workers over 45 yoga, workouts and lifestyle coaching: no effects, except for those who actually took part. Both had strong designs but no psychological theory of aging to explain how they worked.

SOC training: teach older workers to select skills and tasks that fit their abilities and interests, prepare supervisors and teams, and decide which tasks can be handed off; SOC strategies have improved health in bridge employment and work ability. Work redesign: older workers react better to more autonomy and skill variety and less task variety; support them in crafting their jobs, and use mentoring for their generativity motives, which also helps younger workers and keeps organizational memory.

Positive relations between groups: positive exposure to other ages and structured discussions to reduce explicit and implicit stereotypes; train supervisors and teams to build an age diversity climate, communicated from the top. Age-supportive HR: younger workers do better with training opportunities, older workers with maintenance practices such as flexible schedules. Kooij and colleagues' four bundles: development (raise functioning, e.g. training), maintenance (sustain it, e.g. flexible schedules), utilization (help return to earlier levels, e.g. lateral moves), accommodative (function at an adequate but lower level, e.g. part-time work). Studies so far concern perceived, not actual, HR practices.

Work-life support: flexible arrangements, part-time work, telecommuting and eldercare support may help older workers and work through positive social exchange; a model is the family-supportive supervisor training of Hammer and colleagues (2011), after which workers reported higher satisfaction, lower turnover intentions and better health. Training design: extra time, smaller groups, self-paced learning, a learning goal orientation, error management training; frame training as an intrinsic benefit and link it to prior knowledge. Paradox: age-specific training may feed stereotypes; involving older workers in its design may help.

Every proposal rests on theory, but almost none has been tested with age in focus. The other categories (supervisor training for safety, ergonomics, health promotion, career management, Total Worker Health) are in the table below. Next: how to organize and research these interventions.

📝 Exam-style questionWhy do the authors criticize the two existing health promotion interventions for older workers, even though both had strong designs?

Model answer: Both (Hughes and colleagues, 2011; Strijk and colleagues, 2013) used randomized designs and good measures, but neither was based on psychological theory about aging or measured psychological processes. So they cannot explain why the intervention did or did not work for older workers (Strijk's programme only helped people who actually complied). The authors want interventions grounded in theories such as SOC and SST, and studies that compare older and younger workers.

STEP6
The prevention lens · Table 3

Prevent, control, accommodate

❓ This step answers: How can interventions for an aging workforce be organized, and what must research do next?
🗂️ Classify three interventions, then three questions about research.

Longitudinal research follows the same people over time. Multilevel designs look at individuals, groups and organizations together. A generational difference is a difference between people born in different periods, as opposed to a change caused by getting older. In the picture: three tiers, and three research needs below.

Think of dental care (our analogy): brushing for everyone, a filling when a problem starts, a crown when the tooth is damaged. The authors borrow this prevention model from health promotion to organize interventions for an aging workforce by target group and aim: prevention versus accommodation. A second message: support workers of all ages, because today's young workers are tomorrow's older workers, and a positive age diversity climate benefits everyone.

Primary: for all workers, preventing problems so people reach the end of their career with the resources to be healthy and effective (an age-supportive climate, successful aging techniques, less exposure to hazardous chemicals, health promotion). Secondary: for people in mid to late career, reducing the impact of age-related declines (short-term memory interventions, ergonomics, flexible schedules) and using gains such as generativity (mentoring). Tertiary: when a condition cannot be reversed, accommodating workers with reduced work ability to retain them (hearing aids, glasses).

Most research is correlational. To find causes, use experimental and quasi-experimental designs with intervention and control groups, at individual, group or organizational level; longitudinal studies with very long time horizons, to see change within people and to separate aging from generational differences; measures rare in work psychology but crucial for age, such as objective health; and collaboration with sociology, economics, demography, medicine and ergonomics.

Also in Table 3: wise interventions, brief, precise and cheap so even small employers can use them (Walton, 2014), for example supervisor tips on a smartphone. Global research, because country and culture matter: personality matures earlier where people start working earlier, and a country's retirement age affects work motivation. Age differences in adopting versus maintaining change: younger people may start change more easily, older people keep it up better (Rothman, 2006). Holistic interventions in and outside work. A better grasp of what chronological age stands for (health, cognition, motives, subjective age). And technology as a friend: adapt technology to older workers, not only workers to technology.

The review ends with a challenge: identify what employers and societies can do for an aging workforce, and move beyond age as a statistical control variable. Next: the review in one view.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

What are the best workplace interventions for supporting an aging workforce? The honest answer: we do not know yet which work best, because they have hardly been tested. The most promising, grounded in theory: SOC training; work redesign with more autonomy and skill variety, less task variety, and mentoring; positive contact between ages and an inclusive age diversity climate; age-supportive HR bundles (development, maintenance, utilization, accommodative); work-life support such as flexibility and eldercare help; age-friendly training; supervisor training for safety and health; ergonomics; health promotion; career management; and integrated Total Worker Health programmes. Organize them as primary, secondary and tertiary, and design them for workers of all ages. What is sustainable employability? This review does not use the term; it speaks of supporting workers' well-being, health, work ability and continued work as they age. Reading 3 connects this to engaging aging.
