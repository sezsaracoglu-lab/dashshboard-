Why does the aging workforce matter, what gap does the review address, and who counts as an older worker?Why this matters. Reading 3 proposed a model of engaging aging; this review gives the full picture of what changes with age and asks what organizations can actually do. It is written by Donald Truxillo and Leslie Hammer (Portland State University) and David Cadiz (Oregon Nurses Foundation) in the Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior (2015).In this step you learn:1Why workforces are getting older2The gap: almost no tested interventions3Who counts as older4The outcomes the review aims at

⭐ Because people work longer, and older and younger people work together as never before.People age more slowly and need to work longer to fund a longer retirement and keep pension systems going; many countries raise the retirement age, and the economic downturn left many with too little savings. As many as 60% of workers aged 45 to 74 said they worked mainly for financial reasons. Organizations need to keep these workers healthy and well, and keep relations between generations good.So this means: an older, age-mixed workforce is a lasting reality, not a phase.→ Next: what research has missed.

⭐ Hardly any studies test real programmes or policies to see whether they help workers of different ages.Research on age at work has boomed (motives, stereotypes, age climate, caregiving, retirement), and age moved from a control variable (something researchers merely correct for) to a main topic. But intervention research has almost never focused on age. An intervention is a planned change meant to improve something, such as a training or a new policy. Our analogy: a library full of books on why a bridge wears out, with hardly a page on how to repair it.So this means: an exam option saying the review evaluates which interventions work best is wrong: it proposes interventions to be tested.→ Next: who is "older"?

⭐ People approaching retirement age or working a bit beyond it; in the US, people in their late 50s and 60s.Who is older depends on job, culture and law; in the US, legal age protections start at 40. The authors also consider changes across the whole working life (early 20s to 70s). Because individuals age differently, changes within one person over time may matter more than comparing age groups.So this means: age is a moving, individual idea, not a fixed category.→ Next: the goals.

⭐ Both near outcomes (proximal: satisfaction, engagement, strain) and further-away outcomes (distal: safety, performance, health, work ability, retirement).Proximal also includes work-life balance and burnout; distal also turnover and life satisfaction. Work ability (our gloss: how well a worker can meet the job's demands).So this means: interventions should be judged on both near and far outcomes.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Workforces are aging; research on age boomed, but age-focused interventions are almost untested, so the review proposes them.1Why it matters? Longer working lives; age-mixed workplaces; financial need.2Gap? Almost no tested interventions with age in focus.3Older worker? Late 50s and 60s in the US; individual differences matter.4Outcomes? Proximal (satisfaction, strain) and distal (health, work ability).Typical exam questions on this step:?Which statement is correct? (the review proposes interventions; few have been tested)?What does it mean that age moved from a control variable to a main topic?

STEP2
Within-person changes · Table 1

Losses, gains and shifts

❓ This step answers: What changes physically, cognitively, emotionally, in personality and in motivation as people age?

What changes physically, cognitively, emotionally, in personality and in motivation as people age?Why this matters. Aging is not one long decline. Within-person changes happen inside one person over time; a meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. Table 1 lists changes in five areas: some go down, some go up, some shift. And people differ a lot in how they age, more so later in life. Our analogy: a car that gets slower off the line but has a better navigation system and a calmer driver.In this step you learn:1Physical and cognitive changes2Emotional and personality changes3Motivational shifts4What this means for interventions

⭐ Sensory, muscular, aerobic and immune functions decline, and the body restores balance more slowly; yet older workers have fewer injuries.The body is slower to restore homeostasis (its balance), so heat, cold, humidity or night shifts take longer to recover from. Clinical health problems rise with age (disabilities especially after 50), but self-reported physical problems do not. Older workers have fewer injuries but recover more slowly. Little research links these declines to lower job performance, which suggests people adapt.So this means: physical decline is real but rarely shows up as worse performance.→ Next: thinking.

⭐ Fluid intelligence declines; crystallized intelligence may peak around 60 and declines only gradually.Fluid intelligence (Gf): processing speed, working memory, attention; perceptual speed starts to fall at 25 and declines significantly after 60 (Schaie, 1994). Crystallized intelligence (Gc): knowledge, skills, wisdom. Gc gains may offset Gf losses, which may explain why age and job performance show no consistent negative relation. Jobs needing peak Gf (air traffic control) fit older workers less; jobs using accumulated knowledge (senior managers) fit well. More education, good health and complex jobs go with more resilient cognitive aging (Rizzuto and colleagues, 2012).So this means: an exam option saying older workers perform worse because Gf declines is wrong.→ Next: emotions.

⭐ Emotional well-being and emotion regulation improve: the positivity effect.Explanations offered: expertise from experience, a shift to emotional goals (step 3), and strategies to avoid negative events. Scheibe and Zacher (2013) propose that older adults may be less affected by social stressors, because they appraise stress less negatively at first (emotion generation) and focus on positive cues, but more by extreme or chronic stressors, because recovery takes longer (emotion regulation).So this means: older workers handle everyday stress better, but may suffer more from severe or chronic stress.→ Next: personality.

⭐ Conscientiousness and agreeableness rise, neuroticism falls; within extraversion, social dominance rises and social vitality falls.The Big Five are openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism; a facet is a narrower part of one trait. The study: Wille and colleagues (2014) followed young professionals for 15 years. What they found: those who became more extraverted and conscientious and less neurotic also became more satisfied with their jobs; satisfaction in turn influenced changes in agreeableness. In plain words: personality and work shape each other.So this means: personality keeps maturing at work, mostly in a favorable direction.→ Next: motivation.

⭐ It shifts: intrinsic motives, generativity and job security rise, while growth and extrinsic motives fall (Kooij and colleagues, 2011); interest in training also falls (Ng & Feldman, 2012).Intrinsic motives: accomplishment, connection, autonomy. Generativity: helping others and contributing to society, including the next generation. Growth: achievement, mastery. Extrinsic: pay, advancement. Our example: a 60-year-old may care less about the next promotion and more about meaningful work and passing on what she knows.So this means: motivation does not simply decline; it changes direction.→ Next: the implication.

⭐ Compensate for losses, use the gains, fit work to changing motives, and treat older workers as individuals.Because losses, gains and shifts differ by person and by job, one programme for all older workers will miss; interventions should offset declines, use growing knowledge and generativity, and fit work to new motives. Table 1 below lists all changes. Our example: give an experienced technician fewer rushed tasks, more mentoring and more say in how she works.So this means: good interventions follow the actual pattern of losses, gains and shifts.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ With age: physical functions and fluid intelligence decline; crystallized intelligence, emotion regulation and favorable personality traits grow; motives shift toward meaning and generativity.1Physical? Decline, slower recovery, fewer injuries.2Thinking? Gf down, Gc up to about 60; no consistent performance drop.3Emotions? Positivity effect; vulnerable to chronic stress (proposed).4Personality? More conscientious and agreeable, less neurotic.5Motivation? Intrinsic and generativity up; growth and extrinsic down.6Interventions? Compensate, use gains, fit motives, individualize.Typical exam questions on this step:?Why is there no consistent negative relation between age and performance? (Gc may offset Gf)?Which motives increase with age? (intrinsic, generativity, job security)

STEP3
Life-span development theories

Choose, invest, work around

❓ This step answers: Which theories explain how people adapt to aging, and what do they predict at work?

Which theories explain how people adapt to aging, and what do they predict at work?Why this matters. The changes of step 2 do not just happen to people; people adapt to them. These theories explain how, and they are the backbone of the interventions in step 5. Our example: a footballer in his thirties plays fewer minutes, trains smarter and relies on positioning instead of speed.In this step you learn:1Selection, optimization and compensation2Socioemotional selectivity3How work motivation changes4Successful aging

⭐ A life-span theory in which people use selection, optimization and compensation to match their resources to demands (Baltes & Baltes, 1990).SSelection: choose and prioritize a few goals.OOptimization: focus effort and resources on reaching them.CCompensation: find strategies to offset declines and keep performing.People use these strategies all through life; with age, as resources shrink, choosing a few goals instead of spreading resources thin keeps performance up.So this means: an exam option saying selection means organizations select older workers is wrong: the person selects.→ Next: does it work at work?

⭐ Yes, robustly: SOC goes with better work ability, satisfaction, performance and well-being, and buffers poor conditions.Using SOC strategies goes with better work ability, job satisfaction, perceived career success, well-being, goal attainment, supervisor-rated performance and a focus on future opportunities, and with less stress and work-family conflict. SOC also buffers poor conditions: weak family-friendly policies or supervisor support, low-complexity work, high problem-solving demands.So this means: SOC is a strong candidate for training.→ Next: a theory about time.

⭐ How much time you feel you have left steers your goals: open-ended time favors knowledge goals, limited time favors emotional goals (Carstensen and colleagues, 1999).It explains why older workers tend to have more positive job attitudes (confirmed in meta-analyses, Ng & Feldman, 2010), the positivity effect, and why job features such as task and skill variety affect burnout and turnover differently with age.So this means: older workers invest in meaningful relationships and positive experiences.→ Next: motivation.

⭐ Through four patterns of adult development: loss, growth, reorganization and exchange (Kanfer & Ackerman, 2004). Kanfer and colleagues (2013) add that motivation to work, at work and to retire differ.Loss: declining resources such as Gf. Growth: gains such as Gc. Reorganization: changes in emotions and other non-ability traits. Exchange: shifts in which motives matter, through changes in personality, self-concept, interests and values. Kanfer and colleagues (2013) separate motivation to work, at work and to retire, each shaped by the person, finances and health, work conditions such as age climate, and social norms such as the usual retirement age.So this means: motivation changes form across a career; it does not just rise or fall.→ Next: aging well.

⭐ The study of what helps people keep their abilities, from health and longevity to adaptation, control and mastery.Some adults keep their abilities into very old age; others decline earlier. At work, its key contribution is the life-span view: development depends on age and non-age factors, and positive adaptation is possible. Measures of successful workplace aging exist, but what leads to it still needs research.So this means: aging well is partly in people's and organizations' hands.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ People adapt through SOC strategies and shifting goals (socioemotional selectivity); motivation reorganizes; successful aging shows positive adaptation is possible.1SOC? Select, optimize, compensate (Baltes & Baltes, 1990).2Evidence? Robust; buffers poor conditions.3Socioemotional selectivity theory (SST)? Limited time → emotional goals; more positive attitudes.4Motivation? Loss, growth, reorganization, exchange; to work, at work, to retire.5Successful aging? Positive adaptation is possible.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which statement is wrong? "Selection in SOC means organizations select older workers."?What does socioemotional selectivity theory predict for older workers' goals? (emotional goals)

⚠ The trap✗ Selection in SOC theory means that organizations select which older workers to keep.✓ In SOC theory the person selects: choosing and prioritizing a few goals to focus resources on. Optimization and compensation are also strategies of the individual.
STEP4
Stereotypes, discrimination, climate, teams, leaders, work-life

How others see and treat older workers

❓ This step answers: Which age stereotypes hold, how age shapes decisions, teams and leadership, and what we know about work-life support across ages?

Which age stereotypes hold, how does age shape decisions, teams and leadership, and what is known about work-life support?Why this matters. Older workers are shaped not only by their own changes but by how others see and treat them. This step gives you the evidence to answer "old people resist change" in an exam or a team.In this step you learn:1Stereotypes and decisions2Age climate3Teams and leaders4Work-life support

⭐ Mostly not: apart from less interest in training, meta-analyses do not support the common stereotypes.Older people are generally seen as warm but not very competent (Cuddy & Fiske, 2002). Stereotypes of older workers: lower performance and learning ability, resistance to change, shorter tenure, higher cost (Posthuma & Campion, 2009). Apart from less interest in training (Ng & Feldman, 2012), meta-analytic evidence does not support them. Older workers have more positive job attitudes and more citizenship behavior (helping beyond the job description). Some stereotypes are positive (more reliable, Bal and colleagues, 2011; more conscientious and less neurotic in separate studies). Replications show older and younger workers are rated similarly for hiring, training and promotion (Weiss & Maurer, 2004).So this means: most negative beliefs about older workers are myths.→ Next: do myths still matter?

⭐ Yes: higher age goes with worse decisions on advancement, selection and evaluation (Bal and colleagues, 2011), although older workers perform about as well; one possible reason is implicit (unconscious) stereotypes.Judgments were more negative when raters lacked job-relevant information and when older and younger people were rated together (Finkelstein and colleagues, 1995); older candidates were rated less suitable for "young" jobs (based on only two studies). Bias showed more for jobs stereotyped as young and when raters were cognitively busy (Perry and colleagues, 1996). Implicit stereotypes work without awareness. In plain words: with little information and little time, the age label does the judging.So this means: an exam option saying stereotypes do not matter because they are false is wrong.→ Next: the climate.

⭐ Age diversity can bring a discrimination climate that hurts performance, while inclusive HR practices build a positive age diversity climate.A climate is the shared view people in an organization hold of its policies and practices. The problem: in 128 companies, more age diversity went with a stronger age discrimination climate, which went with lower company performance and commitment (Kunze and colleagues, 2011). The lever: in 93 German firms, inclusive HR practices went with a more positive age diversity climate, related to company performance and collective turnover intentions (Böhm and colleagues, 2014). New: meta-stereotypes, what one age group thinks others think of it; only one workplace study exists.So this means: HR can turn age diversity from a risk into a strength.→ Next: teams.

⭐ Risks and chances: age differences can split teams at first, but fade as people learn each other's deep traits; diversity helps complex tasks.Being a different age from your group (relational demography) goes with less integration and more turnover, and subgroups can form (faultlines, weaker for age than for sex or ethnicity). Surface differences such as age seem to fade as people learn personality and ability, and intergenerational contact may reduce ageism. The study: Wegge and colleagues (2008). What they found: age diversity hurt performance on routine tasks and helped on complex decision tasks. Our example: a new team first notices age, then learns who is funny, careful or creative.So this means: mix ages for complex work, and give people time to know each other.→ Next: leaders.

⭐ It depends on the leader's behavior and on followers' stereotypes.Older leaders seen as low in generativity were rated less effective; those high in it were as effective as younger leaders (Zacher and colleagues, 2011). Among German professors and their assistants, leader age mattered only for assistants with negative age stereotypes, who rated older leaders as less proactive and more passive-avoidant (Zacher & Bal, 2012). In plain words: help older leaders show generativity, and work on followers' stereotypes.So this means: leader age itself is not the problem.→ Next: work-life support.

⭐ Many policies exist, but few studies go beyond correlations, and none the authors knew of focused on older workers.Policies: unpaid leave, flexible times, part-time work, telecommuting, compressed weeks, job sharing, child care and eldercare support, employee assistance. The one study comparing caregiving situations found supports related most strongly to satisfaction for employees with eldercare duties in an unsupportive work-family culture (Sahibzada and colleagues, 2005); they were on average older (46). The authors suggest these employees had more job experience and more access to formal supports.So this means: older workers' work-life needs (such as eldercare) are real but understudied.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Most stereotypes are false but still bias decisions; inclusive HR builds a good age climate; age diversity helps complex team work; older leaders do well when generative; work-life support is understudied.1Stereotypes? Mostly unsupported; only less training interest.2Decisions? Still more negative; implicit bias, little information.3Climate? Inclusive HR → positive age diversity climate (Böhm et al., 2014).4Teams? Faultlines fade; diversity helps complex tasks.5Leaders? Generativity matters; follower stereotypes matter.6Work-life? Many policies, little age-focused evidence.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which stereotype of older workers is supported? (less interest in training)?How can an organization build a positive age climate? (inclusive HR practices)

⚠ The trap✗ Age stereotypes do not matter, because meta-analyses show most of them are false.✓ The stereotypes are mostly not supported, but decisions about older people are still more negative (Bal and colleagues, 2011), possibly through implicit stereotypes, and especially for jobs stereotyped as young.

❓ This step answers: What do the few existing intervention studies show, and which interventions do the authors propose, based on which theory?

What do the few existing intervention studies show, and which interventions do the authors propose, based on which theory?Why this matters. Table 2 is a toolbox: eleven intervention categories, each with examples and the theory behind it. The authors stress that older workers are not one group, and that interventions must include coworkers, supervisors and change over time.In this step you learn:1The two example studies and the gaps2SOC training and work redesign3Relations between ages and age-supportive HR4Work-life support and training design

⭐ Two health programmes with strong designs: one improved some health behaviors, the other worked only for those who took part.Study 1: Hughes and colleagues (2011) randomly assigned 423 university employees over 40 to printed materials (control), an online check with personal coaching (COACH), or an online check with training modules (RealAge). What they found: COACH improved fruit and vegetable intake and physical activity; RealAge seemed to improve waist size. Study 2: Strijk and colleagues (2013) compared a programme of yoga, workouts and lifestyle coaching with a control group among 730 Dutch hospital workers over 45, measuring outcomes at 6 and 12 months. What they found: no effects on vitality, engagement, productivity or sick leave, except for those who actually took part.So this means: even strong designs cannot explain their results without a psychological theory of aging.→ Next: what is missing.

⭐ Little attention to psychological processes, to the social context, and to age as the focal variable.Both studies had no psychological theory of aging. Neither study measured psychological processes or used aging theory, neither used what psychology knows about the social context, and age was rarely the focal variable. Griffiths (1999) had called for age-focused intervention research over 15 years earlier.So this means: interventions need aging theory, not only a health programme.→ Next: the person-focused proposals.

⭐ SOC training teaches workers to select fitting tasks; work redesign gives older workers more autonomy, skill variety and mentoring.SOC training: help older workers select skills and tasks that fit their abilities and interests, prepare supervisors and teams, decide which tasks can be handed off; SOC can lead to better health in bridge employment (paid work after a career job, before full retirement) and better work ability. Work redesign: older workers react better to more autonomy and skill variety (using many skills) and less task variety (switching between many tasks); support job crafting; use mentoring for generativity, which also helps younger workers and keeps organizational memory.So this means: design work around the strengths and motives that grow with age.→ Next: the social system.

⭐ Positive contact between ages, stereotype reduction and an age diversity climate; and HR bundles matched to life stage.Positive exposure to other ages and structured discussions to reduce explicit and implicit stereotypes; train supervisors and teams; communicate from the top. Age-supportive HR: younger workers do better with training opportunities, older workers with maintenance practices. Kooij and colleagues' (2014) four bundles, with a 58-year-old employee (our example):1Development (raise functioning): a course to grow further.2Maintenance (sustain functioning): flexible hours.3Utilization (return to earlier levels): a lateral move after illness (a lateral move is a job at the same level).4Accommodative (adequate but lower level): later part-time work.Studies so far concern perceived, not actual, HR practices.So this means: an exam option calling utilization the bundle for a lower level of functioning is wrong: that is accommodative.→ Next: flexibility and training.

⭐ Through flexible work and eldercare support, which may work through positive social exchange.Work-life support may work through positive social exchange (support repaid with effort and loyalty). Telework went with higher productivity and less work-family conflict (Hill and colleagues, 1996, 2003); work-life initiatives predict applicant attraction (Uggerslev and colleagues, 2012); older workers may gain more from flexible shifts because sleep disturbance hits them harder; family-supportive supervisor training raised satisfaction and health and lowered turnover intentions (Hammer and colleagues, 2011).So this means: work-life support may matter more as caregiving and sleep needs change with age.→ Next: how training should be designed.

⭐ With extra time, smaller groups, self-pacing, a learning goal orientation and room for mistakes.Training design: extra time, smaller groups, self-paced learning, a learning goal orientation (aiming to learn rather than to look good), error management training (encouraged to make mistakes), intrinsic framing, links to prior knowledge. Paradox: age-specific training may feed stereotypes; involving older workers in its design may help. The other categories (supervisor training for safety, ergonomics, health promotion, career management, Total Worker Health) are in Table 2 below.So this means: every proposal rests on theory, but almost none has been tested with age in focus.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Few tested studies exist; the authors propose SOC training, work redesign, contact and climate, life-stage HR bundles, work-life support and adapted training.1Existing studies? The prevention lens · Table 3

Prevent, control, accommodate

❓ This step answers: How can interventions for an aging workforce be organized, and what must research do next?

How can interventions for an aging workforce be organized, and what must research do next?Why this matters. The review closes with a framework from health promotion and an agenda for research. A second message: support workers of all ages, because today's young workers are tomorrow's older workers. Our analogy: dental care: brushing for everyone, a filling when a problem starts, a crown when the tooth is damaged.In this step you learn:1Primary, secondary and tertiary interventions2Research designs needed3Other research needs in Table 3

⭐ By target group and aim: primary for all workers, secondary for mid to late career, tertiary to accommodate reduced work ability.1Primary: all workers; prevent problems so people end their career healthy and effective (age-supportive climate, successful aging techniques, less exposure to hazards, health promotion).2Secondary: mid to late career; reduce the impact of declines (memory interventions, ergonomics, flexible schedules) and use gains such as generativity (mentoring).3Tertiary: conditions that cannot be reversed; accommodate to retain workers (hearing aids, glasses).So this means: an exam option saying primary interventions are for older workers with problems is wrong: they are for all workers.→ Next: what research needs.

⭐ Experiments and quasi-experiments, very long longitudinal studies, objective measures and collaboration with other disciplines.Most research is correlational. Use intervention and control groups at individual, group or organizational level; quasi-experimental designs compare groups without random assignment. Longitudinal studies follow the same people over time; a generational difference comes from being born in a different period, not from getting older. Long longitudinal studies show change within people and separate aging from generational differences. Add measures such as objective health, and work with sociology, economics, demography, medicine and ergonomics.So this means: to know what works, test it over time with comparison groups.→ Next: more of the agenda.

⭐ Interventions should be cheap and scalable, tested across cultures, timed to how ages adopt versus keep up change, aimed at the whole person, and built on what chronological age really stands for.Wise interventions are brief, precise and cheap (Walton, 2014), for example supervisor tips on a smartphone. Global research, because country and culture matter: personality matures earlier where people start working earlier (Bleidorn and colleagues, 2013), a country's retirement age affects work motivation (Inceoglu and colleagues, 2009), and subjective age related to stress differently across nations (Barnes-Farrell and colleagues, 2002). Younger people may start change more easily, older people keep it up better (Rothman, 2006). Holistic interventions work in and outside work. And chronological age stands for many things (health, cognition, motives, subjective age); technology should be adapted to older workers too.So this means: the challenge: move beyond age as a statistical control variable.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Organize interventions as primary (all), secondary (mid to late career) and tertiary (accommodation); test them with strong, long, multidisciplinary research.1Organization? 🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

What are the best workplace interventions for supporting an aging workforce? The honest answer: we do not know yet which work best, because they have hardly been tested. The most promising, grounded in theory: SOC training; work redesign with more autonomy and skill variety, less task variety, and mentoring; positive contact between ages and an inclusive age diversity climate; age-supportive HR bundles (development, maintenance, utilization, accommodative); work-life support such as flexibility and eldercare help; age-friendly training; supervisor training for safety and health; ergonomics; health promotion; career management; and integrated Total Worker Health programmes. Organize them as primary, secondary and tertiary, and design them for workers of all ages. What is sustainable employability? This review does not use the term; it speaks of supporting workers' well-being, health, work ability and continued work as they age. Reading 3 connects this to engaging aging.
