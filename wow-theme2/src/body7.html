Changing organizational energy consumption behaviour through comparative feedback

Siero, F. W., Bakker, A. B., Dekker, G. B., & van den Burg, M. T. C. (1996). Changing organizational energy consumption behaviour through comparative feedback. Journal of Environmental Psychology, 16, 235–246.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what they want to know

Can comparing your unit with others save energy?

❓ This step answers: Why study energy behavior at work, why should comparative feedback help, and what was predicted?
Authors
Frans Siero, Arnold Bakker, Gerda Dekker and Marcel van den Burg, University of Groningen, the Netherlands
Type
Empirical article in the Journal of Environmental Psychology (1996): a field study with a quasi-experimental design in a metallurgical company
Question
Does adding comparative feedback (information about how other units perform) to a behavioral change programme reduce energy-wasting behavior more than the programme alone?
Main message
Yes: the unit that also saw other units' results wasted clearly less energy, still half a year later, with hardly any change in attitudes

Why study energy behavior at work, why should comparative feedback help, and what was predicted?Why this matters. Reading 1 named feedback as a lever for green behavior; this classic field study tests one form of it. Feedback is information about how well you are doing; comparative feedback adds how other groups are doing. A field study takes place in a real setting, here a company.In this step you learn:1Why energy behavior at work is a problem2Two reasons comparison should help3Why it might backfire4The hypothesis

⭐ Because at work the saving goes to the company, so employees profit only indirectly, and new technology is worth little unless employees use it well.At home, saving pays off on your own bill; in households behavior change saved up to 30 per cent. At work there is no such personal payoff. So the question is how a company can motivate employees to save. The authors are Frans Siero, Arnold Bakker, Gerda Dekker and Marcel van den Burg (University of Groningen), in the Journal of Environmental Psychology (1996).So this means: at work, saving energy needs a motive other than one's own money.→ Next: why comparison could supply it.

⭐ Because it makes the own group stand out (identity) and creates a wish to beat the other group (competition).Identity: seeing another group makes your own group stand out; by social identity theory (Tajfel, 1978) people want their group to look good, because group membership is part of who they are, and a shared identity can make people cooperate even without personal gain. Competition: by social comparison theory, people judge their performance by comparing with others; earlier studies found that just expecting to be compared, or seeing others' scores on a wall chart, improved performance. Our example: a school recycling league: once your class is compared with class 5B, "we" want to win.So this means: comparison turns an individual chore into a group matter.→ Next: the risk.

⭐ Yes: a unit that keeps losing despite doing its utmost may become demoralized and perform worse.The direction of comparison could not be controlled. A unit that does better gets a positive self-image; a unit that does worse will try harder to keep a positive identity. Only when it keeps losing for a long time, despite doing its utmost, does it lose any chance to feel good about itself, and it may become demoralized. Under competition, people also tend to avoid comparing with better performers. If a unit kept losing, the authors planned to split it to separate upward comparison (with a better group) from downward comparison (with a worse group).So this means: the effect of comparison was not certain in advance.→ Next: the prediction.

⭐ That comparative feedback added to the basic programme leads to an extra reduction of energy-wasting behavior, with more competitive feelings and identification.A hypothesis is a prediction the data can support or not. Attitudes and norms were measured, but apart from reported behavior no changes in them were expected.So this means: the study tests the extra effect of comparison on top of a full programme.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ At work saving energy lacks a personal payoff; comparison might help through identity and competition, though it could demoralize; the hypothesis was an extra reduction in waste.1Why at work? No personal payoff; technology needs good use.2Why comparison? Social identity and social comparison.3Backfire? Repeated losing may demoralize.4Hypothesis? Extra reduction of waste, more competition and identification.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which two theories explain why comparative feedback should work? (social identity, social comparison)?Which statement is wrong? "One unit got the programme and the other nothing." (both got the basic programme)

⚠ The trap✗ The study compares a unit with comparative feedback to a unit that received nothing.✓ Both units received the full basic programme (information, goal-setting, feedback on their own unit, supervision and control). The comparison unit got comparative feedback on top. So the study tests the extra effect of comparison.
STEP2
What both units received

Tell, target, track, supervise

❓ This step answers: What were the four elements of the behavioral change programme, and how was the goal set?

What were the four elements of the behavioral change programme, and how was the goal set?Why this matters. Both units received this basic programme; only one got comparison on top. So the programme is the baseline of the experiment. Our example: a fitness plan: you learn why exercise helps, get a weekly target, a step counter shows progress, and a coach checks in at first.In this step you learn:1Why the parts form a programme2Information3Goal-setting4Feedback and supervision

⭐ Because the parts only work together: a goal does little without feedback, and some supervision is always needed.The four parts: information, goal-setting, feedback, and supervision and control. The programme was based on an earlier study in which it improved the energy-saving driving of a transport company's drivers (Siero and colleagues, 1989).So this means: the four parts are a package; the study adds a fifth.→ Next: part 1.

⭐ Because work behavior is often a habit: information is necessary but seldom sufficient.Information told employees the company wanted different behavior and why: 12 weeks of energy bulletins and magazine items on the social and economic sides of saving, and on lights, heating, compressed air and machines. A habit is behavior that runs automatically in a familiar situation. To change habits, at least goal-setting plus regular feedback on reaching the goal is needed.So this means: knowing why does not by itself change what people do.→ Next: part 2.

⭐ As one common unit goal: a quarter of the pretest waste may remain, a level judged feasible.At work you appeal to the collective interest, so one goal for the unit. Higher and more precise goals lead to better performance (Locke & Latham, 1990), if people accept them and find them feasible: a difficult goal challenges people to do their utmost; a precise goal shows where to direct the effort. Employees of another unit judged 75 per cent of the possible saving feasible. Example (basic unit, lights, Table 3): pretest waste of 68.9% → goal 17.2%.So this means: an exam option saying the goal was to save 75 per cent of total energy is wrong: it was about wasteful behavior.→ Next: part 3.

⭐ A weekly chart showed the unit's savings as a percentage of what was possible, with the goal line, so employees compared with their own past and with the goal.100% = no waste, 75% = goal reached, 0% = as wasteful as before. These are temporal comparisons: better than before, close to the goal? Exact numbers were added only for workstation lights and compressed air leaks; to avoid information overload, there were none for machines or the remaining behaviors.So this means: the basic programme already gave feedback, but only about the own unit.→ Next: part 4.

⭐ First-line managers agreed to encourage and check energy behavior in their departments, because new behavior must first be supervised until it becomes a habit that holds without control.Supervision and control means managers checking that the behavior happens.So this means: the programme also used people, not only charts.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Both units got information, a common goal (a quarter of pretest waste), weekly own-unit feedback, and supervision; the comparison unit also saw other units.1Why a programme? Parts work only together (Siero et al., 1989).2Information? Necessary, seldom sufficient: habits.3Goal? 75% of possible saving; Locke & Latham (1990).4Feedback? Weekly chart; temporal comparison.5Supervision? Managers check until behavior holds.Typical exam questions on this step:?Pretest waste is 40%. What is the goal? (10%)?Which statement is wrong? "The goal was to save 75% of total energy."

⚠ The trap✗ The goal was to save 75 per cent of the energy the units used.✓ The goal was about energy-wasting behavior, not total energy: 75 per cent of the possible savings, so the observed waste had to fall to a quarter of its pretest level (for example 68.9% → 17.2%).
STEP3
How they tested it

Two units, observed at random moments

❓ This step answers: What was the design, how was energy-wasting behavior measured, and what else was measured?

What was the design, how was energy-wasting behavior measured, and what else was measured?Why this matters. The independent variable is what the researchers change (comparative feedback added or not); the dependent variable is what they measure (energy-wasting behavior). Knowing the design tells you how far to trust the result.In this step you learn:1The design2The units3The measurement of waste4The timeline and questionnaires5Strengths and weaknesses

⭐ A quasi-experimental field design: two existing units compared, without random assignment.Random assignment means chance decides who gets which condition; here the units already existed. A pretest is measured before, a post-test after the intervention.So this means: the comparison is real-world, but the units were not chosen by chance.→ Next: the units.

⭐ Two units of a Dutch metallurgical company, 200 miles apart, highly comparable, but of different size (135 versus 50 employees).A metallurgical company works with metals: drilling, rolling and bending steel. The units were comparable in production processes, communication, structure, staff and strategy. The comparison unit's weekly chart also showed the savings of the basic unit and of a third unit not in this report. Employees were not told the units were treated differently.So this means: comparable units, with one weak point: size.→ Next: the measurement.

⭐ By Multi Moment Recording: at random moments an observer noted whether equipment was on while nobody was there.For each selected object (machines, workstation lights, other equipment) the observer noted whether an employee was present and whether it used energy. Waste = "absent and on" divided by all moments the employee was absent. The company had only a rough energy meter, so the study observed wasteful behavior instead of energy use. The measure was about objects, not persons: no record was made of who was absent. Two independent observers agreed on 96.3 per cent of 1016 observations. Compressed air leaks were left out for technical reasons. Our example: a referee walking past at random moments.So this means: an exam option saying the dependent variable was kilowatt hours is wrong: it was observed wasteful behavior.→ Next: the timeline.

⭐ Pretest 10 weeks, campaign 20 weeks (analysed as four periods of 5 weeks), post-test 1 directly after, post-test 2 half a year later; questionnaires before and after.Pretest September to November; campaign November to March; post-test 1 in April (4 weeks); post-test 2 in September (5 weeks). Questionnaires measured behavioral beliefs (beliefs about what a behavior leads to), attitudes, social norms, identification with the own unit (how strongly you feel part of it, asked as "I would again cooperate with the same colleagues") and reported behavior, plus implementation checks (tests whether the conditions really reached people).So this means: the half-year follow-up lets the study see whether effects last.→ Next: how strong it is.

⭐ Strong on measurement and follow-up, weaker on assignment and generalization.Strengths: observed behavior instead of self-report, high observer agreement, a follow-up half a year later. Weaknesses: no random assignment, one company, two units of different size.So this means: trust the behavior data; be careful generalizing beyond this setting.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ A quasi-experimental field study in two units, with observed waste by Multi Moment Recording, a 20-week campaign and a half-year follow-up.1Design? Quasi-experimental; no random assignment.2Units? Comparable, 135 vs 50 employees.3Measure? Multi Moment Recording; 96.3% agreement.4Timeline? Pretest, 4 campaign periods, post-tests 1 and 2.5Strength? Observed behavior, follow-up; weak: assignment, size.Typical exam questions on this step:?Why was it quasi-experimental? (existing units, no random assignment)?How was waste computed? (absent and on / all absent moments)

⚠ The trap✗ The dependent variable was the company's energy consumption in kilowatt hours.✓ It was observed energy-wasting behavior (percentage of moments that equipment was on while nobody was there), because the company only had a rough measure of energy consumption.

Who keeps saving after the campaign?

❓ This step answers: What happened to energy-wasting behavior in both units, during the campaign and half a year later?

What happened to energy-wasting behavior in both units, during the campaign and half a year later?Why this matters. The graphs show the percentage of wasteful observations (lower is better) over seven periods. Our example: like two dieters: what matters is who loses weight and keeps it off, not who weighs least on one day.In this step you learn:1How to read the results2Machines, lights and remaining behaviors3The overall pattern

⭐ By comparing how each unit's waste changed over time, not by one period.The researchers used a multivariate analysis of variance on repeated measures: a test of whether the two units' lines change differently over the repeated measurements. The test used four time points: pretest, the campaign average, post-test 1 and post-test 2. For all three behaviors the units changed differently, and significantly so. A trend is the shape of change: linear (steady) or cubic (bending up and down).So this means: the key question is the change, not the level.→ Next: machines.

⭐ The comparison unit fell from 8.7% to 1.5%, below its goal; the basic unit was back near its start (14.7%) half a year later.The basic unit started at 16.1% and fell to 7.5% at its lowest (Table 2, Figure 1). The comparison unit started lower, which makes further saving harder, yet it declined more and stayed down (goal 2.2%).So this means: an exam option saying the result says nothing because the comparison unit started lower is wrong: the test is the change over time.→ Next: lights.

⭐ A dramatic difference: the comparison unit fell from 74.1% to 13.3% and was at 22.6% half a year later; the basic unit was back at 59.5%.The basic unit fell to between about 41 and 58% during the campaign and 41.6% right after it (Table 3, Figure 2). Both started very wasteful. The comparison unit was below its goal of 18.2% right after the campaign (13.3%) and slightly above it half a year later (22.6%).So this means: the comparison unit's saving lasted; the basic unit's faded.→ Next: the rest.

⭐ The comparison unit went from 34.1% to 11.9%; the basic unit from 38.4% to 20.9%, after jumping back to 34.6% right after the campaign.Remaining behaviors: unplugging equipment, general lights, closing doors and windows (Table 4, Figure 3). Because of the basic unit's capricious pattern, the difference took the form of a cubic trend. (For this pretest the text gives 34.4% where the table gives 38.4%.)So this means: comparison also helped on behaviors without exact feedback.→ Next: the overall picture.

⭐ The basic programme worked, but the comparison unit saved more on every behavior, kept it up without feedback, and transferred it to other behaviors.After six months the basic unit's overall reduction was still 9 per cent. The comparison unit also saved on behaviors for which it got no exact numbers (machines and the remaining behaviors), which the authors read as a likely transfer to related behaviors.So this means: comparative feedback added a lasting extra effect.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

STEP5
Table 1 · checks · alternative explanations

Behavior moved. Did minds move too?

❓ This step answers: What changed in people's thoughts, was the comparison really received, could something else explain the result, and why did comparative feedback work?

What changed in people's thoughts, was the comparison really received, could something else explain the result, and why did comparative feedback work?Why this matters. Behavior changed a lot. This step checks the minds behind it and the alternative explanations. An attitude is how positive or negative you feel about a behavior; a social norm is what you think important others expect.In this step you learn:1What happened to attitudes and beliefs2Whether the manipulation worked3The alternative explanation4Why the effect lasted, and the limits

⭐ No: behavior changed a lot while attitudes hardly changed, so attitude change is not a prerequisite when tools aim at behavior itself.Many theories say attitudes change first, then intentions, then behavior (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1991). Here feedback and comparison targeted behavior directly. The authors note attitudes were already quite positive at the start. Our example: taking the stairs because of an office stairs league, not because you changed your opinion about lifts.So this means: an exam option saying comparative feedback worked by changing attitudes first is wrong.→ Next: what did change.

⭐ After the campaign the comparison unit believed more strongly than the basic unit that switching off machines and lights saves energy, and its reported behavior rose; identification dropped in the basic unit.Table 1: attitudes and social norms did not change differently. After the campaign the comparison unit believed more strongly that switching off machines and lights saves energy (machines 4.6 v 4.0; lights 4.6 v 4.1, both significant after the campaign; for lights the change pattern itself was only borderline). Reported behavior rose there (3.1 → 3.7), not in the basic unit. Identification changed differently, but the significant part was a drop in the basic unit (4.2 → 3.6); the comparison unit went 3.8 → 4.0.So this means: heads changed a little, behavior a lot.→ Next: was the comparison noticed?

⭐ Yes: the comparison unit was more curious about, more aware of and more competitive about other units' results.A manipulation is the condition the researchers create. Implementation checks: curious 3.9 v 3.3; aware 3.6 v 2.3; competitive 4.4 v 3.8.So this means: the comparison reached people as intended.→ Next: the critic's alternative.

⭐ Unlikely, the authors argue: communication and commitment did not differ, the comparison unit felt less supervision, starting attitudes were equal, and its lower start made saving harder.The critic's idea: a small unit talks more, can be supervised more closely and feels more committed. But communication and commitment did not differ, and the small unit felt less supervision (2.1 v 3.0), the opposite of what size predicts. One more difference: the comparison unit knew the goal better and found it more attainable (4.3 v 3.4; 4.0 v 3.2). The authors think the comparison caused this, through extra attention to the goal, and read it as felt competence; but it is a second way the units differed, and could be part of why the comparison unit did better.So this means: the result most likely comes from comparison, with one open alternative: goal knowledge.→ Next: why it lasted.

⭐ Because comparison may have strengthened identification with the group, making people intrinsically motivated and helping new habits form.Intrinsically motivated means doing something because it is valued in itself. If people only wanted to win, the effect should have stopped with the feedback; it lasted half a year. The transfer to behaviors without exact feedback fits this. These are the authors' interpretations; the questionnaire data support them only partly: identification rose only slightly in the comparison unit (3.8 to 4.0); the significant change was the basic unit's drop.So this means: lasting change likely came through group identity and habit, not only competition.→ Next: the limits.

⭐ The study could not test demoralization, and the tool needs specified, measured behavior with feedback that employees see as reliable and contingent on their own behavior.Contingent means clearly caused by one's own behavior. The comparison unit never had a long losing streak, so the study does not show that comparison cannot demoralize.So this means: comparative feedback is powerful, but only with reliable, behavior-specific feedback, and the losing-team risk is untested.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Behavior changed without attitude change; the comparison was received; size is an unlikely explanation; identity and habits may explain the lasting effect; demoralization remains untested.1Attitudes first? No: not a prerequisite.2What changed? Beliefs and reported behavior; identification dropped in the basic unit.3Received? More curious, aware, competitive.4Unit size? Argued away; goal knowledge remains a difference.5Why lasting? Identification, intrinsic motivation, habits (interpretation).6Limits? The authors' conclusion (p. 245), in our words. Showing a unit its own results next to other units' results made people more competitive and work harder. Their energy saving held up six months after the campaign, and it came on top of what the basic programme already saved. The authors call comparative feedback a powerful strategy for changing behavior in organizations.

🧰 What organizations can do with it

Combine information, a common goal, regular feedback and supervision: this programme saves energy by itself. Add feedback that compares units for a larger and longer-lasting effect. The authors think these tools also suit other behaviors, such as safety, quality of work and efficiency, under one condition: the behavior must be described and counted in concrete units, so that employees experience the feedback as trustworthy and clearly caused by what they themselves do.

⚠️ How sure can we be?

A strong field study for its time: observed behavior, high observer agreement, a follow-up half a year later. But it compared only two units of one company, not randomly assigned and of different size; the authors argue the size difference away but cannot rule it out by design. The article is also inconsistent about what it expected: the introduction predicts no change in attitudes, while the discussion says attitude change was expected to come first. Outcome evaluations were dropped from the post-test because pretest answers were already extreme with little spread, which fits the point that attitudes were positive from the start. Some details do not match: reported behavior is said to have three questions but four are listed, and the basic unit's pretest for remaining behaviors is given as 38.4% in Table 4 and 34.4% in the text. The study says nothing about units that keep losing.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

Why does comparative feedback change organizational energy consumption? It makes the own group salient and creates a competitive orientation: people pay more attention to the feedback and want to do better than the other group. The lasting effect suggests that it also strengthened identification with the own group and intrinsic motivation, so the behavior was repeated until it became a habit. It changes behavior directly, without first changing attitudes. How can organizations increase pro-environmental behaviors? This study's answer: a programme of information, a common goal, feedback and supervision, plus comparison with other units, with reliable measurement of concrete behaviors.
