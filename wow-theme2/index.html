Theme 2 · The psychology of sustainability in organizations · Prepared for Sezen

What makes people at work act green, and stay able to work?

A company can promise to be sustainable. Whether it happens depends on what its people do every day, and on whether those people can keep working well for a whole career. This theme gives you the psychology of both.

Reading 1 of 8 · Meeting 3 · Review article · The map of the theme

Employee green behavior as the core of environmentally sustainable organizations

Zacher, H., Rudolph, C. W., & Katz, I. M. (2023). Employee green behavior as the core of environmentally sustainable organizations. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 10, 465–494.

STEP1
What employee green behavior is, and what this review does

Not good intentions: countable behavior at work, for better or worse

❓ This step answers: How is employee green behavior defined, and why do the authors call it the core of a sustainable organization?
🌿 Test the definition: six moments, one at a time. Decide whether each one is employee green behavior. The picture sorts it, then the explanation follows.
Authors
Hannes Zacher (Leipzig University), Cort W. Rudolph (Saint Louis University) and Ian M. Katz (Old Dominion University)
Type
Review article in the Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior (2023): it summarizes a whole research field and proposes a model
Built for
Three goals: review the research, build one integrative model, and give recommendations for research and practice
Main message
Organizations become sustainable through what employees do; that behavior is work performance, shaped at five levels

Some words first. An organization is any company or institution where people work. Sustainable means: able to go on without using up what the future needs. A review article does not report one study; experts summarize what a whole field has found. An antecedent is something that comes before a behavior and may lead to it. In the picture: three boxes. You get six moments from working life. For each, decide: is it employee green behavior that helps, employee green behavior that harms, or not employee green behavior at all?

Employee green behavior (the review shortens it to EGB) refers to "scalable actions and behaviors that employees engage in that are linked with and contribute to or detract from environmental sustainability" (Ones & Dilchert, 2012, quoted on p. 467). Read it in parts. Scalable: it can be measured, by how often it happens or how well it is done. Behaviors: what the employee does and controls, as opposed to the outcomes of behavior. Contribute to or detract from: it can help or harm. And it can be judged by its impact on the natural environment.

Why is it the core? Many organizations now put environmental sustainability goals in their strategy, next to economic and social goals (the triple bottom line). Once that is so, an employee's behavior can be evaluated by what it adds to or takes from those goals. And organizations cannot reach their environmental goals unless employees at different levels of the hierarchy perform green behavior. The strategy is on paper; the behavior makes it real.

The authors treat green behavior as a compound performance domain. Work performance is goal-directed behavior that contributes to the organization's goals. Performance research knows several dimensions: task performance, citizenship (helping beyond your task), counterproductive behavior, team member performance and leadership performance. Green behavior is not one more box next to these. It cuts across all of them, held together by one shared goal: environmental sustainability. The list is not complete; adaptive, proactive and innovative performance can also be carried out with an environmental impact.

Green behavior can be formally prescribed (creating sustainable products, delivering services in sustainable ways) or discretionary, in helpful or harmful ways (switching the computer off, wasting paper). It can ask for much adaptability, initiative or creativity, such as voicing suggestions to make the organization greener. It is more relevant to some jobs (a sustainability officer) than to others (a supermarket cashier), and it occurs at every level, from top managers' purchasing to team members' advocacy among coworkers. It differs from pro-environmental behavior outside work, because it is likely influenced by motives, opportunities, constraints, norms, leaders and a climate that belong to work; but the two may share causes such as general green attitudes, knowledge, skills and habits.

What does the review do? A call in 2012 (Ones & Dilchert) urged organizational psychologists to study the microfoundations of organizational sustainability: the individual behaviors on which the organization's results rest. Since then the field has grown: four edited books, six systematic reviews and two meta-analyses. But most studies looked only at individual antecedents, such as attitudes, values, perceived norms and intentions. The authors argue that research should look at antecedents and consequences at several levels: individual, team, work context, organization, and society and culture. The next step shows their model.

⚠ The trap✗ Employee green behavior is the list of good, voluntary things employees do for the environment.✓ It covers behavior that helps and behavior that harms, and behavior that is required and behavior that is voluntary. What makes it green behavior is that it is countable behavior at work with an impact on the environment.
📝 Exam-style questionA company reports that its energy use per employee fell by 8%. Is this employee green behavior? Explain with the definition.

Model answer: No. Employee green behavior refers to measurable behaviors that are under the employee's control and that contribute to or detract from environmental sustainability. Energy use per employee is an outcome at the level of the organization: it may result from employees' behavior, but also from new machines or a mild winter. The green behavior would be, for example, employees switching off equipment.

STEP2
The whole review in one picture · Figure 1

Five levels in, the core, five levels out

❓ This step answers: What is the authors' model of employee green behavior, and at which levels do its causes and consequences sit?
🗺️ Build the map: six events in and around a company. Tap the level where each one belongs; the level then shows what the model puts there, and what it brings.

A conceptual model is a drawing of how the authors think things hang together. It is a proposal built on the research, not a tested result. Levels are layers around a person: the individual, the team, the work context, the organization, and society and culture. In the picture: on the left, five levels where causes can sit. In the middle, employee green behavior. On the right, the same five levels again, now for what green behavior brings. You get six events; tap the level on the left where each belongs.

In the middle of the model sits green behavior, described in three ways. First, as a compound performance domain with several distinct dimensions (task-related, citizenship, proactive, innovative, counterproductive), which may have shared and unique causes. For example, knowledge and skills may matter more for task-related green behavior, and motivation more for discretionary forms. Second, as behavior that employees regulate themselves in a goal-directed way (step 4 explains this). Third, as something that can be studied at several levels: differences between persons, changes within a person over time, and the behavior of a whole team added together.

The causes work along two kinds of path. At the individual level there are three direct determinants (knowledge, skills, motivation) and several indirect ones (stable individual differences, cognitive and affective states) that work through the direct ones. The contextual levels can influence green behavior directly, when they give opportunities to perform or put up constraints, or indirectly, through the levels below them and in the end through the individual determinants. So a law can work through an organization's strategy, which works through a team's norms, which work through one person's motivation.

The outcomes mirror the causes. Green behavior may contribute bottom-up to outcomes for the individual (need satisfaction, identity development, self-efficacy, well-being, job performance, future intentions), the team (social norms, climate, team environmental performance), the work context (green tasks and jobs, through job creation and green job crafting), the organization (environmental performance, attractiveness) and society (reaching national and international sustainability goals). This could create an environmental competitive advantage. Broader outcomes can also be reached step by step: when team members act green they create a green team climate, which raises the team's environmental performance, which makes it more likely that the organization meets its goals. And some individual outcomes are likely to raise later green behavior: a virtuous cycle.

⚠ The trap✗ In the model, the organization influences employees only through its leaders.✓ Every contextual level can work directly (opportunities and constraints) or indirectly through the levels below. And leadership is placed at the team level, next to coworker support, advocacy, norms and team climate.
📝 Exam-style questionA government introduces a carbon tax. Describe, with the model, two different ways in which this could change an employee's green behavior.

Model answer: Directly: the tax is a factor at the level of society that changes the opportunities and constraints for behavior, for example because wasteful options become unavailable. Indirectly: it works through lower levels. The organization adapts its strategy and starts initiatives, the work context gets new equipment and roles, team norms and leadership shift, and in the end the employee's knowledge, skills and motivation change, which are the direct determinants of green behavior.

STEP3
The kinds of green behavior

The Green Five, a sixth, and three lines to place any behavior on

❓ This step answers: Which five dimensions of employee green behavior does the taxonomy distinguish, how do they differ, and what is the sixth?
🖐️ Sort seven employees: read what each one does and tap the tile that describes it. Only then the tile gets its official name.

A taxonomy is an ordered system of categories, like the system biologists use for animals. A dimension is one broad category in it. A construct is a named idea that researchers measure. Discretionary means: left to your own choice. In the picture: six tiles with a plain description. Seven employees each do something; tap the tile that fits. The tile then shows the name the researchers use.

Early research looked at single green acts, such as recycling paper. Ones and Dilchert (2012) wanted an overview of everything employees do, and built the green five taxonomy from thousands of critical incidents collected in the United States and Europe. It is hierarchical: one overall pro-environmental performance factor at the top, five broad dimensions, and 17 narrow subdimensions. Many other constructs in the literature can be placed in one of the five.

The five. Transforming: adapting work products and processes to make them more sustainable; doing required tasks in sustainable ways belongs here. Conserving: preserving resources and avoiding wastefulness (reducing use, recycling). Avoiding harm: reducing negative impact and mitigating damage (preventing pollution), including environmental management activities that reduce emissions or find solutions to the company's environmental problems. Influencing others: promoting other people's green behavior (educating, training). Taking initiative: proactive and entrepreneurial behaviors (initiating programs and policies, lobbying, activism).

Each of the five has a positive and a negative end (saving energy, wasting energy). But Dilchert (2018) noted that the taxonomies left out the more severe, actively harmful behaviors, and introduced counterproductive sustainability behaviors: behaviors that risk or directly result in environmental harm. A related construct is "nongreen behaviors in the workplace". Because sustainability is a strategic goal of many organizations, harming the environment detracts from the organization's goal and violates its legitimate interests: that is what makes it counterproductive. The five plus this one are the "green six".

One distinction the review handles with care. Some researchers split green behavior into required and voluntary. Ones and colleagues criticized that split, because it confounds (mixes up) the motive for the behavior with its content. Not printing e-mails is the same behavior whether a rule demands it or you choose it. That is why transforming behaviors, whose example measure is task-related, can also be discretionary.

📝 Exam-style questionAn employee convinces her team to stop using disposable cups, and later proposes a new waste policy to the board. Which two dimensions are these, and how do they differ?

Model answer: Convincing the team is influencing others: behavior aimed at spreading sustainability behaviors to other people (encouraging and supporting). Proposing a policy to the board is taking initiative: proactive, entrepreneurial behavior such as initiating programs and policies, which involves personal risk. The first changes what colleagues do; the second tries to change how the organization works.

❓ This step answers: Along which three lines can any green behavior be classified?
📏 Place six behaviors: each one belongs at one end of one line. Choose the end; the behavior lands on the line.

Besides the Green Five, the review gives a second way to classify green behavior: along three continuous dimensions (Francoeur and colleagues, 2021). Continuous means a line with two ends and everything in between, not two boxes. The Green Five say what the behavior is about; the three lines say how it is carried out.

Line 1: in-role or extra-role. In-role is part of the employee's core tasks; extra-role is what the employee does as an organizational citizen. Line 2: direct or indirect. Direct means the employee acts to benefit or harm the environment; indirect means the employee encourages others at work, who may be coworkers, customers, supervisors or people in other departments. Line 3: low or high intensity. Low intensity takes less effort and risk and is more incremental; high intensity takes more effort and risk and is more radical. A related distinction is local versus wide impact.

Why it matters: questionnaires do not cover the lines evenly. In the review of scales, 60% of the items were about direct behavior and 40% about indirect behavior. Most items (41%) were explicitly about voluntary behavior and only 5% explicitly about in-role or task-related behavior; 55% did not say. And 72% of the items were about individual employees, 13% about leaders, 7% about pairs (supervisor and subordinate) and 8% about collective behavior such as a team. So what we know is mostly about voluntary, individual office behavior.

⚠ The trap✗ Extra-role green behavior is the same as high-intensity green behavior.✓ They are different lines. Switching off the lights can be extra-role and low in intensity. Redesigning a production process can be in-role and high in intensity.
STEP4
The psychological theories behind green behavior

Why people act green, and what it takes to do it

❓ This step answers: Which theories from social and environmental psychology are used to explain employee green behavior?
💬 Listen to five employees: each one gives a reason. Tap the tile with the same kind of reason; the theory behind it appears, with the studies.

Some words first. An attitude is how positively or negatively you judge something. A norm is a rule about what one should do: a subjective norm is what you think important others expect, a personal norm is what you feel morally obliged to do. An intention is a plan to act. To mediate means to be the link through which one thing affects another. In the picture: five tiles, each with a kind of reason in plain words. Five employees speak; tap the tile that matches the reason.

Researchers have used many theories to understand the nomological network of green behavior: in our words, the web of relations between a concept, its causes, its consequences and its neighbours. Most of the effort went into the causes. The review picks out five prominent theories from social and environmental psychology.

The first two are easy to mix up. The theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) says that attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control predict intentions, which result in behavior. It emphasizes rational decision-making. The norm-activation model (Schwartz, 1977) says that pro-environmental behavior is influenced by personal norms, feelings of moral obligation, which depend on awareness of the consequences of (not) acting and on feeling responsible. It assumes that behavior is driven by moral values.

The value-belief-norm theory of environmentalism extends the norm-activation model: personal values predict environmental beliefs and, in turn, personal norms and behavior. A social identity approach looks at the group: identification with the in-group, its norms and goals, and collective efficacy beliefs predict environmental action. Social exchange theory says people feel obliged to reciprocate when they receive rewards or bear costs in a relationship. The studies for each are in the scene.

Drawing partly on these theories, organizational scholars built three conceptual models of their own: the comprehensive action determination model (Lülfs & Hahn, 2014), the model of multilevel influences on pro-environmental motivation and green behavior (Norton and colleagues, 2015), and the process framework of macrodeterminants (Young and colleagues, 2015). The first adds situational influences and habits; the second adds controlled ("I have to") and autonomous ("I want to") motivation and outcomes at four levels; the third adds group, organizational and external factors. Open "Three models built by organizational scholars" at the end of the scene for each one.

Each theory points to a different lever. If behavior follows a rational plan, change attitudes, norms and the feeling of control. If it follows moral obligation, make consequences visible and responsibility personal. If it follows the group, use group norms and comparisons. If it follows exchange, give support first. (This translation into levers is ours; the review only says that integrating the theories could be useful for designing interventions.)

⚠ The trap✗ The theory of planned behavior and the norm-activation model both say that norms cause green behavior, so they are the same.✓ Different norms, different engines. Planned behavior: the subjective norm (what others expect) is one input into a rational intention. Norm activation: the personal norm (my own moral obligation) is the engine, switched on by awareness of consequences and responsibility.
📝 Exam-style questionUnit A is told how much energy it saved. Unit B is told how much it saved and how that compares with unit A. Which theory predicts that unit B saves more, and why?

Model answer: The social identity approach. It says that identification with the in-group, in-group norms and goals, and collective efficacy beliefs predict environmental action. Comparative feedback makes the own group and its standing relative to another group salient. The review cites Siero and colleagues (1996): units that were also told how they compared with another unit saved more energy than units that only heard their own result.

❓ This step answers: What do theories of work performance add to the explanation of green behavior?
🛠️ Diagnose six cases: green behavior is not happening, or it is. Decide which of the four parts is involved.

If green behavior is work performance, then what we know about the causes of performance should apply to it. The authors find it surprising that theorizing on green behavior has neglected the well-established frameworks of performance, or focused only on selected predictors, such as motivation. They bring in three such frameworks. Two are here; the third is in the next tab.

Campbell's theory of work performance says that any performance dimension has three direct determinants: declarative knowledge (knowing what), procedural knowledge or skills (knowing how), and motivation, defined as choice behavior about the direction, intensity and duration of effort. These three are assumed to mediate the effects of more distant determinants such as abilities, personality, attitudes, affective states and contextual factors. Individual differences in green attitudes may affect green behavior through motivation; a sustainability training could affect it through knowledge and skills.

A second well-established model adds that, besides ability and motivation, employees must have the opportunity to perform (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982). So contextual factors that facilitate or constrain green behavior must be considered next to individual ones. In the review, "opportunities to perform" and "barriers to perform" become the two faces of the context.

This gives a simple check for any intervention: which part does it address? An information campaign addresses knowledge. A training addresses skills. Feedback and incentives address motivation. Recycling bins and job autonomy address opportunity. An intervention that addresses a part that was not the problem will not help. (The check is our way of using the theory.)

⚠ The trap✗ If employees have green attitudes, green behavior will follow.✓ An attitude is an indirect determinant. It has to pass through motivation, and the behavior still needs knowledge, skills and the opportunity to perform.
❓ This step answers: How does action regulation theory describe the way employees steer their own green behavior?
🔁 Follow Noor for a month: five things she does, in order. Tap the phase each one is. Then two cases about habits.

The third performance framework is action regulation theory. The review calls it a meta-theory: a broad theory about how people regulate (steer) goal-directed behavior at work. It fits green behavior because the authors see that behavior as active and dynamic: employees do not only react to rules, they set goals and act on them.

The theory has two parts. The sequence: developing and selecting goals, orienting yourself in the environment, planning, monitoring the execution, and processing feedback. The structure: regulation happens at several levels, from the sensorimotor or skills level, through flexible action patterns (routines) and the conscious intellectual level, to meta-cognitive heuristics. Behavior can move between levels: routinization (for example developing energy conservation habits) and reintellectualization of routine behavior (for example becoming aware of wasteful behavior).

For practice this suggests two different jobs (our reading; the review itself only proposes research on habits). To build a good habit, let people practise until the behavior runs by itself. To break a bad one, first make it conscious again. For research, the authors recommend studying how employees develop and select green goals, and how regulation opportunities (autonomy, support) and regulation problems (obstacles, uncertainty, overload) help or hinder them.

⚠ The trap✗ A habit is the opposite of regulated behavior.✓ In action regulation theory a habit is behavior regulated at a lower level (flexible action patterns). It got there through practice, and it can be brought back to the conscious level.
STEP5
The empirical findings

What leads to green behavior, and what it brings

❓ This step answers: What are the individual antecedents of employee green behavior, and how strong are they?
📊 Predict before you look: seven characteristics of a person. For each, predict how strongly it goes together with green behavior. The bars then show the numbers.

A correlation is a number that says how strongly two things go together: 0 means not at all, 1 means perfectly. A meta-analysis combines many studies into one estimate; the review reports ρ and k: k is the number of studies and ρ is, in our words, the estimated average correlation. Not significant (n.s.) means the link cannot be told apart from zero. A cross-sectional study measures everything at one moment, so it cannot show what causes what. In the picture: an empty bar chart. You predict, the bars appear.

The review sorts the findings by order (antecedents or consequences) and by level (individual or contextual). Individual factors include stable differences between persons, such as personality, and more dynamic things that change within a person, such as feelings. An early review of 21 studies already concluded that attitudes and past behavior had relatively consistent effects. One warning comes first and applies to everything in this step: studies draw models with causes and effects, but most of them are cross-sectional, so the causality still has to be shown.

The pattern in the numbers. Demographic characteristics (age, tenure, education, gender) are weakly related or unrelated. The building blocks of the theory of planned behavior (attitudes, norms, perceived control, self-efficacy, intentions) are moderately related, between .40 and .46. Of the job attitudes, satisfaction is weak (.15) and commitment (.30) and identification (.35) are stronger. Of personality, conscientiousness, openness and moral reflectiveness are moderately related; extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism are not.

A gap. The performance view says knowledge and skills are direct determinants, yet very few studies look at them or at related predictors such as experience, routines and habits, and none at general mental ability. The authors guess why: these things cannot easily be measured with self-report surveys. One study built a taxonomy of "green competencies", which mixes knowledge, skills, abilities, attitudes, behavior and awareness (Cabral & Dhar, 2019). Open "Knowledge, skills and habits" at the end of the scene for the few studies there are.

Affective and motivational states change from day to day. One finding comes from a daily diary study: low-arousal positive feelings went with task-related green behavior for everyone, while high-arousal positive feelings went with proactive green behavior only for employees with a less favorable green attitude (Bissing-Olson and colleagues, 2013). And autonomous and controlled motivation were found to mediate the positive links of leadership and of green attitude with green behavior.

What does this mean for an organization? Selecting on age or gender makes no sense. Attitudes, norms and the feeling of being able are where the person-side action is, and the barriers found in the person point the same way: unfavorable attitudes, no perceived norm, low self-efficacy, low awareness, lack of knowledge and time, unwillingness to change habits. (The translation into advice is ours, in line with the review's practical section.)

⚠ The trap✗ The meta-analysis shows that a green attitude causes green behavior at work.✓ It shows a moderate association (ρ = .45). The primary studies are mostly cross-sectional, and the review says more than once that the causality still requires experimental and longitudinal research.
📝 Exam-style questionA manager says: "Our older staff will never go green; we should focus on the young." What does the review say?

Model answer: The opposite, if anything. Two meta-analyses found a weak positive association between age and employee green behavior (ρ = .10 and ρ = .08): older employees report slightly more green behavior, not less. The link is weak, so age is not a useful basis for targeting at all. Attitudes, perceived norms, perceived control and intentions are far more strongly related (around .40 to .46).

❓ This step answers: What are the contextual antecedents of employee green behavior, and what is the role of leadership?
🏢 Six questions about the surroundings: choose first. The bars and the studies follow.

Contextual antecedents are factors outside the employee that either facilitate green behavior (opportunities to perform) or constrain it (barriers to perform). The review goes through five groups: the work context, leadership and supervisor support, coworkers and team, organizational factors, and society and culture. Much is known about leadership and about how employees see their organization; little about the work context, and very little about society and culture.

Leadership has received considerable attention. Early studies suggested that the environmental involvement of superiors is an effective way to raise subordinates' green behavior. The meta-analysis agrees: moderate links with environment-specific transformational leadership (.49), servant leadership (.37) and supervisor support (.35). Newer studies ask how leadership works: through coworkers who advocate green behavior, through the perceived green climate among coworkers, and through clear team goals and passion. Coworker support, social norms and team climate matter in their own right, and a lack of supervisory support and of role models is a key barrier.

At the level of the organization the review separates strategies (policies, mission, goals, plans, resources), initiatives (programs, practices, interventions) and pro-environmental culture and climate. Early reviews found weak or inconsistent effects of organizational factors; newer research supports them, at least as perceived by employees. Having a sustainability policy goes with more green behavior; whether a policy is reactive (complying with regulations) or proactive (based on strategic or ethical considerations) has so far been neglected. Most research on initiatives is about green human resource management, whose effects seem to run through psychological mechanisms such as green climate, pride and perceived support. It is generally assumed that shared climate perceptions carry the effect of culture on attitudes and behavior. One limit: research has looked at each employee's own perception (psychological climate), and has neglected culture and climate as shared, team-level or organization-level things.

Which interventions work? Scholars recommend providing information, goal setting and feedback, persuasive communication and participatory designs. A meta-analysis on green behavior outside work found cognitive dissonance, goal setting, social modeling and prompts the most effective treatments (Osbaldiston & Schott, 2012). For organizations, the review of 17 intervention studies points to awareness, feedback, financial incentives, infrastructure and management support. Neglected so far: the effect of creating green tasks, jobs and roles, of green products and services, of specific programs such as recycling initiatives, and of environmental management systems and certification.

Put together: in the meta-analysis the numbers for the surroundings are in the same range as those for the person's own attitudes (this comparison is ours), and in one diary study the surroundings decided whether an intention became behavior the next day. For the question "what is the role of leadership?" our summary of what the review reports has three parts: leaders can model the behavior, their influence partly runs through coworkers and the team climate, and they hold practical levers (facilities, appraisal, feedback). Reading 2 works this out.

Most research has asked what leads to green behavior. Only several recent studies ask what follows from it. ❓ This step answers: How has employee green behavior been studied and measured, and what does that mean for how far we can trust the findings?
🔬 Be the methods reviewer: six questions about designs and measures. Choose first.

Some words first. A research design is the plan of a study. Quantitative studies work with numbers, qualitative studies with words (interviews, documents, observation). A correlational design measures things and looks at how they go together; an experiment changes one thing on purpose and compares groups. Longitudinal means measuring the same people several times. A self-report is a person's own answer about themselves. In the picture: two bars that fill in after your first answer.

Designs. Like most new topics, green behavior was first studied with cross-sectional designs, archival data and employee self-reports. Over the past decade the field has matured. Diary and experience sampling studies look at variation within persons. A few experiments and intervention studies exist: on leadership combined with human resource practices; on emotional displays in fictional news videos (three quasi-experimental studies suggest that anger and fear raised green behavior and sadness reduced it; Russell & Ashkanasy, 2021); on self-concordance: in two intervention studies, linking sustainable energy use and commuting to personally important goals raised employees' intentions to show these behaviors, compared with the control conditions (Unsworth & McNeill, 2017; reading 8); and on feedback in a green human resource intervention, where more feedback went with more later green behavior; in a complex three-way interaction, employees with weak autonomous motivation benefited most from feedback together with high goal commitment (Davis and colleagues, 2020). Qualitative studies include company case studies and interviews; one found obstacles to involving employees in biodiversity management: the complexity of the issue, lack of corporate commitment, externalization of initiatives and lack of training (Boiral and colleagues, 2019). Studies with several sources, levels and waves are still very rare.

Measures. There are self-report scales for discrete behaviors (printing double-sided, using recycling bins, turning off lights) and for performance-based dimensions: task-related and proactive green behavior, organizational citizenship behavior for the environment, and counterproductive sustainability behaviors. Citizenship behavior for the environment is the most widely studied; it is positively related to, yet distinct from, ordinary citizenship behavior and related constructs such as perceived organizational support and affective commitment. But scales with that one label measure very different things, which is the jingle fallacy; its mirror image is the jangle fallacy. A review of 53 studies found that 42% wrote new items, 45% reused published items and 13% used qualitative methods, experiments or direct observation, and asked for a clean-up: theory-based measures, no redundancy, more objective measures, and questions about the last day instead of the last year.

What theory still needs, according to the authors: (1) treat green behavior as active and goal-directed, and study goals, regulation and habits; (2) study neglected forms: green voice (speaking up with suggestions and challenging the status quo), green job crafting (proactively building sustainability into your tasks and work relationships), green commuting (travelling to and from work in a sustainable way), and green entrepreneurship; (3) study the determinants known from performance research (knowledge, skills, motivation, opportunities and constraints) and the causes of counterproductive behavior, where only personality and leadership have been studied; (4) study outcomes at all levels, and green behavior at higher ranks such as CEOs; (5) study possible negative effects of high or low levels of green behavior, for individuals (citizenship fatigue, poor person-organization fit), teams (erosion of social norms, social conflicts) and organizations (low attractiveness, resistance to change); (6) study culture: where a culture puts economic achievement first, employees may hold back, so interventions may need to motivate; where a culture traditionally values living in harmony with nature, employees may not need to be motivated but could still benefit from training in knowledge, skills and habits. The authors present these last ideas as possibilities.

What methods still need: (1) designs that fit the question (cross-sectional surveys are in most cases not suitable for theories that involve causality, such as mediation models): experiments and interventions, experience sampling and diaries, and longitudinal studies with three or more waves; interventions with one component at a time; multilevel designs with individuals nested in teams or organizations. (2) More than one source: self-reports together with peers, supervisors and objective measures. (3) Studies that compare competing explanations, with individual and contextual predictors together, and all six dimensions in one study, to find shared and unique causes. (4) Shorter, better-tested measures of the dimensions, with the green six as a starting point.

⚠ The trap✗ Jingle and jangle are two words for the same mistake.✓ They mirror each other. Jingle: same label, so we wrongly assume the same thing. Jangle: different labels, so we wrongly assume different things.
📝 Exam-style questionA study asks 300 employees once, by questionnaire, about their supervisor's green leadership and their own green behavior, and concludes that leadership increases green behavior. Give two reasons, from the review, to doubt the conclusion.

Model answer: (1) The design is cross-sectional: everything is measured at one moment, so the causality implied by the model cannot be shown; this needs experimental or longitudinal research. (2) All data come from one source, self-reports, which raises concerns about common method bias, and self-reports of green behavior can be biased by memory flaws and self-enhancement; a meta-analysis found only a moderate association (r = .46) between self-reports and objective measures of pro-environmental behavior.

Who wrote it, and which problem they want to solve

"Green leadership" mixes up three things

❓ This step answers: What do the authors find wrong with earlier research on leadership and green behavior, and what do they set out to do?

Authors
Hannes Zacher and Clara Kühner (Leipzig University), Ian M. Katz (Old Dominion University) and Cort W. Rudolph (Wayne State University). Three of them wrote reading 1
Type
Conceptual paper in Group & Organization Management (2024): it reports no new data. It reviews studies, defines concepts and proposes a model
Question
How should leadership for environmental sustainability be defined, and how do its causes and consequences hang together across levels?
Main message
Study what leaders do (leader green behavior), not style labels; it is the link between causes and results at the levels of organization, leader and followers

Some words first. In our words, a leader is someone who directs or influences others at work; a follower is someone who is led. A leadership style is a pattern of leader behaviors, usually named with an adjective: transformational (in our words: inspiring with a vision), servant, ethical. An antecedent comes before a behavior and may lead to it; a consequence follows from it. A conceptual paper builds ideas and proposals from existing research; it does not collect data. In the picture: three boxes in a row: before, the behavior, after. Sort six statements about a leader called Dana.

Many studies have looked at leadership and employees' green behavior, and a meta-analysis found positive, moderate links with environmentally-specific (green) transformational leadership, servant leadership and supervisory support (Katz and colleagues, 2022). Single studies also explored mechanisms (for example green human resource management) and boundary conditions (for example employees' green identity). The authors value this work but see a basic problem in it: what, exactly, was measured? An "adjective leadership" style bundles a set of behaviors with the good or bad results they are assumed to produce. So the label already contains the leader's assumed intentions and the assumed outcomes. One definition even called environmental leadership an ability: being able to get people and organizations moving toward long-term ecological sustainability (Egri & Herman, 2000). The authors point at the word ability; in our reading the definition names an antecedent and a result, and no behavior.

The authors name two limits of the literature. First, the focus on broad, positively-valenced (positive-sounding) style labels that conflate the content of leader behavior with its possible antecedents or consequences (the term "adjective leadership" is from Day & Antonakis, 2013; the criticism from Fischer & Sitkin, 2023). Second, fragmentation: every study uses its own combination of leadership constructs, mediators, moderators and levels, so the findings do not add up to one picture. What is needed: clear definitions, a separation of antecedents and consequences at several levels, and one integrating model.

From this follow three goals. (1) After a brief review of the research, define leadership in the context of environmental sustainability and the construct of leader green behavior, by combining the behavioral approach to leadership with the theory on employee green behavior from reading 1. (2) Present an integrative model of antecedents and consequences of leader green behavior at the levels of the leader, the followers and the organization, with eight propositions; the structure of pathways is borrowed from a model on leadership and employee health (Wegge and colleagues, 2014). (3) Give recommendations for future research. They name two contributions: applying the six-form taxonomy of employee green behavior to leaders, and positioning leader green behavior as the critical link between antecedents and consequences at three levels.

⚠ The trap✗ The authors show that green transformational leadership does not work.✓ They do not dispute the findings. Their point is about what the label measures: a style mixes behavior with assumed causes and results, so you cannot tell which of them does the work.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher defines green leadership as "the capacity of leaders to make their teams environmentally friendly". Which criticism from the article applies?

Model answer: The definition conflates leader behavior with its assumed antecedents and consequences. A "capacity" is an antecedent (an ability), and "making teams environmentally friendly" is a consequence (follower behavior). Nothing in the definition says what the leader does. The authors call for clear construct definitions that describe concrete leader actions and keep antecedents and consequences separate.

The two definitions the article stands on

Leadership as a process, leader green behavior as actions

❓ This step answers: How do the authors define leadership in the context of environmental sustainability, and what is leader green behavior?

A process is something that unfolds between people over time, as opposed to a property of one person. Scalable means it can be measured on a scale, here by how proficiently (how well) it is done. Direct means the leader's own act touches the environment; indirect means it works through something or someone else. In the picture: a leader on the left, the environment on the right, and four routes between them. Six actions; tap the route each one takes.

First definition. Leadership in the context of environmental sustainability is a process: one or more people (the leaders) use their own actions to steer what other people (the followers) do, and/or what the organization as a whole does, in the direction of environmental sustainability goals. Those involved share these goals to a greater or lesser degree (the authors build on a general definition of leadership by Judge & Long, 2012). Environmental sustainability itself means dealing responsibly with the natural environment so that quality of life on Earth can continue. Three things to notice: it is a process and not a trait; the target can be followers or the organization (for example by introducing a new policy); and the goals need only be "more or less" shared.

Second definition. Leader green behavior (also: leader pro-environmental behavior) is built on the definition of employee green behavior from reading 1: the set of things a leader does on purpose, that can be rated for how well they are done, and that help or harm environmental sustainability. The actions are under the leader's control. They benefit or harm the environment directly, or indirectly through changes in the context (new policies or work tasks), in follower behavior (orders, inspirational motivation, behavioral modeling) or in organizational behavior (influence on environmental investment decisions).

The domain is divided with the green six from reading 1 (the Green Five plus counterproductive sustainability behaviors), and placed on the same three lines: in-role or extra-role, direct or indirect, low or high intensity. Two more points. According to Ones and colleagues (2018), green behaviors can be performed at different levels of the hierarchy. And the authors add that leader green behaviors may be shown by individuals without an official leadership role: shared or emergent leadership. Open the two cards at the end of the scene for the dimensions and lines with leader examples.

Why this definition is better than a style, according to the authors: a style only describes the pattern of behaviors a leader uses to influence followers. The green six also cover what leaders do themselves (direct behaviors) and what they change in the work and organizational context. And because the definition names actions only, antecedents and consequences stay outside it and can be studied separately. That is the precondition for the model in the next steps.

⚠ The trap✗ Leader green behavior is the "influencing others" part of the Green Five.✓ It is all six dimensions. Influencing others is one of them. A leader who recycles, redesigns a process or flies unnecessarily also shows leader green behavior.

Proposition 1 applies the performance theory from reading 1 to leaders. Leader green behavior is primarily influenced by three direct determinants: declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge or skills, and motivation (choices about the direction, intensity and duration of effort). Abilities, values, personality, attitudes, beliefs and affect influence it indirectly, via those three. Parts of this are supported by studies; the chain as a whole is new and untested.

Proposition 2 is, in our view, the most unusual one: a bottom-up effect. Followers influence their leader (a) indirectly, by educating, persuading or encouraging the leader (their own "influencing others" behavior), which changes the leader's antecedents; and (b) directly, because leaders may observe and model what followers do, green or counterproductive.

Proposition 3 brings in the context: the organization's strategy, initiatives, culture, climate, equipment, materials, technology and work design. It influences leader green behavior (a) indirectly via leader antecedents, (b) directly, and (c) by changing the strength of the effects of leader antecedents. Part (c) rests on two theories: situation strength and trait activation. Evidence the authors cite for the context in general: the meta-analytic links of corporate social responsibility, green human resource management, perceived organizational support and green psychological climate with green behavior, and studies showing that having an environmental policy goes with more green behavior.

⚠ The trap✗ Situation strength theory and trait activation theory predict the same thing.✓ Both are about interaction, and the article presents them as complementary. A strong situation (a clear policy that limits discretion) makes the leader's own motivation matter less. A trait-activating context (a green office design) makes it show more.

How a leader's green behavior reaches followers

❓ This step answers: What is the role of leadership in promoting employee green behavior: by which pathways does it work?

This is where the model answers the question most studies asked: how does leadership relate to followers' green behavior? Instead of one arrow, the authors propose several pathways, borrowed from a model of how leadership affects employee health. The authors call propositions 4 and 5 reverse pathways: they run from the leader back to followers and context. The pathways differ in what the leader works on: the follower as a person, her own example, or the system around the follower.

Proposition 4. Leader green behavior influences follower green behavior (a) indirectly via follower antecedents: person-focused action, in which the leader trains, instructs, persuades and encourages and thereby improves followers' knowledge, skills and motivation or their attitudes; (b) directly through behavioral modeling: followers observe and copy the leader's green or counterproductive behaviors, so leaders may serve a powerful role-model function; and (c) by changing the strength of the effects of follower antecedents on follower behavior, in line with situation strength and trait activation theories.

Proposition 5. Leader green behavior also works through the wider context. (a) System-focused action: by changing contextual antecedents such as policies, practices and work design, leaders indirectly influence follower antecedents, follower behavior, or the link between them. (b) Moderating action: leaders strengthen or soften the impact that contextual antecedents have on followers, for example by deciding whether and how to implement a new performance management policy that includes sustainability.

For a leader who wants a greener team, the model therefore gives a wider repertoire than "be inspiring": teach and encourage, do it yourself where your team can see it, change what you can in policies and work design, and take care how organizational policies land in your team. Our summary; the article itself draws conclusions for research, not for practice. Also note that the same routes can carry harm: a leader who models counterproductive behavior can be copied too.

⚠ The trap✗ Trickle-down means the leader tells followers what to do.✓ In the article, the trickle-down effect studied by Shao and colleagues (2023) is about modeling: followers see the leader's own green behavior, which works through role model influence and their green self-efficacy. Telling and training is person-focused action.
📝 Exam-style questionA director introduces a rule that every project proposal needs a carbon estimate. One department head applies it strictly; another lets it slide. Name the pathway each person illustrates.

Model answer: The director shows system-focused action (proposition 5a): she changes a contextual antecedent, an organizational practice, which then influences followers' antecedents and behavior. The two department heads show moderating action (proposition 5b): by deciding whether and how to implement the practice, they strengthen or weaken its impact on their followers' green behavior.

The authors' recommendations for future research

How to study leader green behavior properly

❓ This step answers: What do the authors recommend for theory and for methods?

STEP1
Work engagement, and the question of this chapter

Can employees stay engaged as they grow older?

❓ This step answers: What is work engagement, why does it matter, and what does the chapter want to find out about age?
🔥 Listen to five employees: tap the tile that fits what each one says. Four tiles: three parts of engagement, and one that is something else.
Authors
Arnold B. Bakker and Jari J. Hakanen
Type
A short book chapter (2019), written as Wilmar Schaufeli's retirement approaches; his definition and questionnaire set off work engagement research
Built for

Main message

Some words first. A resource is anything that helps you reach your goals or cope: energy, skills, health, support. Personal resources sit in the person; job resources sit in the job. HR practices are what an organization does to manage its people (HR = human resources). Proactive means acting on your own initiative, ahead of problems. In the picture: four tiles with a plain description. Five employees speak; tap the tile that fits.

Work engagement is defined as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication and absorption (Schaufeli and colleagues, 2002). Vigor: high levels of energy and the willingness to invest effort in the job. Dedication: being strongly involved in the work and experiencing significance, enthusiasm and inspiration. Absorption: being fully immersed in work activities, so that time passes quickly. For two decades most research has measured it with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale.

Engagement differs from other positive states such as happiness and job satisfaction because it is an activated state. A satisfied person can feel calm and content; an engaged person feels excited and enthusiastic, and is therefore prepared to put thinking power and energy into the work. That is also why it matters to organizations: open "Why it is so popular" at the end of the scene for what it has been related to.

Research on what leads to engagement produced Job Demands-Resources theory, which you know from theme 1. Engagement is most likely when job challenges are combined with personal or job resources: interesting, challenging tasks together with social support, autonomy and skill variety; or work pressure and deadlines on days when people feel optimistic and self-efficacious. One principle of the theory carries this chapter: employees need to be proactive to stay engaged.

Now the question. The average age of the workforce in industrialized countries is clearly rising. Findings so far point to a positive link between age and engagement: older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones (Hakanen and colleagues, 2019). The authors want to explain how engagement can be kept up with increasing age. Their plan: look at what people lose and gain as they age, combine that with what is known about proactivity, and build a model they call "engaging aging".

⚠ The trap✗ An employee who is satisfied with the job is engaged.✓ Satisfaction can be calm and passive. Engagement is an activated state: energy (vigor), meaning (dedication) and immersion (absorption).
📝 Exam-style questionA nurse says: "My shifts fly by, I feel strong and energetic, and my work really means something to me." Name the three dimensions of work engagement in her words.

Model answer: "My shifts fly by" is absorption: being fully immersed, so that time passes quickly. "I feel strong and energetic" is vigor: high levels of energy and willingness to invest effort. "My work really means something to me" is dedication: strong involvement with a sense of significance, enthusiasm and inspiration.

The model of engaging aging

❓ This step answers: What is the model of engaging aging, and how do loss, gain and HR practices lead to proactive behavior?
🗺️ Follow Karin, 58, through the model: six questions. Each answer lights up one part of the figure.

A model is a drawing of how the authors think things hang together; this one is a proposal. A motivational state is a condition that makes you want and able to act. A moderator is something that makes a link stronger or weaker. A plus sign on an arrow means: more of the first goes with more of the second. In the picture: age on the left; loss of resources above, gain below; HR practices and proactive behavior in the middle; work outcomes on the right.

The authors use proactivity theory to argue that all employees have to keep fine-tuning what they do at work and in their free time if they want to stay engaged and perform well, and that this is more needed with increasing age. Proactive behavior at work is self-initiated, anticipatory action, such as taking charge, voicing concerns and seeking feedback (Parker & Bindl, 2017). There is considerable evidence that it goes with favorable performance outcomes: person and job come to fit better, people get more chances to use their resources and strengths, and work may become more meaningful (Kooij and colleagues, 2017).

According to Parker, Bindl and Strauss (2010), three motivational states prompt and sustain proactivity. "Can do": do I believe I can, is it feasible, and what will it cost me (self-efficacy, feasibility, costs)? "Reason to": why would I? Feeling personally responsible, and wanting from within to take initiative. "Energized to": do I have the energy? A state of feeling and motivation that supplies the energy for proactive action.

The chapter connects each state to aging. Gain of personal resources facilitates "can do". Loss of personal resources provides the "reason to": as physical abilities slowly decline and the body recovers its balance less easily, the need to be proactive grows. And because age itself is only weakly and unsystematically related to proactive behavior, something has to supply the energy: age-focused HR practices initiated by the organization. They also act as moderators, and loss and gain may have a combined impact, as conservation of resources theory suggests.

⚠ The trap✗ In the model, losing resources makes older employees less proactive.✓ The model proposes the opposite: loss provides the reason to be proactive. What may be missing is the energy, which is why HR practices are in the model.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain with the model why two 60-year-olds with the same health complaints may differ in how proactively they adapt their work.

Model answer: Both have a "reason to" be proactive, because loss of personal resources (health, physical abilities) provides that reason. They may differ in "can do": the one who has gained more personal resources (job knowledge, judgment, wisdom, socio-emotional abilities) is better able to adjust. And they may differ in "energized to": age-focused HR practices in the organization (a health behavior climate, job crafting training, idiosyncratic deals) energize employees and strengthen the effect of loss and gain on proactive work behaviors.

STEP3
Resource loss and gain with increasing age

What goes down, what goes up, and what stays level

❓ This step answers: Which resources do employees lose and gain with increasing age?
⚖️ Predict eight changes: for each characteristic, decide whether it goes down, goes up, or shows no clear link with age.

The demographic trend toward an older workforce matters for organizations because individuals lose and gain resources as they age. The chapter reviews both sides, largely from the review that is reading 4 of this theme (Truxillo, Cadiz & Hammer, 2015).

Lost. Physical ability is the obvious one, with possible health problems and reduced work ability as a result. Cognitive ability changes too: fluid intelligence declines. And people lose social resources: fewer close friends, possibly less support from grown-up children, and a moderate decrease in social vitality, meaning that people approach life with less anticipation, enthusiasm and excitement.

Gained. Crystallized intelligence increases: knowledge, skills and wisdom. Personality develops in a favorable direction: more conscientiousness and agreeableness, less neuroticism. Although personality traits are usually defined as relatively stable, research shows these changes over the life span (Soto and colleagues, 2011). And emotional abilities grow: older people are better at perceiving and regulating emotions.

Hold on to two things for the rest of the chapter. First, the two lists return, in part, as the two boxes in Figure 1: loss (information processing capabilities, physical abilities, fluid cognitive abilities, health) and gain (job knowledge, judgment, wisdom, socio-emotional abilities). Second, proactivity does not rise with age by itself, even though older employees have both a reason and the means. That is the puzzle the model answers with HR practices.

⚠ The trap✗ Because cognitive abilities decline, older employees perform worse.✓ It is particularly fluid intelligence that declines; crystallized abilities increase. And research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance.
📝 Exam-style questionA manager wants to move all employees over 55 away from customer contact "because they are worn out". Give two findings from the chapter that argue against this.

Model answer (any two): Older people are better at perceiving and regulating emotions, which is very useful in work that involves interacting with clients. Agreeableness and emotional stability increase with age: older workers are more caring, less irritable and less anxious, and cope better. Social stressors may have a smaller impact on older people, who appraise stressful events less negatively. Research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance. Older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones.

STEP4
Age-focused HR practices

Three ways an organization can supply the energy

❓ This step answers: Which HR practices foster engaging aging, according to the chapter?
🏢 Six situations in a company: tap the practice each one shows. The tile then gets its name.

Because employees' resources change so much with age, the authors find it crucial that organizations adjust their usual HR practices, so that people stay healthy, engaged and productive (Truxillo and colleagues, 2015). In the model, age-focused HR practices are the third pillar: they energize employees to be proactive, and they strengthen the translation of "reason to" and "can do" into behavior. The chapter discusses three examples.

1. A health behavior climate. Management communicates, through strategy and vision, that it cares for its employees' health, and it arranges the environment so that healthy behavior is the easy option: a nudge. In this way organizations may indirectly improve employees' health, work engagement and performance.

2. Job crafting training. Employees learn to redefine their job in personally meaningful ways: they learn to identify their most important job demands and job resources, and to set and carry out goals to optimize their job design. This can take the form of a one-day training or workshop that ends in a personal job crafting plan carried out over several weeks. There is evidence that such interventions work, and the authors find them particularly suited to an aging workforce because they are tailored to individual needs and abilities.

3. Idiosyncratic deals (i-deals). Personalized arrangements negotiated between one employee and the organization. They vary in content and differ from employee to employee; the most common are about schedule flexibility and about development, and they can also be about tasks. They may protect against age-related loss (flexible hours allow recovery) and help with learning demands (development), and they show the employee that the organization is willing to deal with individual needs.

⚠ The trap✗ Job crafting training and idiosyncratic deals are the same thing: both fit the job to the person.✓ An i-deal is negotiated with the organization and results in an agreed arrangement. Job crafting is done on the employee's own initiative; the training only teaches and encourages it.
📝 Exam-style questionA hospital wants to keep its nurses over 50 engaged. Propose one measure for each of the three HR practices in the chapter.

Model answer (examples): Health behavior climate: include health behavior in the hospital's strategy and vision and arrange the environment so that healthy choices are the easy ones (for example healthy food and opportunities to be active during shifts). Job crafting training: a one-day workshop in which nurses make a personal job crafting plan and carry it out in the following weeks. Idiosyncratic deals: allow individual nurses to negotiate personalized arrangements, such as flexible working hours that allow recovery from physical strain, or development opportunities to keep up with new technology.

STEP5
Proactive work behaviors

Three behaviors that keep employees engaged

❓ This step answers: What strategies can employees use to stay engaged with increasing age?
🙋 Seven actions, in order: first follow an older manager through three steps, then four more actions. Tap the tile each action belongs to.

The model proposes that aging employees have the reason and the ability to be proactive, that HR practices energize them, and that employees can be encouraged to handle age-related changes in their resources with several proactive behaviors. The chapter discusses three with high potential for positive outcomes.

SOC strategies come from the theory of selective optimization with compensation (Baltes, 1997). Its central proposition: selection, optimization and compensation help individuals to allocate their limited resources optimally. Selection is choosing and prioritizing goals. Optimization is investing additional resources in the selected goals. Compensation is replacing means that no longer work with more effective ones.

Job crafting you know from theme 1. It was originally defined as the changes people make, in what they do and in how they see it, to the tasks and the relationships of their work (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). In the Job Demands-Resources approach it means changing your job demands and job resources on your own initiative, so that the work fits your abilities and preferences better (Tims & Bakker, 2010).

Proactive vitality management is the third behavior. Where job crafting changes aspects of the job or the work environment, this behavior directly manages one's own physical and mental energy, with various small strategies that help to stay vital or regain energy.

⚠ The trap✗ Compensation means lowering your goals when you get older.✓ Choosing fewer or different goals is selection. Compensation keeps the goal and changes the means: using other, more effective ways to reach it.
📝 Exam-style questionA 62-year-old teacher can no longer stand all day. She decides to concentrate on her exam classes, prepares those more thoroughly, and uses a stool and recorded instructions. Label the three SOC strategies.

Model answer: Concentrating on the exam classes is selection: choosing and prioritizing the goals to pursue in order to deal with resource loss. Preparing them more thoroughly is optimization: investing additional resources to achieve the selected goals. Using a stool and recorded instructions is compensation: replacing means that no longer contribute to reaching the goal with more effective means.

But a strategy has to be translated into what employees do, and the author sees HR practices as the translator: green HR practices align selection, training and performance assessment with green objectives. Large-scale sustainability begins with what individuals do.

Three gaps. Tourism research had looked at corporate social responsibility far more than at green HR practices. Research on green behavior had stayed at the level of the individual, although teams matter for carrying out a strategy. And two things had been studied separately but not together: green HRM and leadership. The article's own Table 1 lists twenty key earlier studies. The closest one, Dumont and colleagues (2017), found in a Chinese subsidiary of an Australian paper-packaging manufacturer that green HRM influences in-role and extra-role green behavior through psychological green climate, with individual green values as a moderator. In the table, none examined the interaction of green HR practices with leadership, or a team-level crafting mechanism.

The author names several contributions. The study adds team-level voluntary green behavior as an outcome, next to the individual level. It brings evidence from tourism in a South-East Asian emerging market, where earlier work focused on manufacturing or on other services such as hospitality and education. It uses conservation of resources theory to explain how and when the practices work, with a new mediator, collective green crafting, which joins HRM research and job crafting research. And it examines the interaction between green HR practices and a leadership style that was new to this field: environmentally specific servant leadership.

⚠ The trap✗ The study is about whether green HR practices make employees do their green tasks better.✓ The outcome is voluntary green behavior: citizenship behavior for the environment, which is not specified in the job description.

Organizational citizenship behavior is helpful behavior beyond your formal job. OCBE is the short form the article uses for organizational citizenship behavior for the environment. Collective means: of the team as a whole. Job crafting, from theme 1, is employees changing their own job demands and job resources. An item is one sentence in a questionnaire that people rate. OCBE. Green behavior is treated as a form of organizational citizenship behavior. It reflects how willing an employee is to join the organization and colleagues in behavior that helps the natural environment and goes beyond the job role (Daily and colleagues, 2009). The author extends this to the team: collective OCBE. He follows the three dimensions of Boiral and Paillé (2012), which you met in reading 1 as one of the citizenship scales: eco-civic engagement, eco-helping and eco-initiatives.

Green HR practices. HR practices in general are designed to carry out an organization's HRM strategy. The green ones consist of recruitment and selection, training, performance management, rewarding and involvement, and they aim to build green values as well as knowledge and skills for green activities. Details per practice are in the scene. One point the article stresses: green training should also aim at attitudes and emotional involvement, and integrated training links the programs to performance management, which helps to create a green climate.

Collective green crafting rests on the idea that employees are more motivated when they can redesign their job in terms of tasks and relationships and experience it as more meaningful. Jobs can be crafted individually and collectively. When team members interact and craft resources together in the direction of the team's green goals, that is collective green crafting: more structural and relational resources, more challenging demands, fewer hindering demands, all for green work.

Environmentally specific servant leadership starts from servant leadership: leading with the motivation to serve others, being a role model with empathy and altruistic values, and feeling a moral responsibility for the organization, its employees and the community. The author argues that this style may suit green behavior well, because environmental psychologists suggest that altruistic, other-oriented reasons may bring about green behavior, and because leader conscientiousness and moral reflectiveness have been linked to voluntary workplace green behavior. Open "Servant versus other leadership styles" at the end of the scene for the comparison.

⚠ The trap✗ Collective OCBE is the average of how much voluntary green behavior each team member shows.✓ The article defines it as the synergy of members' efforts in green activities, not the sum of individual contributions. In the study it was rated for the team as a whole, by the manager.

STEP3
The theory behind the predictions

Conservation of resources: who has, invests

❓ This step answers: Why would green HR practices lead to voluntary green behavior, and what are the seven hypotheses?
🎒 Reason like the author: six cases. Each answer lights up the hypotheses it supports.

Resources, in this theory, are personal attributes, energies or conditions that a person values: knowledge, skills, support, self-esteem. Conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll) says people try to keep, protect and build resources; you met it in theme 1. Proactive means acting on your own initiative. In the picture: two situations at the top (ample or limited resources) and three rows of hypotheses that light up as you solve the cases.

The theory has two sides. A lack or loss of resources may have negative psychological effects, so people try to avoid loss; with limited resources they turn defensive and perform at the minimum. With ample resources they take a proactive resource gain strategy: they go above and beyond minimum expectations with proactive, voluntary or extra-role behavior. Bordia and colleagues (2017) see such behavior as a trade: people put resources in and get growing resources back (resource gain spirals), structural (knowledge, skills), social (relationships) and personal (self-esteem, pride). And resources can change form: the resource caravan.

Hypotheses 1 and 2. Green HR practices are organizational-level resources. Training gives green values, knowledge and skills; communication channels keep employees informed; involvement gives opportunities for problem-solving on environmental issues. The practices also create a green learning atmosphere and a norm for behaving pro-environmentally and helping each other do so. With these resources, team members are expected to engage in OCBE individually and collectively. Earlier evidence pointed the same way for green behavior, extra-role green behavior and green creativity.

Hypotheses 3 and 4. Employees are active players. When they perceive ample organizational resources for green activities, they are motivated to build more, and one way is collective green crafting. Crafting then feeds OCBE: for the team through a stronger norm, person-group fit and shared values; for the individual through shared knowledge, peer coaching and feedback, observational learning from more role models, and open communication that helps turn knowledge into initiatives.

Hypotheses 5 to 7. Leadership has been found to moderate the effects of HRM practices. Environmentally specific servant leaders are role models who reinforce the green norm that HR practices cultivate; followers spread these values through the team in a contagion process; managers act as gatekeepers for the norm. Because managers often give less support to green behavior than to other management activities, leadership and HR practices are not equivalent sources of resources: the leader is less likely to replace the HR practices than to add to them.

⚠ The trap✗ The study explains green citizenship behavior with social exchange theory: employees pay the organization back for good treatment.✓ Paying back is the social exchange explanation. The author's main explanation is conservation of resources: people with ample resources invest them to gain more. He offers it as an alternative that does not challenge social exchange theory, and notes that investing in OCBE is also in line with an exchange of resources with the organization.
📝 Exam-style questionA company cuts all green training and stops informing staff about its environmental plans. Predict with conservation of resources theory what happens to voluntary green behavior, and name the strategy.

Model answer: Voluntary green behavior is expected to drop. Green training and information are organizational-level resources. Without them, employees possess limited green-related resources and are prone to a defensive resource strategy: they protect or conserve their remaining resources and perform at the minimum level, so they do not invest in behavior above and beyond minimum expectations such as OCBE or green crafting. With ample resources they would take a proactive resource gain strategy.

STEP4
Research method

Two questionnaires, two months apart, employees and their managers

❓ This step answers: How was the study carried out, and what does that allow us to conclude?
🧪 Be the researcher: six design decisions. Choose what you would do; the timeline fills in with what the study did.

A survey collects answers to questionnaires. A wave is one round of data collection. A sample is the group of people who took part. A scale is a set of items that together measure one concept. A control variable is something measured so that its influence can be taken out. In the picture: a timeline with two moments and four empty boxes.

Who and where. Tour operators based in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, with at least 100 employees, an established green strategy, and tour departments of at least ten employees. The author used his connections and asked chief executives to introduce other operators (snowball sampling, which is not a random sample). Employees and their direct managers in the tour departments were invited by telephone and received different questionnaires by e-mail; only employees who had worked under their current manager for at least one year were contacted.

What was measured. Five scales, all answered on five points: green HR practices (6 items; Dumont and colleagues, 2017), collective green crafting (8 items adapted from the collective job crafting scale of Tims and colleagues, 2013; how often the team did each thing), environmentally specific servant leadership (12 items; Luu, 2018, adapted from the servant leadership scale of Liden and colleagues, 2008), collective OCBE (10 items adapted from Boiral and Paillé, 2012; rated by managers) and individual OCBE (10 items; Boiral and Paillé, 2012). Response: 71% of the invited employees at time 1; the final sample was 51% of the employees and 68% of the managers.

How it was analysed. With multilevel structural equation modeling: a technique that estimates all paths of a model at once and respects that employees are nested in teams. Mediation was tested through the indirect effect with bootstrapped confidence intervals; moderation through interaction terms and simple slopes. The numbers behind the checks are in the card "The checks, for the curious".

⚠ The trap✗ Because the survey had two waves, the study shows that green HR practices cause green crafting.✓ Two waves separate the measurements in time, which helps against common method bias. But it was not a cross-lagged study, and the author states that causal relationships might not be derived.
📝 Exam-style questionName two things the study did to reduce common method bias, and one reason why its measure of green HR practices may still differ from reality.

Model answer: Against common method bias (any two): a two-month time lag between the measurement of the independent variables and of the mediator and outcomes; collective OCBE rated by direct managers and not by the employees; a marker variable test; the moderation tests (such bias would deflate interaction effects, not create them). Why the measure may differ from reality: green HR practices were measured as employees' perceptions, and the author notes that data collected through perceptions may not reflect what actually happens in the workplace; he suggests reports from HR departments for future research.

🧰 What organizations should do, according to the author

Implement green HR practices to reach the green goals. Through green training, give employees green-related resources: awareness and values, other-oriented values, and the knowledge and skills for green activities and eco-initiatives such as eco tours or destination greening programs. Include employee involvement activities that give team members autonomy, so that they can push the green agenda themselves and craft resources for green activities together. Ensure consistency in signals from HR practices and managers. Build environmentally specific servant leadership across the organization through leadership training and succession planning, and let managers share their experience with it in training sessions or through internal communication.

⚠️ Limitations the author names

No cross-lagged design, so causal relationships might not be derived. Data based on participants' perceptions, which may not reflect what actually happens; future studies could use HR department reports for the practices and participant observation for OCBE. Self-reported questionnaires carry a risk of common method bias, although the author judges it not a serious concern here because of the marker variable test, the moderation tests and the two sources. Results from tourism in Vietnam may not generalize to other industries and markets; the collectivistic nature of Vietnamese culture might influence the degree of team green behavior, so the model should be compared in collectivistic and individualistic cultures.

🔍 Three things that do not quite add up (our observation)

The article says the time lag separates independent, mediator and dependent variables, but the mediator and the outcomes were both measured at time 2, and crafting and individual OCBE came from the same employees at the same moment. The reported shares of variance at team and at employee level do not add up to 100%. And the article sometimes uses causal words such as impact and enhanced, although its own limitations say causality cannot be derived. None of this changes the numbers above; it is a reason to describe the results as relationships.

Why is sustainability in supply chains so hard, and who can move it?

Authors

Type
Conceptual article in Organizational Psychology Review (2024): it builds a framework with ten propositions. It collects no data

How can individual employees who work at the border between organizations get sustainability implemented in a supply chain?
Main message
Motivated employees at the border can act as sustainability champions: by proactively regulating their own and their partner's sustainability demands and resources, they may steer sustainability from the bottom up (proposed, not yet tested)

Some words first. A supply chain is the row of organizations a product passes through: from raw materials to production, storage and delivery. A supplier sells to a buying firm; the large firm that leads a chain is called the focal firm. Inter-organizational means between organizations. A conceptual article develops ideas and predictions from existing research; a proposition is such a prediction, offered for others to test. In the picture: a supplier and a buying firm, and between them a place that is still empty.

Think of a group project where your grade depends on a teammate who has no time and no laptop. Sending reminders will not help; lending your laptop might. Supply chains work the same way. Supply chain sustainability means taking people, planet and profit (the triple P, after Elkington, 1997) into account during a product's whole journey: raw materials, production, storage, delivery and transport. A firm's sustainability therefore depends on partners that may lack the expertise, know-how, means or motivation to change.

The authors give three reasons in their plain language summary: several players have to change their behaviour, it is generally costly, and people lack the resources to make a difference. And most management of it is demand-focused: regulations, policies, managerial decisions. On its own that is often ineffective, because the employees who must act lack the resources. In plain words: rules from the top do not change what happens at the supplier's desk.

Change between organizations does not happen through top-down pressure alone; it needs change agents where sourcing decisions are made: the border between organizations. People who work there are boundary spanners; the motivated ones are sustainability champions. Central claim: a champion should improve the partner's balance of sustainability demands and resources, and so indirectly the own balance, because both sides are linked. The authors do not throw out top-down models; they add a bottom-up route.

Three contributions. To supply chain research: a behavioural, bottom-up solution, a possible missing link in a field with little work on individuals. To Job Demands-Resources theory: one employee's crafting can activate an employee in another organization, and this can build into gain or loss spirals. To change research: concrete roles for individuals (the initiator, the resource-providing partner). Table 1 (open it below) shows the micro-level topics studied so far; what employees at the border actually do is not among them.

The article is conceptual: it collects no data, it builds a framework with ten propositions for others to test. Keep that in mind for every step: the propositions are proposed, not shown. Next: the building blocks of the framework, sustainability demands and resources.

STEP2
The building blocks · propositions 2 and 3

Sustainability demands and resources, on both sides

❓ This step answers: What are sustainability demands and resources, and why are those of actor and partner tied together?
⚖️ Sort six situations: a demand or a resource? In the last three, watch for whom.

Job Demands-Resources theory (Demerouti and colleagues, 2001) is the theory from theme 1. Job demands are aspects of work that require sustained physical or psychological effort and therefore have costs. Job resources are aspects of work that help to achieve work goals, reduce demands and their costs, or stimulate learning, growth and development. Demands drain energy; resources can generate motivation; people thrive when the two are in balance. In the picture: two baskets, one for demands and one for resources.

Think of a seesaw with a person on each end: whatever one does moves the other. Job Demands-Resources theory, from theme 1, says work consists of demands (they cost effort) and resources (they help reach goals, reduce demands, or help you grow), and that people thrive when the two are in balance. Implementing sustainability disturbs that balance, and in a supply chain there is a person on each end of the seesaw.

Sustainability demands in the article: sourcing new products, extra information, new ways of storage and transport, cultural differences, hazardous working conditions in manufacturing, compliance checks; also time and money pressure. Sustainability resources: capital, sustainability training, autonomy, know-how, a network, managerial support. They come on top of regular duties such as deadlines and quality. Proposition 2: implementing sustainability adds sustainability demands and requires specific sustainability resources, for actors and partners.

Actor and partner are bound by the exchange between their firms. The article's example: a buyer asks for more sustainable packaging, so the supplier's manager gets a new demand and must find new resources. Expertise is spread over firms, so resources have to be shared. And when demands and resources cross the border they may change in nature: a higher price is a resource for the supplier and a demand on the buyer's budget. Proposition 3: the partners' demands and resources are (inversely) related, so a change on one side prompts an update on the other.

The theory was mostly applied to one person at a time. Studies of colleagues showed the link already: when one employee dropped administrative tasks, a close colleague's workload went up (Tims and colleagues, 2015). The authors move this to two people in different organizations who negotiate the costs. Next: what such a demand does inside the partner.

⚠ The trap✗ A resource is a resource: when a buyer provides resources, everybody's balance improves.✓ Across a boundary, demands and resources may change in nature. What the partner receives as a resource (specialized knowledge, a higher price) may be an additional demand for the actor who has to provide it. This is why proposition 3 says "(inversely) related", and why proposition 7 (step 4) says that helping the partner is costly at first.
📝 Exam-style questionA purchaser asks her supplier for a fully traceable product. Use propositions 2 and 3 to describe what this does to the work of the supplier's account manager, and what it may do to the purchaser's own work.

Model answer: For the account manager the request is an additional sustainability demand on top of economic and operational demands (proposition 2): extra information has to be collected and delivered, and specific resources are needed, such as know-how, time or capital. Because the demands and resources of the two are interrelated (proposition 3), the supplier's side has to update its resource pool, and may need resources from the purchaser's side, such as knowledge or a higher price. Providing those is a resource for the account manager and may be a new demand for the purchaser. (The traceable product is our example.)

STEP3
The right-hand side of the framework · propositions 4 and 5

The same demand: motivation or strain?

❓ This step answers: What happens in the partner when an initiator brings sustainability demands, and what decides whether the result is promoting or undermining behavior?
🤝 Predict first: six cases. Two suppliers get the same demand; watch which route lights up.

Motivation is the wish and energy to do something. Strain is the wear that follows from demands that keep draining a person. An audit is a formal check, here of whether a supplier meets sustainability standards. A code of conduct is a set of rules a buying firm asks its suppliers to follow. A first-tier supplier delivers directly to the focal firm; a second-tier supplier delivers to that supplier. In the picture: on the left what arrives at the partner, in the middle what the partner feels, on the right what the partner does.

Think of a teacher who raises the bar. With tutoring and good books, students rise to it; with only extra tests and penalties, some start to cheat. Job Demands-Resources theory names two routes. In the motivational process, resources build motivation and better performance. In the health-impairment process, chronic demands drain people, causing strain and worse performance. What decides is the balance: resources soften demands (the buffer hypothesis) and demands make resources more valuable (the boosting hypothesis).

A demand from the initiator costs the partner resources. With high access to sustainability resources the drain is buffered: the partner becomes more motivated and shows more sustainability-promoting behaviour. That is proposition 4. Two kinds of support. Among colleagues, an employee who raised challenging demands together with resources increased co-workers' work enjoyment and willingness to collaborate (Fong and colleagues, 2022). Among firms, suppliers reduced violations when buyers gave training or price premiums along with codes of conduct and audits.

With low access to resources, the same demand may lead to strain and to sustainability-undermining behaviour: behaviour that creates obstacles, such as hiding practices or audit fraud. That is proposition 5. Many buyers manage exactly this way: codes of conduct, audits, fines or ending the contract. Relying on sticks without carrots often fails in the long term and can push suppliers with few resources to hide information from auditors (Plambeck & Taylor, 2016).

The trigger sits in another organization: the initiator's demand starts the process, the partner's resources decide the route. So the lesson is not "ask less" but "give as well". The authors hedge with "may": these routes are expected, not yet tested between individual employees. Next: what the champion can do about it.

⚠ The trap✗ Sustainability demands on suppliers are bad: they cause strain and cheating.✓ The demand is not the problem; the balance is. With high access to resources the same demand is a challenge that raises motivation and promoting behavior (proposition 4). Only with low access to resources is it expected to lead to strain and undermining behavior (proposition 5). The framework does not advise firms to stop asking; it advises them to give as well.
📝 Exam-style questionA newspaper reveals that a factory kept two sets of records: one for the auditors of its Western buyers and one real. Explain with the framework how this may have come about, and what the buyers could have done differently.

Model answer: The framework would read this as sustainability-undermining behavior, the end of the impairment route (proposition 5): the people at the factory were challenged with sustainability demands (codes of conduct, audits, the threat of fines or losing the contract) while they had low access to sustainability resources, which may lead to strain and to hiding practices or audit fraud. Buyers could have provided resources along with the demands, such as training, knowledge or price premiums, so that the resources buffer the demands and the motivational route (proposition 4) becomes more likely. (The newspaper case is our example; the article mentions hiding information from auditors and audit fraud.)

STEP4
The left-hand side of the framework · propositions 1, 6, 7 and 8

Regulating my own balance, and my partner's

❓ This step answers: Which proactive strategies can a sustainability champion use, why do they cost something, and what has to be in place first?
🏅 Follow Noor, a buyer (our example): match each thing she does to a tile, then answer two questions about what it takes.

Proactive means acting on your own initiative, before you are told to. Job crafting, from theme 1, is the proactive changing of your own job demands and job resources. The authors use the word regulation for the same kind of behavior, so that it can also cover changing someone else's demands and resources. In the picture: four tiles, two for what a champion does for herself and two for what she does toward the partner.

Think of the oxygen-mask rule on a plane: put on your own mask first, then help your neighbour. A sustainability champion does both. It starts with a spark: an individual's own wish to work sustainably, shaped by attitudes, social norms and perceived control (Swaim and colleagues, 2016). But a spark needs tools: autonomy, support, room to grow. Proposition 1: implementation requires a motivated, proactive initiator who can mobilize enough sustainability resources in the own work environment.

Partner-focused regulation means intentionally changing the demands and resources of a person in another organization: imposing sustainability demands and providing sustainability resources. Firms call this supplier development: direct (investments, training) or indirect (certification, auditing). Proposition 6: actors can use it to improve the partner's balance and trigger the partner's motivation and promoting behaviour. To do it well they need meta-knowledge: knowing what the partner can handle and lacks.

What you give, you no longer have. Resources provided to the partner come out of the actor's own pool; crafting also takes energy and can bring role conflict, disappointment and regret in the short term, and the payoff of crafting someone else's job is even less certain. Proposition 7: partner-focused regulation may draw on the actor's own resources, risking an initial disbalance. In plain words: helping the supplier can hurt your own targets at first.

Buyers often lack autonomy, managerial support, training, capital and room to decide, and their sustainability effort is voluntary, so the organization does not replace what they spend. Self-focused regulation repairs this: optimizing own demands and seeking own resources. Proposition 8: it optimizes the actor's own balance so that partner-focused regulation becomes possible. An earlier study found these behaviours went with more daily proactive sustainability behaviour (Pekaar & Demerouti, 2023). So what: four strategies, two for me and two for my partner. Next: what happens over time.

⚠ The trap✗ Partner-focused regulation is just job crafting with a side effect on others.✓ Earlier research showed that self-directed crafting can affect colleagues as a side effect. Partner-focused regulation is different in two ways: the change in the other person's demands and resources is intended, and the other person works in a different organization. The authors present this as an extension of Job Demands-Resources theory to pairs of people across organizations.
📝 Exam-style questionAn enthusiastic buyer spends months coaching a supplier on cleaner production. Her own targets suffer and her manager is displeased. Which propositions describe her situation, and what would the framework have advised her to do first?

Model answer: She used partner-focused regulation by providing resources (proposition 6). Those resources came from her own pool, which may create an initial disbalance in her own demands and resources (proposition 7): time and energy went to the supplier while her regular demands stayed. The framework advises self-focused regulation first or alongside (proposition 8): seek resources in her own organization, such as managerial support, autonomy, time or budget, and optimize her own demands, so that her own balance can carry the investment. Proposition 1 makes the same point: an initiator needs sufficient resources in the own work environment.

STEP5
The framework over time · propositions 9 and 10

A journey, not a destination

❓ This step answers: What does the framework expect over many exchanges, how does it spread through a longer chain, and how could it be tested?
🌀 Play it forward: six rounds. Watch which spiral grows.

A spiral is a loop that strengthens itself: each round makes the next one more likely. Lagged means with a delay: the effect comes later. A dyad is a pair. Longitudinal research measures the same people several times. In the picture: two empty frames, one for what grows when exchange is balanced and one for what grows when it is not.

Think of a tennis rally: if both keep returning playable balls the rally grows; if one only smashes, the other starts missing and the game falls apart. Job Demands-Resources theory already has loops inside one person: resources lead to engagement, which leads to more resources (gain), or demands lead to strain, which leads to more demands (loss). The article moves these loops between two people in different organizations. In the article's words, sustainability is a journey, not a destination.

A demand makes the partner's resources more valuable and prompts a motivated search for more (boosting). The partner then shares what it finds, so resources come back to the initiator. Proposition 9: a balanced resource exchange is related to a gain spiral in which the initiator's sharing of resources and the partner's promoting behaviour strengthen each other over time. The authors cite reading 5 (Luu, 2019) as an example of gain spirals inside organizations, and add that in today's resource-poor supply chains gain spirals are not likely.

People under strain use poor strategies (poor communication, careless mistakes, conflict) that add to their demands. Between firms: demands without resources push the partner toward undermining behaviour and the initiator toward sharing less. Suppliers audited by many buyers can develop audit fatigue, which distracts from real improvement and may end in audit fraud. Proposition 10: an unbalanced exchange (high demands, low resources) is related to a loss spiral of low sharing and undermining behaviour.

The framework describes a dyad, but it is reversible: a motivated partner can become the initiator toward its own supplier. So people in the chain have a dual role, meeting demands from above and passing demands and resources on. The result can ripple up and down the chain. Fitting finding: first-tier suppliers' resources decide whether they can drive sustainability to second-tier suppliers (Wilhelm and colleagues, 2016).

The propositions are a research agenda. Collect data from both sides of the pair. Choose the time frame: diary or event-sampling studies for quick reactions, a longitudinal panel for slow ones; the spirals need several measurements. Analyse with Actor-Partner Interdependence Models, which separate what my behaviour does for me from what it does for my partner, or simulate the dynamics (agent-based modelling, system dynamics). So what: next, the whole framework in one view.

⚠ The trap✗ The article shows that balanced exchange produces gain spirals in supply chains.✓ It proposes this. The article reports no data of its own. The spirals are derived from Job Demands-Resources theory, from studies of colleagues inside organizations and from firm-level supply chain research. Whether they occur between individual employees of two organizations still has to be tested, with repeated measurements on both sides.
📝 Exam-style questionYou want to test proposition 9. Describe a suitable design: who you measure, how often, and how you analyze it.

Model answer: Measure both members of the pair: employees at the border in two connected organizations, for example buyers and their contact persons at suppliers (multi-source data). Measure several times, because a spiral is a process over time and partners need time to respond: a longitudinal panel, or diary or event-sampling waves. Measure the initiator's sharing of sustainability resources and the partner's sustainability-promoting behavior at each wave, and ideally the organizational exchange too. Analyze with an Actor-Partner Interdependence Model to separate actor and partner effects and to test influence in both directions; a gain spiral predicts that each one's earlier behavior is related to the other's later behavior, with levels rising over time.

Behavioral regulation strategies at the micro level, between organizations, might steer implementation from the bottom up and in the end add up to gain or loss spirals of sustainability resources through the supply chain. The framework is meant to offer new explanations for why sustainability development between organizations is stimulated or held back.

🧰 What to do with it, according to the authors

Employees at the border (actors and partners): recognize your own sustainability demands and resources and craft your job to create room for sustainability; invest in understanding your partner's demands and resources, through more effective and transparent communication, so that partner-focused regulation fits and the response is more collaborative. Leaders: model proactive and sustainable behavior and promote it through resource-providing and empowering leadership. Organizations: select team leads of buyers and salespeople on these criteria or train them; give buyers and salespeople extra resources for sustainability initiatives and reduce the trade-off with other performance outcomes, for instance by including sustainability performance in the key performance indicators (the measures on which people are judged); offer job crafting training. Supply chain managers: encourage resource-sharing across all organizational levels.

🌍 The place of context

The bottom-up focus does not replace top-down models. Local work designs are shaped by context at several levels: global and industrial (government regulation, technology), organizational (strategy, initiatives, climate) and team (social dynamics, leadership). According to the authors, context works mainly through social norms, which communicate what sustainability behavior is expected and whether proactivity is valued, and which shape both self-focused and partner-focused regulation. Context can also disturb the balance directly, through crises such as the pandemic, technological disruptions or new regulations.

⚠️ How sure can we be?

This is a conceptual article. Its propositions are derived from Job Demands-Resources theory, from studies of colleagues inside organizations, and from supply chain research at the level of firms. The step to individual employees in two different organizations is the authors' proposal, and they present the propositions as a research agenda. The note under Figure 1 adds two limits: influence between actor and partner rarely is a direct exchange between two individuals, because it is part of a broader exchange between their organizations, which both can influence only to some degree; and the macro-level process of that exchange (negotiations, market forces) is outside the model. The authors do believe the framework reaches beyond sustainability: in a study of 225 manufacturer-distributor pairs, resource-providing behavior between salespeople and individual buyers was more strongly related to beneficial exchange between the organizations than the same behavior between top executives (Huang and colleagues, 2016).

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

How can employees proactively drive green initiatives within their organizations? This article's answer, and it reaches across the organization's border: by being a motivated initiator with enough resources (proposition 1); by self-focused regulation, that is optimizing the own sustainability demands and seeking sustainability resources (proposition 8); and by partner-focused regulation, that is imposing sustainability demands on the partner together with providing sustainability resources (proposition 6), guided by knowledge of what the partner can handle. What obstacles do sustainability champions face? Sustainability demands on top of regular duties and trade-offs with cost; a work environment that does not support it (little autonomy, managerial support, training, capital, latitude); voluntary investments that the organization does not replace; dependence on a partner who may lack means or motivation; the short-term cost of helping the partner (proposition 7); and the risk of a loss spiral when exchange is unbalanced. How can they deal with them? Secure the own balance first, learn the partner's situation, give resources together with demands, and ask the organization for support: leadership that provides resources, sustainability in the performance measures, and job crafting training.
