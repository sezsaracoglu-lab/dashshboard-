Theme 2 · The psychology of sustainability in organizations · Prepared for Sezen

What makes people at work act green, and stay able to work?

A company can promise to be sustainable. Whether it happens depends on what its people do every day, and on whether those people can keep working well for a whole career. This theme gives you the psychology of both.

The story of this theme · 4 short chapters

What is this theme really for, and how do the readings fit?

🔭 Zoomed out. Theme 1 gave you one engine for the whole course: job demands, job resources, and what employees themselves do with them (job crafting, playful work design). Theme 2 uses that engine for a new question: sustainability. The word has two meanings here. One is sustainability for the planet: how do employees, leaders and human resource practices make an organization greener? The other is sustainability for the people: how do employees stay engaged and employable as they grow older?

Two meetings, eight readings. Meeting 3, Sustainability models, gives the frameworks: employee green behavior (reading 1), the green behavior of leaders (reading 2), staying engaged with increasing age (reading 3) and supporting an aging workforce (reading 4). Meeting 4, Sustainability crafting and interventions, asks what can be done: green human resource practices and collective green crafting (reading 5), sustainability champions (reading 6), comparative feedback (reading 7) and self-concordance (reading 8).

Why a psychologist learns this: technology and laws set the frame, but, as Stern (2000) noted, what organizations do is the biggest direct source of many environmental problems, and what organizations do is the behavior of people. Changing behavior at work is the psychologist's field.

Employee green behavior as the core of environmentally sustainable organizations

Zacher, H., Rudolph, C. W., & Katz, I. M. (2023). Employee green behavior as the core of environmentally sustainable organizations. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 10, 465–494.

STEP1
What employee green behavior is, and what this review does

Not good intentions: countable behavior at work, for better or worse

❓ This step answers: How is employee green behavior defined, and why do the authors call it the core of a sustainable organization?
Authors
Hannes Zacher (Leipzig University), Cort W. Rudolph (Saint Louis University) and Ian M. Katz (Old Dominion University)
Type
Review article in the Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior (2023): it summarizes a whole research field and proposes a model
Built for
Three goals: review the research, build one integrative model, and give recommendations for research and practice
Main message

How is employee green behavior defined, and why do the authors call it the core of a sustainable organization?Why this matters. This is the map reading for the whole theme: every later reading builds on its definition. An organization becomes sustainable (able to go on without using up what the future needs) only through what people in it do, so you need a precise idea of that behavior first.In this step you learn:1The definition, read in parts2Why the behavior is the core of a sustainable organization3Why it counts as work performance, and the forms it takes4What this review sets out to do

⭐ "Scalable actions and behaviors that employees engage in that are linked with and contribute to or detract from environmental sustainability" (Ones & Dilchert, 2012).The review shortens employee green behavior to EGB (quoted on p. 467). Read it in parts:1Scalable: it can be measured, by how often it happens or how well it is done.2Behaviors: what the employee does and controls, not the outcomes of behavior.3Contribute to or detract from: it can help or harm, judged by its impact on the natural environment.So this means: green behavior is countable behavior at work, for better or worse, not good intentions.→ Next: why it matters so much.

⭐ Because organizations cannot reach their environmental goals unless employees at different levels actually perform green behavior.Many organizations now put environmental sustainability goals in their strategy, next to economic and social goals: the triple bottom line. Once that is so, an employee's behavior can be judged by what it adds to or takes from those goals. Our example: a company pledges to halve its waste; that pledge only becomes real when people at the printer, in purchasing and in the board room act on it.So this means: the strategy is on paper; the behavior makes it real.→ Next: how the authors classify this behavior.

⭐ Because green behavior cuts across the established dimensions of work performance, held together by one goal: environmental sustainability.Work performance is goal-directed behavior that contributes to the organization's goals. Its established dimensions include task performance, citizenship (helping beyond your task), counterproductive behavior, team member performance and leadership performance. A compound performance domain is not one more box next to these; it runs through them, and the list is not comprehensive. Adaptive, proactive and innovative performance can also have an environmental impact.So this means: if green behavior is performance, everything known about the causes of performance applies to it (step 4).→ Next: the forms it takes.

⭐ Prescribed or discretionary, helpful or harmful, more relevant to some jobs, and present at every level of the hierarchy.Prescribed: creating sustainable products, delivering services in sustainable ways. Discretionary (left to your own choice): switching the computer off, or wasting paper. It can ask for initiative or creativity, such as voicing suggestions. It matters more for a sustainability officer than for a supermarket cashier, and runs from top managers' purchasing to team members' advocacy among coworkers. It differs from pro-environmental behavior outside work (behavior that harms the environment as little as possible, or even benefits it; Steg & Vlek, 2009), because work brings its own motives, opportunities, constraints, norms, leaders and climate; but the two may share causes such as green attitudes, knowledge and habits.So this means: an exam option saying green behavior is only the good, voluntary things employees do is wrong.→ Next: what the review adds.

⭐ It reviews the field, builds one integrative model, and gives recommendations, looking at causes and consequences at several levels.A review article summarizes what a whole field has found. In 2012, Ones and Dilchert urged organizational psychologists to study the microfoundations of organizational sustainability: the individual behaviors on which the organization's results rest. Since then the field grew: four edited books, six systematic reviews and two meta-analyses. But most studies focused on individual antecedents (things that come before a behavior and may lead to it), such as attitudes, values, perceived norms and intentions. The authors argue for causes and consequences at five levels: individual, team, work context, organization, and society and culture.So this means: the review widens the lens from the person to everything around the person.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Employee green behavior is measurable behavior at work that helps or harms the environment; it is a performance domain and the core of a sustainable organization.1Definition? Scalable behaviors that contribute to or detract from environmental sustainability (Ones & Dilchert, 2012).2Why the core? Environmental goals are reached only through employees' behavior.3Performance domain? It cuts across all performance dimensions.4Forms? Prescribed or discretionary, helpful or harmful, at every level.5What the review does? Review, model, recommendations; causes and consequences at five levels.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which statement about employee green behavior is wrong? ("only voluntary, helpful acts")?Why do the authors call green behavior a compound performance domain??How does employee green behavior differ from pro-environmental behavior outside work?

⚠ The trap✗ Employee green behavior is the list of good, voluntary things employees do for the environment.✓ It covers behavior that helps and behavior that harms, and behavior that is required and behavior that is voluntary. What makes it green behavior is that it is countable behavior at work with an impact on the environment.

What is the authors' model of employee green behavior, and at which levels do its causes and consequences sit?Why this matters. Step 1 defined the behavior. ⭐ Green behavior, described three ways: as a compound performance domain, as self-regulated behavior, and as something studied at several levels.1A compound performance domain with distinct dimensions (task-related, citizenship, proactive, innovative, counterproductive) that may have shared and unique causes; knowledge and skills may matter more for task-related forms, motivation more for discretionary ones.2Self-regulated: employees steer it in a goal-directed way (step 4).3Multilevel: differences between persons, changes within a person over time, and a whole team's behavior added together.So this means: the middle of the map is richer than one behavior; it is a family of behaviors that people steer.→ Next: how the causes reach it.

⭐ Through three direct determinants (knowledge, skills, motivation) and indirect ones that work through them.Direct determinants: knowledge, skills and motivation. Indirect ones: stable individual differences (such as personality) and cognitive and affective states, which act through the direct ones. Our example: a green attitude only turns into recycling if it raises motivation, and the person also knows how and can do it.So this means: every individual cause has to pass through knowing, being able, or wanting.→ Next: and the levels around the person?

⭐ Directly, by giving opportunities or putting up constraints, or indirectly, through the levels below them.The levels are the team, the work context (technology, equipment, job roles such as a sustainability officer, work design such as autonomy), the organization (strategies, initiatives, culture and climate), and society and culture (laws, technology, external stakeholders, cultural values). ⭐ Benefits that may build bottom-up from the individual to the team, the organization and society, possibly giving a competitive advantage and feeding back into more green behavior.For the individual: need satisfaction, identity development, self-efficacy, well-being, job performance, future intentions. Team: norms, climate, team environmental performance. Work context: green tasks and jobs, through job creation and green job crafting. Organization: environmental performance, attractiveness. Society: national and international sustainability goals. Outcomes can build step by step: members act green → green team climate → better team environmental performance → organizational goals met. Some individual outcomes may raise later green behavior: a virtuous cycle.So this means: the model mirrors itself: five levels in, five levels out.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

STEP3
The kinds of green behavior

The Green Five, a sixth, and three lines to place any behavior on

❓ This step answers: Which five dimensions of employee green behavior does the taxonomy distinguish, how do they differ, and what is the sixth?

Which five dimensions of employee green behavior does the taxonomy distinguish, how do they differ, and what is the sixth?Why this matters. A taxonomy is an ordered system of categories. With it, you can name any green behavior in a case, and see which kinds research has neglected.In this step you learn:1Why a taxonomy was built2The Green Five3The sixth: counterproductive sustainability behaviors4Why the required/voluntary split is criticized

⭐ Because early research focused mostly on single green acts, such as recycling paper; an overview of everything employees do was missing.Ones and Dilchert (2012) built the Green Five from thousands of critical incidents (real examples of good or bad behavior) collected in the United States and Europe. It is hierarchical: one overall pro-environmental performance factor, five broad dimensions, and 17 narrow subdimensions. Many constructs in the literature fit into one of the five.So this means: the taxonomy turns a pile of single acts into an ordered map.→ Next: the five.

⭐ Transforming, conserving, avoiding harm, influencing others and taking initiative.🔁Transforming: adapting work products and processes to make them more sustainable; doing required tasks in sustainable ways belongs here.💡Conserving: preserving resources and avoiding waste (reducing use, recycling).🛡️Avoiding harm: reducing negative impact and mitigating damage, including environmental management activities.📣Influencing others: promoting other people's green behavior (educating, training).🚀Taking initiative: proactive, entrepreneurial behaviors (initiating programs, lobbying, activism) involving personal risk and sacrifice.Each has a positive and a negative end (saving energy, wasting energy). Table 1 below gives subcategories and measures.So this means: to classify a behavior, ask what it is about: the process, resources, damage, other people, or initiative.→ Next: what the five leave out.

⭐ Counterproductive sustainability behaviors: actively harmful behaviors that risk or directly result in environmental harm (Dilchert, 2018).Dilchert noted that the taxonomies left out the more severe, actively harmful behaviors. Because sustainability is a strategic goal of many organizations, harming the environment detracts from that goal and violates the organization's legitimate interests: that makes it counterproductive. A related construct is "nongreen behaviors". The five plus this one are the "green six".So this means: wasting energy is the low end of conserving; the sixth is for more severe harm.→ Next: a split the review rejects.

⭐ Because it confounds the motive for a behavior with its content.Ones and colleagues criticized the split: it confounds (mixes up) why a behavior happens with what it is. Not printing e-mails is the same behavior whether a rule demands it or you choose it. So transforming, whose example measure is task-related, can also be discretionary.So this means: classify a green behavior by what it does, not by why the person does it.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ The Green Five (transforming, conserving, avoiding harm, influencing others, taking initiative) plus counterproductive sustainability behaviors make the green six.1Why a taxonomy? Research looked at single acts; Ones & Dilchert (2012) built an overview.2Green Five? Transforming, conserving, avoiding harm, influencing others, taking initiative.3Sixth? Counterproductive sustainability behaviors (Dilchert, 2018).4Required vs voluntary? Confounds motive with content.Typical exam questions on this step:?Classify: an engineer redesigns a process to use less water (transforming)?Which statement is wrong? "Wasting energy is a counterproductive sustainability behavior, outside the Green Five." (it is the low end of conserving)

Along which three lines can any green behavior be classified?Why this matters. The Green Five say what a behavior is about. The three lines (Francoeur and colleagues, 2021) say how it is carried out. Continuous means a line with two ends and everything in between, not two boxes.In this step you learn:1The three lines2What they reveal about the research

The psychological theories behind green behavior

Why people act green, and what it takes to do it

❓ This step answers: Which theories from social and environmental psychology are used to explain employee green behavior?

Which theories from social and environmental psychology are used to explain employee green behavior?Why this matters. Researchers used many theories to understand the web of relations around green behavior (the review calls it the nomological network). Most effort went into the causes. Each theory suggests a different way to change behavior (our reading; the review itself only says that combining the theories could help design interventions).In this step you learn:1Two theories that are easy to mix up2Three more: value-belief-norm, social identity, social exchange3Three models built by organizational scholars

⭐ A theory of rational decision-making: attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control predict intentions, which result in behavior (Ajzen, 1991).An attitude is how positively or negatively you judge something. A subjective norm is what you think important others expect. Perceived behavioral control is how able you feel to do it. An intention is a plan to act. Our example: "I think cycling is good, my team expects it, and I can do it, so I plan to cycle tomorrow."So this means: in this theory, green behavior follows a reasoned plan.→ Next: a theory about moral obligation.

⭐ A theory of moral obligation: personal norms, switched on by awareness of consequences and feeling responsible, drive behavior (Schwartz, 1977).A personal norm is what you feel morally obliged to do. Our example: "If I leave the machine running, the river gets polluted, and that is on me."So this means: an exam option saying planned behavior and norm activation are the same because both use norms is wrong: subjective norm (others) versus personal norm (my own obligation).→ Next: three more theories.

⭐ Each adds a different source of motivation: personal values (value-belief-norm), belonging to a group (social identity), and giving back what you receive (social exchange).1Value-belief-norm theory extends norm activation: personal values predict environmental beliefs, then personal norms and behavior.2A social identity approach: identification with the in-group, its norms and goals, and collective efficacy beliefs predict environmental action.3Social exchange theory: people feel obliged to reciprocate when they receive rewards or bear costs in a relationship.The studies, in one line each: planned behavior: intentions to commute sustainably and to voice suggestions (Yuriev and colleagues, 2020); norm activation: awareness and responsibility led to personal norms, which led to energy saving (Zhang and colleagues, 2013); value-belief-norm: personal norms mediated the link between environmental worldviews and energy conservation (Scherbaum and colleagues, 2008); social identity: units that got comparative feedback (how they did against another unit) saved more energy than units that saw only their own results (Siero and colleagues, 1996; reading 7); social exchange: organizational support went with green behavior among employees with a strong exchange ideology (Paillé & Mejía-Morelos, 2019).So this means: each theory names a different engine: values, group belonging, or giving back.→ Next: models made for organizations.

⭐ Three: the comprehensive action determination model, a multilevel model of pro-environmental motivation, and a process framework of macrodeterminants.Lülfs and Hahn (2014) add situational influences and habits. Norton and colleagues (2015) add controlled ("I have to") and autonomous ("I want to") motivation and outcomes at four levels. Young and colleagues (2015) add group, organizational and external factors. Our reading: each theory points to a lever: change attitudes and control (plan), make consequences visible (moral), use group norms (identity), give support first (exchange). The review only says integrating the theories could help design interventions.So this means: organizational models add the work context that general theories leave out.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Five theories explain green behavior through plans, moral norms, values, group identity or exchange; three organizational models add situation, motivation type and wider factors.1Planned behavior? Attitudes, subjective norms, control → intention → behavior.2Norm activation? Awareness + responsibility → personal norm → behavior.3Other three? Value-belief-norm; social identity; social exchange.4Organizational models? Lülfs & Hahn; Norton et al.; Young et al.Typical exam questions on this step:?What is the difference between the theory of planned behavior and the norm-activation model??Which theory fits: "My team does it, so I do it too"? (social identity)

⚠ The trap✗ The theory of planned behavior and the norm-activation model both say that norms cause green behavior, so they are the same.✓ Different norms, different engines. Planned behavior: the subjective norm (what others expect) is one input into a rational intention. Norm activation: the personal norm (my own moral obligation) is the engine, switched on by awareness of consequences and responsibility.
❓ This step answers: What do theories of work performance add to the explanation of green behavior?

What do theories of work performance add to the explanation of green behavior?Why this matters. If green behavior is work performance, what we know about the causes of performance should apply to it. The authors find it surprising that research neglected these well-established frameworks.In this step you learn:1Campbell's three direct determinants2Opportunity to perform3A simple check for any intervention

⭐ Declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge or skills, and motivation (Campbell's theory of work performance).Declarative knowledge: knowing what. Procedural knowledge or skills: knowing how. Motivation: choice behavior about the direction, intensity and duration of effort. These three mediate (carry) the effects of more distant determinants such as abilities, personality, attitudes, feelings and context. A green attitude may work through motivation; a training through knowledge and skills.So this means: an exam option saying a green attitude by itself leads to green behavior is wrong.→ Next: what the person cannot supply.

⭐ Because, besides ability and motivation, employees must have the opportunity to perform (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982).So contextual factors that facilitate or constrain green behavior must be considered next to individual ones. In the review, opportunities to perform and barriers to perform become the two faces of the context. Our example: a motivated, skilled employee cannot recycle if there are no bins.So this means: know, can, want, and a chance: all four are needed.→ Next: a practical check.

⭐ Ask which of the four parts it addresses, and whether that part was the problem.An information campaign addresses knowledge; a training, skills; feedback and incentives, motivation; recycling bins and job autonomy, opportunity. An intervention aimed at a part that was not the problem will not help. (This check is our way of using the theory.)So this means: diagnose first, then choose the lever.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Green behavior needs knowledge, skills and motivation (Campbell) plus the opportunity to perform; interventions should target the missing part.1Direct determinants? Declarative knowledge, skills, motivation.2Opportunity? Context gives opportunities or barriers (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982).3Using it? Match the intervention to the missing part.Typical exam questions on this step:?Diagnose: employees know how and want to, but there are no bins (opportunity)?Which statement is wrong? "Green attitudes are enough for green behavior."

⚠ The trap✗ If employees have green attitudes, green behavior will follow.✓ An attitude is an indirect determinant. It has to pass through motivation, and the behavior still needs knowledge, skills and the opportunity to perform.
❓ This step answers: How does action regulation theory describe the way employees steer their own green behavior?

How does action regulation theory describe the way employees steer their own green behavior?Why this matters. The authors see green behavior as active and dynamic: employees do not only react to rules, they set goals and act on them. Action regulation theory describes how.In this step you learn:1The sequence of steering an action2The levels of regulation, and habits3What this suggests for research and practice

⭐ A meta-theory of how people regulate goal-directed behavior at work, through a sequence of steps.A meta-theory is a broad theory that frames others. The sequence: developing and selecting goals, orienting yourself in the environment, planning, monitoring the execution, and processing feedback. Our example: Noor sets a goal to cut paper use, checks where paper is used, plans to go digital, watches her progress, and adjusts after a month.So this means: green behavior is steered like any goal at work.→ Next: the levels.

⭐ Through practice, regulation moves down to a lower level (routinization); awareness can move it back up (reintellectualization).The structure: regulation happens at several levels, from the sensorimotor or skills level, through flexible action patterns (routines), and the conscious intellectual level, to meta-cognitive heuristics. Routinization: developing energy conservation habits. Reintellectualization: becoming aware of wasteful behavior again.So this means: an exam option calling a habit the opposite of regulated behavior is wrong: it is behavior regulated at a lower level.→ Next: what follows.

⭐ The authors call for research on how employees set green goals and what helps or hinders their self-regulation; for practice, our reading is: build good habits and make bad ones conscious again.The authors recommend research on how employees develop and select green goals, and how regulation opportunities (autonomy, support) and regulation problems (obstacles, uncertainty, overload) affect them. Our reading for practice: to build a good habit, let people practise until it runs by itself; to break a bad one, first make it conscious again.So this means: changing a habit starts by bringing it back into awareness.→ Next: check what you can now answer.

⭐ Action regulation theory: people steer green behavior through goals, planning, monitoring and feedback, at levels from habits to conscious thought.1What is it? A meta-theory of goal-directed regulation: goal, orient, plan, monitor, feedback.2Habits? Routinization moves behavior down; reintellectualization brings it back up.3Implications? Research on green goals and regulation; build or break habits.Typical exam questions on this step:?Which phase: Noor checks her printing counter weekly? (monitoring)?Which statement is wrong? "A habit is the opposite of regulated behavior."

⚠ The trap✗ A habit is the opposite of regulated behavior.✓ In action regulation theory a habit is behavior regulated at a lower level (flexible action patterns). It got there through practice, and it can be brought back to the conscious level.

The empirical findings

What leads to green behavior, and what it brings

❓ This step answers: What are the individual antecedents of employee green behavior, and how strong are they?

What are the individual antecedents of employee green behavior, and how strong are they?Why this matters. This is the person side of the model, with numbers from a meta-analysis. A correlation says how strongly two things go together (0 not at all, 1 perfectly). A meta-analysis combines many studies into one estimate. The review reports ρ (in our words, the estimated average correlation across studies) and k (the number of studies).In this step you learn:1A warning about cause and effect2Demographics, attitudes, norms and intentions3Job attitudes and personality4The gaps: knowledge, skills and feelings

🧠 Why this matters

For the planet, the argument is simple: Stern (2000) noted that what organizations do is the biggest direct source of many environmental problems, and what organizations do is carried out by employees. For a psychologist, the review turns a moral topic into a workable one: green behavior can be defined, measured, explained with known theories, and supported with known tools (selection, training, feedback, leadership, work design). It also keeps you honest: much of what we "know" rests on one-moment self-report surveys.

Leadership and environmental sustainability

Zacher, H., Kühner, C., Katz, I. M., & Rudolph, C. W. (2024). Leadership and environmental sustainability: An integrative conceptual model of multilevel antecedents and consequences of leader green behavior. Group & Organization Management, 49(2), 365–394.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and which problem they want to solve

"Green leadership" mixes up three things

❓ This step answers: What do the authors find wrong with earlier research on leadership and green behavior, and what do they set out to do?
🧑‍💼 Sort six statements about a leader: is it something she is, knows or wants; something she does; or something that results?
Authors
Hannes Zacher and Clara Kühner (Leipzig University), Ian M. Katz (Old Dominion University) and Cort W. Rudolph (Wayne State University). Three of them wrote reading 1
Type
Conceptual paper in Group & Organization Management (2024): it reports no new data. It reviews studies, defines concepts and proposes a model
Question
How should leadership for environmental sustainability be defined, and how do its causes and consequences hang together across levels?
Main message
Study what leaders do (leader green behavior), not style labels; it is the link between causes and results at the levels of organization, leader and followers

Some words first. In our words, a leader is someone who directs or influences others at work; a follower is someone who is led. A leadership style is a pattern of leader behaviors, usually named with an adjective: transformational (in our words: inspiring with a vision), servant, ethical. An antecedent comes before a behavior and may lead to it; a consequence follows from it. A conceptual paper builds ideas and proposals from existing research; it does not collect data. In the picture: three boxes in a row: before, the behavior, after. Sort six statements about a leader called Dana.

Think of calling a manager a "great coach" (our example): the label already says the result (people improve) without saying what she actually does. The authors find the same problem in green leadership research. Many studies have looked at leadership and employees' green behavior, and a meta-analysis found positive, moderate links with environmentally-specific (green) transformational leadership, servant leadership and supervisory support (Katz and colleagues, 2022). Single studies also explored mechanisms (for example green human resource management) and boundary conditions (for example employees' green identity). The authors value this work but see a basic problem in it: what, exactly, was measured? An "adjective leadership" style bundles a set of behaviors with the good or bad results they are assumed to produce. So the label already contains the leader's assumed intentions and the assumed outcomes. One definition even called environmental leadership an ability: being able to get people and organizations moving toward long-term ecological sustainability (Egri & Herman, 2000). The authors point at the word ability; in our reading the definition names an antecedent and a result, and no behavior.

The authors name two limits of the literature. First, the focus on broad, positively-valenced (positive-sounding) style labels that conflate the content of leader behavior with its possible antecedents or consequences (the term "adjective leadership" is from Day & Antonakis, 2013; the criticism from Fischer & Sitkin, 2023). Second, fragmentation: every study uses its own combination of leadership constructs, mediators, moderators and levels, so the findings do not add up to one picture. What is needed: clear definitions, a separation of antecedents and consequences at several levels, and one integrating model.

From this follow three goals. (1) After a brief review of the research, define leadership in the context of environmental sustainability and the construct of leader green behavior, by combining the behavioral approach to leadership with the theory on employee green behavior from reading 1. (2) Present an integrative model of antecedents and consequences of leader green behavior at the levels of the leader, the followers and the organization, with eight propositions; the structure of pathways is borrowed from a model on leadership and employee health (Wegge and colleagues, 2014). (3) Give recommendations for future research. They name two contributions: applying the six-form taxonomy of employee green behavior to leaders, and positioning leader green behavior as the critical link between antecedents and consequences at three levels.

⚠ The trap✗ The authors show that green transformational leadership does not work.✓ They do not dispute the findings. Their point is about what the label measures: a style mixes behavior with assumed causes and results, so you cannot tell which of them does the work.
📝 Exam-style questionA researcher defines green leadership as "the capacity of leaders to make their teams environmentally friendly". Which criticism from the article applies?

Model answer: The definition conflates leader behavior with its assumed antecedents and consequences. A "capacity" is an antecedent (an ability), and "making teams environmentally friendly" is a consequence (follower behavior). Nothing in the definition says what the leader does. The authors call for clear construct definitions that describe concrete leader actions and keep antecedents and consequences separate.

STEP3
The two definitions the article stands on

Leadership as a process, leader green behavior as actions

❓ This step answers: How do the authors define leadership in the context of environmental sustainability, and what is leader green behavior?
🌍 Trace six actions: each one reaches the environment by a route. Tap the route.

A process is something that unfolds between people over time, as opposed to a property of one person. Scalable means it can be measured on a scale, here by how proficiently (how well) it is done. Direct means the leader's own act touches the environment; indirect means it works through something or someone else. In the picture: a leader on the left, the environment on the right, and four routes between them. Six actions; tap the route each one takes.

First definition. Leadership in the context of environmental sustainability is a process: one or more people (the leaders) use their own actions to steer what other people (the followers) do, and/or what the organization as a whole does, in the direction of environmental sustainability goals. Those involved share these goals to a greater or lesser degree (the authors build on a general definition of leadership by Judge & Long, 2012). Environmental sustainability itself means dealing responsibly with the natural environment so that quality of life on Earth can continue. Three things to notice: it is a process and not a trait; the target can be followers or the organization (for example by introducing a new policy); and the goals need only be "more or less" shared.

Second definition. Leader green behavior (also: leader pro-environmental behavior) is built on the definition of employee green behavior from reading 1: the set of things a leader does on purpose, that can be rated for how well they are done, and that help or harm environmental sustainability. The actions are under the leader's control. They benefit or harm the environment directly, or indirectly through changes in the context (new policies or work tasks), in follower behavior (orders, inspirational motivation, behavioral modeling) or in organizational behavior (influence on environmental investment decisions).

The domain is divided with the green six from reading 1 (the Green Five plus counterproductive sustainability behaviors), and placed on the same three lines: in-role or extra-role, direct or indirect, low or high intensity. Two more points. According to Ones and colleagues (2018), green behaviors can be performed at different levels of the hierarchy. And the authors add that leader green behaviors may be shown by individuals without an official leadership role: shared or emergent leadership. Open the two cards at the end of the scene for the dimensions and lines with leader examples.

Why this definition is better than a style, according to the authors: a style only describes the pattern of behaviors a leader uses to influence followers. The green six also cover what leaders do themselves (direct behaviors) and what they change in the work and organizational context. And because the definition names actions only, antecedents and consequences stay outside it and can be studied separately. That is the precondition for the model in the next steps.

⚠ The trap✗ Leader green behavior is the "influencing others" part of the Green Five.✓ It is all six dimensions. Influencing others is one of them. A leader who recycles, redesigns a process or flies unnecessarily also shows leader green behavior.
📝 Exam-style questionA plant manager changes the production schedule so that machines run when solar power is available. Classify this with the definition: direct or indirect, and which dimension?

Model answer: It is leader green behavior that works indirectly, through a change in contextual work characteristics (the schedule), and it belongs to transforming: adapting work processes to make them more environmentally sustainable. If scheduling is part of the manager's prescribed duties it is in-role. (One could also argue for conserving; the key point is the route: through the work context.)

STEP4
The model, part 1 · Figure 1 · propositions 1 to 3

What leads to leader green behavior

❓ This step answers: According to the model, what makes a leader show green behavior?
🧩 Six cases about Ravi, a team leader: choose the path the model proposes. The numbered pin of that proposition lights up on the model.

A proposition is a statement the authors propose to be true and that research still has to test. Multilevel means the model has layers: here the organization (highest), the leader, and the followers (lowest). A pathway is a route by which one thing influences another. An interaction means that the strength of one effect depends on something else. In the picture: the model as a grid. Rows are levels. Columns run from what comes before, through green behavior, to what follows. Eight pins mark the eight propositions; this step lights 1, 2 and 3.

The model has processes and levels. Leader green behavior sits in the centre. In Figure 1, dark arrows are effects of or on leader green behavior; light arrows are effects among other constructs; solid arrows are more-established propositions and dashed arrows less-established ones. The eight propositions come in three groups: what leads to leader green behavior (1, 2, 3), how it influences followers and context (4, 5), and what it brings (6, 7, 8).

Proposition 1 applies the performance theory from reading 1 to leaders. Leader green behavior is primarily influenced by three direct determinants: declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge or skills, and motivation (choices about the direction, intensity and duration of effort). Abilities, values, personality, attitudes, beliefs and affect influence it indirectly, via those three. Parts of this are supported by studies; the chain as a whole is new and untested.

Proposition 2 is, in our view, the most unusual one: a bottom-up effect. Followers influence their leader (a) indirectly, by educating, persuading or encouraging the leader (their own "influencing others" behavior), which changes the leader's antecedents; and (b) directly, because leaders may observe and model what followers do, green or counterproductive.

Proposition 3 brings in the context: the organization's strategy, initiatives, culture, climate, equipment, materials, technology and work design. It influences leader green behavior (a) indirectly via leader antecedents, (b) directly, and (c) by changing the strength of the effects of leader antecedents. Part (c) rests on two theories: situation strength and trait activation. Evidence the authors cite for the context in general: the meta-analytic links of corporate social responsibility, green human resource management, perceived organizational support and green psychological climate with green behavior, and studies showing that having an environmental policy goes with more green behavior.

⚠ The trap✗ Situation strength theory and trait activation theory predict the same thing.✓ Both are about interaction, and the article presents them as complementary. A strong situation (a clear policy that limits discretion) makes the leader's own motivation matter less. A trait-activating context (a green office design) makes it show more.
📝 Exam-style questionA team of nurses repeatedly asks their head nurse to replace disposable materials. After some weeks she starts a pilot. Which proposition describes this, and which of its two paths?

Model answer: Proposition 2: follower green behavior influences leader green behavior. It is path (a), the indirect path via leader antecedents: the nurses use the "influencing others" dimension (educating, persuading, encouraging) and thereby change the head nurse's knowledge, skills, motivation or attitudes, which lead to her behavior. Path (b) would be modeling: the leader copying green behavior she observes in her followers.

STEP5
The model, part 2 · propositions 4 and 5

How a leader's green behavior reaches followers

❓ This step answers: What is the role of leadership in promoting employee green behavior: by which pathways does it work?
🧭 Six cases about Maya and her team: tap the tile that describes how her behavior reaches the team. The tile then gets its name.

This is where the model answers the question most studies asked: how does leadership relate to followers' green behavior? Instead of one arrow, the authors propose several pathways, borrowed from a model of how leadership affects employee health. The authors call propositions 4 and 5 reverse pathways: they run from the leader back to followers and context. The pathways differ in what the leader works on: the follower as a person, her own example, or the system around the follower.

Proposition 4. Leader green behavior influences follower green behavior (a) indirectly via follower antecedents: person-focused action, in which the leader trains, instructs, persuades and encourages and thereby improves followers' knowledge, skills and motivation or their attitudes; (b) directly through behavioral modeling: followers observe and copy the leader's green or counterproductive behaviors, so leaders may serve a powerful role-model function; and (c) by changing the strength of the effects of follower antecedents on follower behavior, in line with situation strength and trait activation theories.

Proposition 5. Leader green behavior also works through the wider context. (a) System-focused action: by changing contextual antecedents such as policies, practices and work design, leaders indirectly influence follower antecedents, follower behavior, or the link between them. (b) Moderating action: leaders strengthen or soften the impact that contextual antecedents have on followers, for example by deciding whether and how to implement a new performance management policy that includes sustainability.

For a leader who wants a greener team, the model therefore gives a wider repertoire than "be inspiring": teach and encourage, do it yourself where your team can see it, change what you can in policies and work design, and take care how organizational policies land in your team. Our summary; the article itself draws conclusions for research, not for practice. Also note that the same routes can carry harm: a leader who models counterproductive behavior can be copied too.

⚠ The trap✗ Trickle-down means the leader tells followers what to do.✓ In the article, the trickle-down effect studied by Shao and colleagues (2023) is about modeling: followers see the leader's own green behavior, which works through role model influence and their green self-efficacy. Telling and training is person-focused action.
📝 Exam-style questionA director introduces a rule that every project proposal needs a carbon estimate. One department head applies it strictly; another lets it slide. Name the pathway each person illustrates.

Model answer: The director shows system-focused action (proposition 5a): she changes a contextual antecedent, an organizational practice, which then influences followers' antecedents and behavior. The two department heads show moderating action (proposition 5b): by deciding whether and how to implement the practice, they strengthen or weaken its impact on their followers' green behavior.

What leader green behavior brings, and what we actually know

❓ This step answers: Which consequences does the model propose for leaders, followers and the organization, and how much evidence is there?

The right-hand column of the model holds the consequences, one box per level. Here the model is at its most speculative for leaders and followers and has the most studies behind it for the organization, although far fewer than there are on follower behavior. Because this is a conceptual paper, read every sentence in this step as "the authors propose", and look at the dots for how much has been studied.

Proposition 6. Leader green behavior influences individual leader consequences: need satisfaction, environmental performance and leadership success, depending on relevant leader and contextual antecedents. The condition is congruence: green behavior satisfies needs when it fits the leader's environmental values, and it earns favorable evaluations of leadership success when the organization values sustainability. No study has examined this yet.

Proposition 7. Leader green behavior influences (a) follower consequences, such as need satisfaction, and (b) the strength of the effects of follower green behavior on follower consequences. The second part means: whether acting green pays off for a follower depends on the leader, who can support and reward it or penalize its absence. In Figure 1 the follower consequences are need satisfaction and individual and team environmental performance.

Proposition 8. Leader green behavior influences (a) organizational consequences, including environmental performance and organizational attractiveness, and (b) the strength of the effects of contextual antecedents on organizational consequences. It contributes directly, and indirectly through the leader and follower consequences of propositions 6 and 7: the bottom-up logic of emergence. Example for (b): high environmental leadership strengthened the link between environmental management practices and green innovation performance (Zhang & Ma, 2021).

⚠ The trap✗ The article shows that green leaders are happier and more successful.✓ That is proposition 6, and the authors state that no research has examined effects of leader green behavior on leader outcomes. It is also conditional: it depends on the leader's values and on whether the organization values sustainability.
📝 Exam-style questionTwo leaders show the same green behavior. One feels fulfilled and is praised; the other feels nothing special and is seen as distracted from "real" work. Explain with proposition 6.

Model answer: Proposition 6 says the consequences for the leader depend on relevant leader and contextual antecedents. The first leader probably has strong environmental values, so green behavior satisfies needs (and then well-being), and works in an organization that values sustainability, so the behavior is evaluated as leadership success. For the second leader the behavior is not congruent with personal values and/or the organization does not value sustainability. The authors note this proposition has not been tested.

STEP7
The authors' recommendations for future research

How to study leader green behavior properly

❓ This step answers: What do the authors recommend for theory and for methods?
🧪 Six questions for a research designer: choose first. Three of them fill in what each instrument really measures.

A moratorium is a pause: here, stop developing and using adjective style labels. Self-transcendence (Schwartz) is a group of values about caring beyond yourself; in our words: universalism (concern for all people and nature) and benevolence (concern for people close to you). For theory, the authors suggest: treat leadership as a process and define leader green behavior as concrete actions, using the green six; put a moratorium on adjective styles; study the micro-dynamics of how leader antecedents interact with follower behavior and context, for example with action regulation theory (goal development, orientation, planning, execution, feedback, and the forming, changing and abandoning of habits); examine whether there is one higher-order leader green behavior factor above the six dimensions; and extend the model. The model treats the followers of one leader as a team, nested within that leader. Three extensions are named. The societal and cultural level: national regulations, traditions and values, such as Schwartz's value of self-transcendence with its universalism and benevolence dimensions. Team and within-person processes: coworker advocacy, team climate, and how leader and follower green behavior fluctuate over time. And fit between leader and follower: what happens when their environmental knowledge, motivation or behavior differ or clash, how leaders deal with followers who resist or boycott green efforts, and how conflicting messages between organization, leaders and followers limit the leader's influence.

For methods, five points, which you played in the scene: know what each measure captures; use several sources and time points, and experiments for causality; sample different hierarchical levels to study trickle-down and bottom-up effects; test the incremental contribution beyond established leadership constructs; and compare predictors at different levels in one study.

These recommendations are also a checklist for reading the other articles in this theme. When a study claims that a leadership style "leads to" green behavior, ask: was it a style or a behavior, one moment or several, one source or several, and was anything manipulated?

⚠ The trap✗ Objective measures such as energy savings are the best measure of green behavior.✓ They are objective, but they measure outcomes, which may not be fully under a person's control. The authors treat them as indicators of environmental consequences, to be combined with other sources.

STEP1

❓ This step answers: What is work engagement, why does it matter, and what does the chapter want to find out about age?

Authors
Arnold B. Bakker and Jari J. Hakanen
Type
A short book chapter (2019), written as Wilmar Schaufeli's retirement approaches; his definition and questionnaire set off work engagement research

Some words first. A resource is anything that helps you reach your goals or cope: energy, skills, health, support. Personal resources sit in the person; job resources sit in the job. HR practices are what an organization does to manage its people (HR = human resources). Proactive means acting on your own initiative, ahead of problems. In the picture: four tiles with a plain description. Five employees speak; tap the tile that fits.

Work engagement is defined as a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication and absorption (Schaufeli and colleagues, 2002). Vigor: high levels of energy and the willingness to invest effort in the job. Dedication: being strongly involved in the work and experiencing significance, enthusiasm and inspiration. Absorption: being fully immersed in work activities, so that time passes quickly. For two decades most research has measured it with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale.

Engagement differs from other positive states such as happiness and job satisfaction because it is an activated state. A satisfied person can feel calm and content; an engaged person feels excited and enthusiastic, and is therefore prepared to put thinking power and energy into the work. That is also why it matters to organizations: open "Why it is so popular" at the end of the scene for what it has been related to.

Research on what leads to engagement produced Job Demands-Resources theory, which you know from theme 1. Engagement is most likely when job challenges are combined with personal or job resources: interesting, challenging tasks together with social support, autonomy and skill variety; or work pressure and deadlines on days when people feel optimistic and self-efficacious. One principle of the theory carries this chapter: employees need to be proactive to stay engaged.

Now the question. The average age of the workforce in industrialized countries is clearly rising. Findings so far point to a positive link between age and engagement: older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones (Hakanen and colleagues, 2019). The authors want to explain how engagement can be kept up with increasing age. Their plan: look at what people lose and gain as they age, combine that with what is known about proactivity, and build a model they call "engaging aging".

⚠ The trap✗ An employee who is satisfied with the job is engaged.✓ Satisfaction can be calm and passive. Engagement is an activated state: energy (vigor), meaning (dedication) and immersion (absorption).
📝 Exam-style questionA nurse says: "My shifts fly by, I feel strong and energetic, and my work really means something to me." Name the three dimensions of work engagement in her words.

Model answer: "My shifts fly by" is absorption: being fully immersed, so that time passes quickly. "I feel strong and energetic" is vigor: high levels of energy and willingness to invest effort. "My work really means something to me" is dedication: strong involvement with a sense of significance, enthusiasm and inspiration.

The model of engaging aging

A model is a drawing of how the authors think things hang together; this one is a proposal. A motivational state is a condition that makes you want and able to act. A moderator is something that makes a link stronger or weaker. A plus sign on an arrow means: more of the first goes with more of the second. In the picture: age on the left; loss of resources above, gain below; HR practices and proactive behavior in the middle; work outcomes on the right.

The authors use proactivity theory to argue that all employees have to keep fine-tuning what they do at work and in their free time if they want to stay engaged and perform well, and that this is more needed with increasing age. Proactive behavior at work is self-initiated, anticipatory action, such as taking charge, voicing concerns and seeking feedback (Parker & Bindl, 2017). There is considerable evidence that it goes with favorable performance outcomes: person and job come to fit better, people get more chances to use their resources and strengths, and work may become more meaningful (Kooij and colleagues, 2017).

According to Parker, Bindl and Strauss (2010), three motivational states prompt and sustain proactivity. "Can do": do I believe I can, is it feasible, and what will it cost me (self-efficacy, feasibility, costs)? "Reason to": why would I? Feeling personally responsible, and wanting from within to take initiative. "Energized to": do I have the energy? A state of feeling and motivation that supplies the energy for proactive action.

The chapter connects each state to aging. Gain of personal resources facilitates "can do". Loss of personal resources provides the "reason to": as physical abilities slowly decline and the body recovers its balance less easily, the need to be proactive grows. And because age itself is only weakly and unsystematically related to proactive behavior, something has to supply the energy: age-focused HR practices initiated by the organization. They also act as moderators, and loss and gain may have a combined impact, as conservation of resources theory suggests.

⚠ The trap✗ In the model, losing resources makes older employees less proactive.✓ The model proposes the opposite: loss provides the reason to be proactive. What may be missing is the energy, which is why HR practices are in the model.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain with the model why two 60-year-olds with the same health complaints may differ in how proactively they adapt their work.

Model answer: Both have a "reason to" be proactive, because loss of personal resources (health, physical abilities) provides that reason. They may differ in "can do": the one who has gained more personal resources (job knowledge, judgment, wisdom, socio-emotional abilities) is better able to adjust. And they may differ in "energized to": age-focused HR practices in the organization (a health behavior climate, job crafting training, idiosyncratic deals) energize employees and strengthen the effect of loss and gain on proactive work behaviors.

STEP3
Resource loss and gain with increasing age

What goes down, what goes up, and what stays level

❓ This step answers: Which resources do employees lose and gain with increasing age?
⚖️ Predict eight changes: for each characteristic, decide whether it goes down, goes up, or shows no clear link with age.

The demographic trend toward an older workforce matters for organizations because individuals lose and gain resources as they age. The chapter reviews both sides, largely from the review that is reading 4 of this theme (Truxillo, Cadiz & Hammer, 2015).

Lost. Think of a long-distance runner in her fifties (our example): slower, but running smarter races. Physical ability declines, with possible health problems and lower work ability. Fluid intelligence, the ability to reason and handle new information (working memory, abstract reasoning, attention, processing of novel information), declines too. And people lose social resources: fewer close friends, possibly less support from grown-up children, and a moderate decrease in social vitality: approaching life with less anticipation, enthusiasm and excitement.

Gained. Crystallized intelligence, the knowledge, skills and wisdom built up over the years, increases. Personality develops in a favorable direction: more conscientiousness (organized, careful) and agreeableness (caring for others), less neuroticism (irritable, anxious); traits are fairly stable, but research shows these shifts over the life span (Soto and colleagues, 2011). Emotional abilities grow: older people are better at perceiving and regulating emotions. So what: loss gives a reason to act, gain gives the means.

Hold on to two things for the rest of the chapter. First, the two lists return, in part, as the two boxes in Figure 1: loss (information processing capabilities, physical abilities, fluid cognitive abilities, health) and gain (job knowledge, judgment, wisdom, socio-emotional abilities). Second, proactivity does not rise with age by itself, even though older employees have both a reason and the means. That is the puzzle the model answers with HR practices.

⚠ The trap✗ Because cognitive abilities decline, older employees perform worse.✓ It is particularly fluid intelligence that declines; crystallized abilities increase. And research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance.
📝 Exam-style questionA manager wants to move all employees over 55 away from customer contact "because they are worn out". Give two findings from the chapter that argue against this.

Model answer (any two): Older people are better at perceiving and regulating emotions, which is very useful in work that involves interacting with clients. Agreeableness and emotional stability increase with age: older workers are more caring, less irritable and less anxious, and cope better. Social stressors may have a smaller impact on older people, who appraise stressful events less negatively. Research generally shows no significant relationship between age and job performance. Older employees are slightly more engaged than younger ones.

STEP4
Age-focused HR practices

Three ways an organization can supply the energy

❓ This step answers: Which HR practices foster engaging aging, according to the chapter?
🏢 Six situations in a company: tap the practice each one shows. The tile then gets its name.

Because employees' resources change so much with age, the authors find it crucial that organizations adjust their usual HR practices, so that people stay healthy, engaged and productive (Truxillo and colleagues, 2015). In the model, age-focused HR practices are the third pillar: they energize employees to be proactive, and they strengthen the translation of "reason to" and "can do" into behavior. The chapter discusses three examples.

1. A health behavior climate. Management communicates, through strategy and vision, that it cares for its employees' health, and it arranges the environment so that healthy behavior is the easy option: a nudge. In this way organizations may indirectly improve employees' health, work engagement and performance.

2. Job crafting training. Employees learn to redefine their job in personally meaningful ways: they learn to identify their most important job demands and job resources, and to set and carry out goals to optimize their job design. This can take the form of a one-day training or workshop that ends in a personal job crafting plan carried out over several weeks. There is evidence that such interventions work, and the authors find them particularly suited to an aging workforce because they are tailored to individual needs and abilities.

3. Idiosyncratic deals (i-deals). Personalized arrangements negotiated between one employee and the organization. They vary in content and differ from employee to employee; the most common are about schedule flexibility and about development, and they can also be about tasks. They may protect against age-related loss (flexible hours allow recovery) and help with learning demands (development), and they show the employee that the organization is willing to deal with individual needs.

⚠ The trap✗ Job crafting training and idiosyncratic deals are the same thing: both fit the job to the person.✓ An i-deal is negotiated with the organization and results in an agreed arrangement. Job crafting is done on the employee's own initiative; the training only teaches and encourages it.
📝 Exam-style questionA hospital wants to keep its nurses over 50 engaged. Propose one measure for each of the three HR practices in the chapter.

Model answer (examples): Health behavior climate: include health behavior in the hospital's strategy and vision and arrange the environment so that healthy choices are the easy ones (for example healthy food and opportunities to be active during shifts). Job crafting training: a one-day workshop in which nurses make a personal job crafting plan and carry it out in the following weeks. Idiosyncratic deals: allow individual nurses to negotiate personalized arrangements, such as flexible working hours that allow recovery from physical strain, or development opportunities to keep up with new technology.

STEP5
Proactive work behaviors

Three behaviors that keep employees engaged

❓ This step answers: What strategies can employees use to stay engaged with increasing age?
🙋 Seven actions, in order: first follow an older manager through three steps, then four more actions. Tap the tile each action belongs to.

The model proposes that aging employees have the reason and the ability to be proactive, that HR practices energize them, and that employees can be encouraged to handle age-related changes in their resources with several proactive behaviors. The chapter discusses three with high potential for positive outcomes.

SOC strategies come from the theory of selective optimization with compensation (Baltes, 1997): selection, optimization and compensation help people use their limited resources as well as possible. Think of a 60-year-old pianist (our example) who plays fewer pieces (selection: choosing and prioritizing goals), practises those more (optimization: investing extra resources in the chosen goals), and plays slower passages before fast ones to make them sound faster (compensation: replacing means that no longer work with more effective ones). At work (also ours): a nurse who focuses on training new colleagues, takes a coaching course, and uses a checklist for details she now forgets.

Job crafting you know from theme 1. It was originally defined as the changes people make, in what they do and in how they see it, to the tasks and the relationships of their work (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). In the Job Demands-Resources approach it means changing your job demands and job resources on your own initiative, so that the work fits your abilities and preferences better (Tims & Bakker, 2010).

Proactive vitality management is the third behavior. Where job crafting changes aspects of the job or the work environment, this behavior directly manages one's own physical and mental energy, with various small strategies that help to stay vital or regain energy.

⚠ The trap✗ Compensation means lowering your goals when you get older.✓ Choosing fewer or different goals is selection. Compensation keeps the goal and changes the means: using other, more effective ways to reach it.
📝 Exam-style questionA 62-year-old teacher can no longer stand all day. She decides to concentrate on her exam classes, prepares those more thoroughly, and uses a stool and recorded instructions. Label the three SOC strategies.

Model answer: Concentrating on the exam classes is selection: choosing and prioritizing the goals to pursue in order to deal with resource loss. Preparing them more thoroughly is optimization: investing additional resources to achieve the selected goals. Using a stool and recorded instructions is compensation: replacing means that no longer contribute to reaching the goal with more effective means.

Some words first. An intervention is a planned change meant to improve something, such as a training or a new policy. A review summarizes the research on a topic. Age-diverse means mixing people of different ages. IO/OB is short for industrial-organizational psychology and organizational behavior: the research fields of work psychology. In the picture: four boxes: the trend, the gap, the definition and the outcomes.

Think of a library full of books on why a bridge wears out, with hardly a page on how to repair it (our analogy). People are aging more slowly and will need to work longer to fund a longer retirement and keep pension systems going; many countries are raising the retirement age, and the economic downturn left many with too little savings. So workforces get older, and older and younger people work together as never before. As many as 60% of workers aged 45 to 74 said they worked mainly for financial reasons. Organizations and societies need to keep these workers healthy and well, and keep relations between generations good.

Research on age at work has boomed: motives, stereotypes, age climate, caregiving, retirement. Age has moved from a control variable (something researchers merely correct for) to a main topic. But intervention research at work has almost never focused on age: hardly any studies test real programmes or policies to see whether they help workers of different ages. The authors want to fill that gap with theory-based proposals.

Who is "older" depends on job, culture and law; in the US legal age protections start at 40, and the idea shifts as people age more slowly. The authors use older worker for people approaching retirement age or working a bit beyond it, in the US people in their late 50s and 60s, and consider changes across the normal working life (early 20s to 70s). Because individuals age differently, changes within one person over time may matter more than comparing age groups.

The review aims at better proximal (close-by) outcomes (work-life balance, job satisfaction, engagement, burnout, strain) and distal (further away) ones (safety, performance, health, work ability, retirement, turnover, life satisfaction). Its route: what changes with age inside the person (step 2), how people adapt (step 3), the social context (step 4), interventions (step 5) and research needs (step 6).

⚠ The trap✗ The review evaluates which workplace interventions for older workers work best.✓ It finds that almost no such interventions have been tested. It reviews what is known about aging at work and proposes interventions for future research.
📝 Exam-style questionGive two reasons why the workforce in industrialized countries is getting older, and explain what the authors mean when they say age should move beyond being a control variable.

Model answer: People are aging more slowly and must work longer to support themselves in a longer retirement and to keep retirement systems going; many countries raise the retirement age; and many workers have too little retirement savings after the economic downturn. "Beyond a control variable": in earlier research age was only statistically corrected for; the authors want it treated as a focus of study and of interventions, asking what employers and societies can do to support workers as they age.

STEP2
Within-person changes · Table 1

Losses, gains and shifts

❓ This step answers: What changes physically, cognitively, emotionally, in personality and in motivation as people age?
🔽🔼 Sort six changes: does it decline, increase, or is it mixed?

Within-person changes happen inside one person over time. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. A trait is a stable characteristic, such as conscientiousness (careful, reliable) or neuroticism (prone to worry). In the picture: three columns: declines, increases, and mixed.

Think of a car that gets slower off the line but has a better navigation system and a calmer driver (our analogy). Aging is not one long decline. Table 1 lists changes in five areas: physical, cognitive, affective (emotions), personality and motivation. Some go down, some go up, some shift. And people differ a lot in how they age, more so in later years.

Sensory, muscular, aerobic and immune functions decline, and the body is slower to restore balance (homeostasis), so heat, cold, humidity or night shifts take longer to recover from. Clinical health problems rise with age (disabilities especially after 50), but self-reported physical problems do not. Older workers have fewer injuries but recover more slowly. There is little research linking these declines to lower job performance, which suggests people adapt.

Fluid intelligence (processing speed, working memory, attention) declines; perceptual speed starts to fall at 25 and declines significantly after 60 (Schaie, 1994). Crystallized intelligence (knowledge, skills, wisdom) may peak around 60 and declines only gradually. This may explain why age and job performance show no consistent negative relation: Gc gains may offset Gf losses. Jobs needing peak Gf (air traffic control) fit older workers less; jobs using accumulated knowledge (senior managers) fit well. People with more education, good health and complex, challenging jobs seem to age more resiliently in cognition (Rizzuto and colleagues, 2012).

Emotional well-being and emotion regulation improve with age (the positivity effect): explanations offered are expertise gained through experience, a shift to emotional goals (socioemotional selectivity, step 3) and strategies to avoid negative events (strength and vulnerability integration theory). Scheibe and Zacher (2013) propose that older adults may be less affected by social stressors, because they appraise stress less negatively (emotion generation: the first appraisal of an event) and focus on positive cues, but more by extreme or chronic stressors, because recovery takes longer (emotion regulation: managing and coping with it). Conscientiousness and agreeableness rise and neuroticism falls; within extraversion social dominance rises and social vitality falls. Over 15 years, young professionals who became more extraverted and conscientious and less neurotic also became more satisfied with their jobs; satisfaction in turn influenced changes in agreeableness, so the influence runs both ways (Wille and colleagues, 2014). (The Big Five are five broad personality traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism; a facet is a narrower part of one trait.)

A meta-analysis found age related positively to intrinsic motives (accomplishment, connection, autonomy), helping others and contributing to society (generativity) and job security, and negatively to growth (achievement, mastery) and extrinsic motives (pay, advancement) (Kooij and colleagues, 2011). Interest in training falls too. In plain words: a 60-year-old may care less about the next promotion and more about doing meaningful work and passing on what she knows (our example). Motivation does not simply decline; it changes.

Interventions should compensate for losses, use the gains, and fit work to changing motives, while treating older workers as individuals rather than one group. Next: the theories that explain how people adapt.

STEP3
Life-span development theories

Choose, invest, work around

❓ This step answers: Which theories explain how people adapt to aging, and what do they predict at work?
🧭 Follow Fatima, a 61-year-old nurse (our example): match what she does to a strategy, then two questions.

Life-span development theories describe how people change and adapt across their whole life. To adapt is to adjust so that you keep functioning well. In the picture: four tiles: three strategies of one theory and one other theory.

Think of a footballer in his thirties (our example): he plays fewer minutes, trains smarter, and relies on positioning instead of speed. The first life-span theory, selection, optimization and compensation (SOC) (Baltes & Baltes, 1990), says people use three strategies all through life to match their resources to the demands. Selection: choose and prioritize a few goals instead of spreading resources thin. Optimization: focus effort and resources on reaching them. Compensation: find strategies to offset declines and keep performing.

Support at work is robust. Using SOC strategies goes with better work ability, job satisfaction, perceived career success and well-being, goal attainment, supervisor-rated performance and a focus on future opportunities, and with less job and family stress and work-family conflict. SOC also buffers poor conditions: weak family-friendly policies or supervisor support, low-complexity work, high problem-solving demands.

Socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensen and colleagues, 1999): how much time you feel you have left steers your social goals. With open-ended time (younger people) knowledge goals come first; with limited time (older people) emotional goals come first. It explains why older workers tend to have more positive job attitudes (they focus on positive experiences and avoid negative ones; meta-analyses confirm the more positive attitudes, Ng & Feldman, 2010), the positivity effect of step 2, and why job features such as task and skill variety affect burnout and turnover differently with age.

Kanfer and Ackerman (2004) name four patterns that change work motivation: loss (declining resources such as fluid intelligence), growth (gains such as crystallized intelligence), reorganization (changes in emotions and other non-ability traits) and exchange (shifts in which motives matter, through personality, self-concept, interests and values). Kanfer and colleagues (2013) separate motivation to work, at work and to retire, each shaped by the person, finances and health, work conditions such as age climate, and social norms such as the usual retirement age.

Some adults keep their abilities into very old age; others decline earlier. The successful aging literature studies what helps people maintain abilities, from health and longevity to adaptation, control and mastery. Its key contribution at work: bringing in the life-span view (SOC, SST), which says development depends on age and non-age factors and that positive adaptation is possible. Measures of successful workplace aging exist, but what leads to it still needs research.

These theories are the backbone of the interventions in step 5: teach SOC strategies, redesign work to fit shifting motives, and match HR practices to life stage. Next: the social context that older workers face.

⚠ The trap✗ Selection in SOC theory means that organizations select which older workers to keep.✓ In SOC theory the person selects: choosing and prioritizing a few goals to focus resources on. Optimization and compensation are also strategies of the individual.
📝 Exam-style questionUse socioemotional selectivity theory to explain why older workers tend to report more positive job attitudes than younger workers.

Model answer: SST says that perceived time left steers goal selection. Older people perceive their time as more limited, so they prioritize emotion-related goals over knowledge-related goals. At work this means older workers focus on positive, emotionally meaningful experiences and tend to ignore or avoid negative aspects of the job and work environment. That focus is expected to show up as more positive job attitudes, which is in line with meta-analytic findings (Ng & Feldman, 2010).

STEP4
Stereotypes, discrimination, climate, teams, leaders, work-life

How others see and treat older workers

❓ This step answers: Which age stereotypes hold, how age shapes decisions, teams and leadership, and what we know about work-life support across ages?
🗣️ Fact or myth? Six findings; each answer fills a tile.

A stereotype is a generalized belief about a group. Explicit stereotypes are ones people know they hold; implicit ones work without awareness. Discrimination is treating people worse because of group membership. A climate is what people in an organization share as their view of its policies and practices. In the picture: six tiles, one per area.

Think of a new team (our example) where everyone first notices each other's age, then learns who is funny, careful or creative, and age stops mattering. Older workers are shaped not only by their own changes but by how others see and treat them: stereotypes, discrimination, climate, team composition, leadership and work-life policies.

Older people are generally seen as warm but not very competent (Cuddy & Fiske, 2002). Stereotypes of older workers: lower performance and learning ability, resistance to change, shorter tenure, higher cost (Posthuma & Campion, 2009). Meta-analyses: apart from less interest in training, most are not supported; older workers have more positive job attitudes and more citizenship behavior (helping beyond the job description). Some stereotypes are positive (more reliable, more conscientious, less neurotic), and replications show older and younger workers rated similarly for hiring, training and promotion.

Stereotypes that do not hold can still steer decisions. Higher age goes with worse decisions on advancement, selection and evaluation (Bal and colleagues, 2011), although older workers perform about as well. One possible reason: implicit stereotypes. Judgments of older workers were more negative when raters lacked job-relevant information and when older and younger people were rated together (Finkelstein and colleagues, 1995); that meta-analysis also found older candidates rated less suitable for "young" jobs (based on only two studies), with no bias for "older" jobs. Bias also showed more when raters were cognitively busy (Perry and colleagues, 1996). In plain words: with little information and little time, the age label does the judging.

The climate can work for or against older workers. First the problem: in 128 companies, more age diversity went with a stronger age discrimination climate, which in turn went with lower company performance and commitment (Kunze and colleagues, 2011). Then the lever: in 93 German firms, inclusive HR practices went with a more positive age diversity climate, which was related to company performance and to collective turnover intentions (Böhm and colleagues, 2014). Also new: meta-stereotypes, what one age group thinks others think of it; only one workplace study exists.

Mixed-age teams carry risks and chances. Being a different age from the rest of your group (relational demography) goes with less integration and more turnover, and subgroups can form (faultlines, weaker for age than for sex or ethnicity). But surface differences such as age seem to fade as people learn each other's deep traits (personality, ability), and intergenerational contact may reduce ageism. Age diversity hurt performance on routine tasks and helped on complex decision tasks (Wegge and colleagues, 2008).

Leader age matters, depending on behavior and on followers. Older leaders seen as low in generativity were rated less effective; those high in it were as effective as younger leaders (Zacher and colleagues, 2011). Among German professors and their research assistants, leader age mattered only for assistants with negative age stereotypes, who rated older leaders as less proactive and more passive-avoidant (Zacher & Bal, 2012). In plain words: help older leaders show generativity, and work on followers' stereotypes.

Many work-life policies exist (unpaid leave, flexible times, part-time work, telecommuting, compressed weeks, job sharing, child care and eldercare support, employee assistance), but few studies go beyond correlations and none the authors knew of focused on older workers. The one study comparing caregiving situations found supports related most strongly to satisfaction for employees with eldercare duties in an unsupportive work-family culture (Sahibzada and colleagues, 2005); they were on average older (46). The authors' explanation: older employees have more experience and access to formal supports, and may have chosen to stay in supportive firms.

The evidence points to levers: give raters real information, build an inclusive age climate, create positive contact between ages, develop leaders' generativity, and tailor work-life support. Next: what has been tried, and what the authors propose.

⚠ The trap✗ Age stereotypes do not matter, because meta-analyses show most of them are false.✓ The stereotypes are mostly not supported, but decisions about older people are still more negative (Bal and colleagues, 2011), possibly through implicit stereotypes, and especially for jobs stereotyped as young.

SOC training: teach older workers to select skills and tasks that fit their abilities and interests, prepare supervisors and teams, and decide which tasks can be handed off; SOC strategies can lead to better health in bridge employment (paid work after a career job, before full retirement) and better work ability. Work redesign: older workers react better to more autonomy and skill variety (using many different skills) and less task variety (switching between many different tasks); support them in crafting their jobs, and use mentoring for their generativity motives, which also helps younger workers and keeps organizational memory.

Studies so far concern perceived, not actual, HR practices.

Work-life support: flexible arrangements, part-time work, telecommuting and eldercare support may help older workers, through positive social exchange (support from the employer is repaid with effort and loyalty). Training design: extra time, smaller groups, self-paced learning, a learning goal orientation (aiming to learn rather than to look good), error management training (being encouraged to make mistakes); frame training as an intrinsic benefit and link it to prior knowledge. The prevention lens · Table 3

Prevent, control, accommodate

❓ This step answers: How can interventions for an aging workforce be organized, and what must research do next?
🗂️ Classify three interventions, then three questions about research.

Longitudinal research follows the same people over time. Multilevel designs look at individuals, groups and organizations together. A generational difference is a difference between people born in different periods, as opposed to a change caused by getting older. In the picture: three tiers, and three research needs below.

Think of dental care (our analogy): brushing for everyone, a filling when a problem starts, a crown when the tooth is damaged. The authors borrow this prevention model from health promotion to organize interventions for an aging workforce by target group and aim: prevention versus accommodation. A second message: support workers of all ages, because today's young workers are tomorrow's older workers, and a positive age diversity climate benefits everyone.

Primary: for all workers, preventing problems so people reach the end of their career with the resources to be healthy and effective (an age-supportive climate, successful aging techniques, less exposure to hazardous chemicals, health promotion). Secondary: for people in mid to late career, reducing the impact of age-related declines (short-term memory interventions, ergonomics, flexible schedules) and using gains such as generativity (mentoring). Tertiary: when a condition cannot be reversed, accommodating workers with reduced work ability to retain them (hearing aids, glasses).

Most research is correlational. To find causes, use experimental and quasi-experimental (comparing groups without random assignment) designs with intervention and control groups, at individual, group or organizational level; longitudinal studies with very long time horizons, to see change within people and to separate aging from generational differences; measures rare in work psychology but crucial for age, such as objective health; and collaboration with sociology, economics, demography, medicine and ergonomics.

Also in Table 3: wise interventions, brief, precise and cheap so even small employers can use them (Walton, 2014), for example supervisor tips on a smartphone. Global research, because country and culture matter: personality matures earlier where people start working earlier, a country's retirement age affects work motivation, and subjective age related to stress differently across nations (Barnes-Farrell and colleagues, 2002). Age differences in adopting versus maintaining change: younger people may start change more easily, older people keep it up better (Rothman, 2006). Holistic interventions in and outside work. A better grasp of what chronological age stands for (health, cognition, motives, subjective age). And technology as a friend: adapt technology to older workers, not only workers to technology.

STEP3
The theory behind the predictions

Conservation of resources: who has, invests

❓ This step answers: Why would green HR practices lead to voluntary green behavior, and what are the seven hypotheses?
🎒 Reason like the author: six cases. Each answer lights up the hypotheses it supports.

Resources, in this theory, are personal attributes, energies or conditions that a person values: knowledge, skills, support, self-esteem. Conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll) says people try to keep, protect and build resources; you met it in theme 1. Proactive means acting on your own initiative. In the picture: two situations at the top (ample or limited resources) and three rows of hypotheses that light up as you solve the cases.

The theory has two sides. A lack or loss of resources may have negative psychological effects, so people try to avoid loss; with limited resources they turn defensive and perform at the minimum. With ample resources they take a proactive resource gain strategy: they go above and beyond minimum expectations with proactive, voluntary or extra-role behavior. Bordia and colleagues (2017) see such behavior as a trade: people put resources in and get growing resources back (resource gain spirals), structural (knowledge, skills), social (relationships) and personal (self-esteem, pride). And resources can change form: the resource caravan.

Hypotheses 1 and 2. The prediction in plain words: the more green HR practices, the more voluntary green behavior, of the team and of the individual. Why: Green HR practices are organizational-level resources. Training gives green values, knowledge and skills; communication channels keep employees informed; involvement gives opportunities for problem-solving on environmental issues. The practices also create a green learning atmosphere and a norm for behaving pro-environmentally and helping each other do so. With these resources, team members are expected to engage in OCBE individually and collectively. Earlier evidence pointed the same way for green behavior, extra-role green behavior and green creativity.

Hypotheses 3 and 4. The prediction in plain words: green HR practices lead teams to craft their green work together, and that this crafting is the route (mediator) through which they raise voluntary green behavior (team and individual). Why: Employees are active players. When they perceive ample organizational resources for green activities, they are motivated to build more, and one way is collective green crafting. Crafting then feeds OCBE: for the team through a stronger norm, person-group fit and shared values; for the individual through shared knowledge, peer coaching and feedback, observational learning from more role models, and open communication that helps turn knowledge into initiatives.

Hypotheses 5 to 7. The prediction in plain words: the link between green HR practices and team crafting, and between green HR practices and voluntary green behavior (team and individual), is stronger when followers see their leader as high on environmentally specific servant leadership, and weaker when low: the leader amplifies what HR starts. Why: Leadership has been found to moderate the effects of HRM practices. Environmentally specific servant leaders are role models who reinforce the green norm that HR practices cultivate; followers spread these values through the team in a contagion process; managers act as gatekeepers for the norm. Because managers often give less support to green behavior than to other management activities, leadership and HR practices are not equivalent sources of resources: the leader is unlikely to take the place of the HR practices, but adds to them.

⚠ The trap✗ The study explains green citizenship behavior with social exchange theory: employees pay the organization back for good treatment.✓ Paying back is the social exchange explanation. The author's main explanation is conservation of resources: people with ample resources invest them to gain more. He offers it as an alternative that does not challenge social exchange theory, and notes that investing in OCBE is also in line with an exchange of resources with the organization.
📝 Exam-style questionA company cuts all green training and stops informing staff about its environmental plans. Predict with conservation of resources theory what happens to voluntary green behavior, and name the strategy.

Model answer: Voluntary green behavior is expected to drop. Green training and information are organizational-level resources. Without them, employees possess limited green-related resources and are prone to a defensive resource strategy: they protect or conserve their remaining resources and perform at the minimum level, so they do not invest in behavior above and beyond minimum expectations such as OCBE or green crafting. With ample resources they would take a proactive resource gain strategy.

Who wrote it, and what they want to solve

Why is sustainability in supply chains so hard, and who can move it?

❓ This step answers: What problem does the article start from, what is its idea, and what does it add to earlier research?
🔎 Think along with the authors: five short decisions. Each one fills in a part of the picture.
Authors
Keri Pekaar (Tilburg University and Eindhoven University of Technology), Evangelia Demerouti and Piet van Gool (Eindhoven University of Technology), the Netherlands
Type
Conceptual article in Organizational Psychology Review (2024): it builds a framework with ten propositions. It collects no data
Question
How can individual employees who work at the border between organizations get sustainability implemented in a supply chain?
Main message
Motivated employees at the border can act as sustainability champions: by proactively regulating their own and their partner's sustainability demands and resources, they may steer sustainability from the bottom up (proposed, not yet tested)

Some words first. A supply chain is the row of organizations a product passes through: from raw materials to production, storage and delivery. A supplier sells to a buying firm; the large firm that leads a chain is called the focal firm. Inter-organizational means between organizations. A conceptual article develops ideas and predictions from existing research; a proposition is such a prediction, offered for others to test. The best-known definition of sustainable development comes from the Brundtland Report (1987): development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own. Elkington (1997) turned it into the triple bottom line: economic prosperity, environmental quality and social equity, or people, planet and profit. Most supply chain research covered only one of the three. In the picture: a supplier and a buying firm, and between them a place that is still empty.

Think of a group project (our analogy) where your grade depends on a teammate who has no time and no laptop. Sending reminders will not help; lending your laptop might. Supply chains work the same way. Supply chain sustainability means taking people, planet and profit (the triple P, after Elkington, 1997) into account during a product's whole journey: raw materials, production, storage, delivery and transport. A firm's sustainability therefore depends on partners that may lack the expertise, know-how, means or motivation to change.

The authors give three reasons in their plain language summary: several players have to change their behaviour, it is generally costly, and people lack the resources to make a difference. And most management of it is demand-focused: regulations, policies, managerial decisions. On its own that is often ineffective, because the employees who must act lack the resources. In plain words: rules from the top, on their own, often do not change what happens at the supplier's desk.

Change between organizations does not happen through top-down pressure alone; it needs change agents where sourcing decisions are made: the border between organizations. People who work there are boundary spanners; the motivated ones are sustainability champions. Central claim: a champion should improve the partner's balance of sustainability demands and resources, and so indirectly the own balance, because both sides are linked. The authors do not throw out top-down models; they add a bottom-up route. The framework is triadic: three parts in every sustainability decision, the initiating actor, the partner, and the exchange of demands and resources between their organizations.

Three contributions. To supply chain research: a behavioural, bottom-up solution, a possible missing link in a field with little work on individuals. To Job Demands-Resources theory: one employee's crafting can activate an employee in another organization, and this can build into gain or loss spirals. To change research: concrete roles for individuals (the initiator, the resource-providing partner). Table 1 (open it below) shows the micro-level topics studied so far; what employees at the border actually do is not among them. In the T-shirt case (ours): for supply chain research, a buyer's daily choices become the lever; for the theory, the buyer's crafting changes the job of someone at the supplier; for change research, the buyer is the initiator and the supplier's manager the partner.

The article is conceptual: it collects no data, it builds a framework with ten propositions for others to test. Keep that in mind for every step: the propositions are proposed, not shown. Next: the building blocks of the framework, sustainability demands and resources.

STEP2
The building blocks · propositions 2 and 3

Sustainability demands and resources, on both sides

❓ This step answers: What are sustainability demands and resources, and why are those of actor and partner tied together?
⚖️ Sort six situations: a demand or a resource? In the last three, watch for whom.

Job Demands-Resources theory (Demerouti and colleagues, 2001) is the theory from theme 1. Job demands are aspects of work that require sustained physical or psychological effort and therefore have costs. Job resources are aspects of work that help to achieve work goals, reduce demands and their costs, or stimulate learning, growth and development. Demands drain energy; resources can generate motivation; people thrive when the two are in balance. In the picture: two baskets, one for demands and one for resources.

Think of a seesaw (our analogy): whatever the person on one end does moves the other. The article's own story: a buyer asks her supplier for more sustainable packaging. At the supplier's desk this is a new demand (develop or buy that packaging), which calls for new resources (know-how, time, money). If the buyer helps, for example with knowledge, her own desk gets a new demand. Two desks, one seesaw: that is the picture behind propositions 2 and 3.

Sustainability demands in the article: sourcing new products, extra information, new ways of storage and transport, cultural differences, hazardous working conditions in manufacturing, compliance checks; also time and money pressure. Sustainability resources: capital, sustainability training, autonomy, know-how, a network, managerial support. They come on top of regular duties such as deadlines and quality. Proposition 2: implementing sustainability adds sustainability demands and requires specific sustainability resources, for actors and partners.

Actor and partner are bound by the exchange between their firms. The article's example: a buyer asks for more sustainable packaging, so the supplier's manager gets a new demand and must find new resources. Expertise is spread over firms, so resources have to be shared. And when demands and resources cross the border they may change in nature: a higher price is a resource for the supplier and (our illustration) a demand on the buyer's budget. Proposition 3: the partners' demands and resources are (inversely) related, so a change on one side prompts an update on the other.

The theory was mostly applied to one person at a time. Studies of colleagues showed the link already: when one employee's hindering demands, such as administrative tasks, decreased, a close colleague who took them over got more work (Tims and colleagues, 2015; Tims & Parker, 2020). The authors move this to two people in different organizations who negotiate the costs. Next: what such a demand does inside the partner.

⚠ The trap✗ A resource is a resource: when a buyer provides resources, everybody's balance improves.✓ Across a boundary, demands and resources may change in nature. What the partner receives as a resource (specialized knowledge, a higher price) may be an additional demand for the actor who has to provide it. This is why proposition 3 says "(inversely) related", and why proposition 7 (step 4) says that helping the partner is costly at first.
📝 Exam-style questionA purchaser asks her supplier for a fully traceable product. Use propositions 2 and 3 to describe what this does to the work of the supplier's account manager, and what it may do to the purchaser's own work.

Model answer: For the account manager the request is an additional sustainability demand on top of economic and operational demands (proposition 2): extra information has to be collected and delivered, and specific resources are needed, such as know-how, time or capital. Because the demands and resources of the two are interrelated (proposition 3), the supplier's side has to update its resource pool, and may need resources from the purchaser's side, such as knowledge or a higher price. Providing those is a resource for the account manager and may be a new demand for the purchaser. (The traceable product is our example.)

STEP3
The right-hand side of the framework · propositions 4 and 5

The same demand: motivation or strain?

❓ This step answers: What happens in the partner when an initiator brings sustainability demands, and what decides whether the result is promoting or undermining behavior?
🤝 Predict first: six cases. Two suppliers get the same demand; watch which route lights up.

Motivation is the wish and energy to do something. Strain is the wear that follows from demands that keep draining a person. An audit is a formal check, here of whether a supplier meets sustainability standards. A code of conduct is a set of rules a buying firm asks its suppliers to follow. A first-tier supplier delivers directly to the focal firm; a second-tier supplier delivers to that supplier. In the picture: on the left what arrives at the partner, in the middle what the partner feels, on the right what the partner does.

Think of a teacher who raises the bar (our analogy). With tutoring and good books, students rise to it; with only extra tests and penalties, some start to cheat. Job Demands-Resources theory names two routes. In the motivational process, resources build motivation and better performance. In the health-impairment process, chronic demands drain people, causing strain and worse performance. What decides is the balance: resources soften the drain of demands (the buffer hypothesis). Proposition 4 is the motivational process and proposition 5 the impairment process, both running inside the partner.

A demand from the initiator costs the partner resources. With high access to sustainability resources the drain is buffered: the partner becomes more motivated and shows more sustainability-promoting behaviour. That is proposition 4. Two kinds of support. Among colleagues, an employee who raised challenging demands together with resources increased co-workers' work enjoyment and willingness to collaborate (Fong and colleagues, 2022). Among firms, suppliers reduced violations when buyers gave training or price premiums along with codes of conduct and audits (Porteous and colleagues, 2015; Yawar & Seuring, 2018).

With low access to resources, the same demand may lead to strain and to sustainability-undermining behaviour: behaviour that creates obstacles, such as hiding practices or audit fraud. That is proposition 5. Many buyers manage exactly this way: codes of conduct, audits, fines or ending the contract. Relying on sticks without carrots often fails in the long term and can push suppliers with few resources to hide information from auditors (Plambeck & Taylor, 2016).

The trigger sits in another organization: the initiator's demand starts the process, the partner's resources decide the route. So the lesson is not "ask less" but "give as well". The authors hedge with "may": these routes are expected, not yet tested between individual employees. Next: what the champion can do about it.

⚠ The trap✗ Sustainability demands on suppliers are bad: they cause strain and cheating.✓ The demand is not the problem; the balance is. With high access to resources the same demand is a challenge that raises motivation and promoting behavior (proposition 4). Only with low access to resources is it expected to lead to strain and undermining behavior (proposition 5). The framework does not advise firms to stop asking; it advises them to give as well.
📝 Exam-style questionA newspaper reveals that a factory kept two sets of records: one for the auditors of its Western buyers and one real. Explain with the framework how this may have come about, and what the buyers could have done differently.

Model answer: The framework would read this as sustainability-undermining behavior, the end of the impairment route (proposition 5): the people at the factory were challenged with sustainability demands (codes of conduct, audits, the threat of fines or losing the contract) while they had low access to sustainability resources, which may lead to strain and to hiding practices or audit fraud. Buyers could have provided resources along with the demands, such as training, knowledge or price premiums, so that the resources buffer the demands and the motivational route (proposition 4) becomes more likely. (The newspaper case is our example; the article mentions hiding information from auditors and audit fraud.)

STEP4
The left-hand side of the framework · propositions 1, 6, 7 and 8

Regulating my own balance, and my partner's

❓ This step answers: Which proactive strategies can a sustainability champion use, why do they cost something, and what has to be in place first?
🏅 Follow Noor, a buyer (our example): match each thing she does to a tile, then answer two questions about what it takes.

Proactive means acting on your own initiative, before you are told to. Job crafting, from theme 1, is the proactive changing of your own job demands and job resources. Following recent extensions of the theory, the authors call such crafting behavior regulation: it covers crafting aimed at yourself and at someone else, and stresses that the same dynamics play out at different levels (note 2, p. 611). In the picture: four tiles, two for what a champion does for herself and two for what she does toward the partner.

Think of the oxygen-mask rule on a plane (our analogy): put on your own mask first, then help your neighbour. A sustainability champion does both. It starts with a spark: an individual's own wish to work sustainably, shaped by attitudes, social norms and perceived control, and especially influential when the organization's objectives are unclear (Swaim and colleagues, 2016). But a spark needs tools: autonomy, support, room to grow. Proposition 1: implementation requires a motivated, proactive initiator who can mobilize enough sustainability resources in the own work environment.

Partner-focused regulation means intentionally changing the demands and resources of a person in another organization: imposing sustainability demands and providing sustainability resources. Firms call this supplier development: direct (investments, training) or indirect (certification, auditing). Proposition 6: actors can use it to improve the partner's balance and trigger the partner's motivation and promoting behaviour. To do it well they need meta-knowledge: knowing what the partner can handle and lacks.

What you give, you no longer have. Resources provided to the partner come out of the actor's own pool; crafting also takes energy and can bring role conflict, disappointment and regret in the short term, and the payoff of crafting someone else's job is even less certain. Proposition 7: partner-focused regulation may draw on the actor's own resources, risking an initial disbalance. In plain words: helping the supplier can hurt your own targets at first.

Buyers often lack autonomy, managerial support, training, capital and room to decide, and their sustainability effort is voluntary, so the organization does not replace what they spend. Self-focused regulation repairs this: optimizing own demands and seeking own resources. Why first? Conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll, 1989), which you met in theme 1: people with few resources protect what they have; only with a starting pool do they invest. Proposition 8: self-focused regulation optimizes the actor's own balance so that partner-focused regulation becomes possible. An earlier study found these behaviours went with more daily proactive sustainability behaviour (Pekaar & Demerouti, 2023). So what: four strategies, two for me and two for my partner. Next: what happens over time.

⚠ The trap✗ Partner-focused regulation is just job crafting with a side effect on others.✓ Earlier research showed that self-directed crafting can affect colleagues as a side effect. Partner-focused regulation is different in two ways: the change in the other person's demands and resources is intended, and the other person works in a different organization. The authors present this as an extension of Job Demands-Resources theory to pairs of people across organizations.
📝 Exam-style questionAn enthusiastic buyer spends months coaching a supplier on cleaner production. Her own targets suffer and her manager is displeased. Which propositions describe her situation, and what would the framework have advised her to do first?

Model answer: She used partner-focused regulation by providing resources (proposition 6). Those resources came from her own pool, which may create an initial disbalance in her own demands and resources (proposition 7): time and energy went to the supplier while her regular demands stayed. The framework advises self-focused regulation first or alongside (proposition 8): seek resources in her own organization, such as managerial support, autonomy, time or budget, and optimize her own demands, so that her own balance can carry the investment. Proposition 1 makes the same point: an initiator needs sufficient resources in the own work environment.

STEP5
The framework over time · propositions 9 and 10

A journey, not a destination

❓ This step answers: What does the framework expect over many exchanges, how does it spread through a longer chain, and how could it be tested?
🌀 Play it forward: six rounds. Watch which spiral grows.

A spiral is a loop that strengthens itself: each round makes the next one more likely. Lagged means with a delay: the effect comes later. A dyad is a pair. Longitudinal research measures the same people several times. In the picture: two empty frames, one for what grows when exchange is balanced and one for what grows when it is not.

Think of a tennis rally (our analogy): if both keep returning playable balls the rally grows; if one only smashes, the other starts missing and the game falls apart. Job Demands-Resources theory already has loops inside one person: demands and resources, proactive behaviour and outcomes strengthen each other over time, for better (gain) or worse (loss) (Bakker & Demerouti, 2018). The article moves these loops between two people in different organizations. Sustainability, as the authors put it citing Bouchery and colleagues (2024), is a journey, not a destination.

Two kinds of demands matter here. Challenging demands stretch people and can motivate; hindering demands only obstruct. In a gain spiral the actor raises challenging demands and removes hindering ones through support. By the boosting hypothesis, a challenging demand makes the partner's resources more valuable and prompts a motivated search for more. The partner then shares what it finds, so resources come back to the initiator. Proposition 9: a balanced resource exchange is related to a gain spiral in which the initiator's sharing of resources and the partner's promoting behaviour strengthen each other over time. The authors cite reading 5 (Luu, 2019) as an example inside organizations, with support from colleagues and managers as the active mechanism, and add that in today's resource-poor supply chains gain spirals are not likely.

People with few resources, by conservation of resources theory, defend what they have; under strain they use poor strategies (poor communication, careless mistakes, conflict) that add to their demands. Among colleagues, crafting driven by avoiding losses reduced the partner's cooperation and raised conflict (Fong and colleagues, 2022). Between firms: demands without resources push the partner toward undermining behaviour and the initiator toward sharing less. Suppliers audited by many buyers can develop audit fatigue, which distracts from real improvement and may end in audit fraud. Proposition 10: an unbalanced exchange (high demands, low resources) is related to a loss spiral of low sharing and undermining behaviour.

The framework describes a dyad, but it is reversible: a motivated partner can become the initiator toward its own supplier. So people in the chain have a dual role, meeting demands from above and passing demands and resources on. The result can ripple up and down the chain. Fitting finding: first-tier suppliers' resources decide whether they can drive sustainability to second-tier suppliers (Wilhelm and colleagues, 2016).

The propositions are a research agenda. Collect data from both sides of the pair. Choose the time frame: a diary or event-sampling study (short questions every day or after each event) for quick reactions, a longitudinal panel (the same people measured a few times, months apart) for slow ones; the spirals need several measurements. Analyse with Actor-Partner Interdependence Models, which separate what my behaviour does for me from what it does for my partner, or let a computer simulate the loops (agent-based modelling, system dynamics). So what: next, the whole framework in one view.

📝 Exam-style questionYou want to test proposition 9. Describe a suitable design: who you measure, how often, and how you analyze it.

Model answer: Measure both members of the pair: employees at the border in two connected organizations, for example buyers and their contact persons at suppliers (multi-source data). Measure several times, because a spiral is a process over time and partners need time to respond: a longitudinal panel, or diary or event-sampling waves. Measure the initiator's sharing of sustainability resources and the partner's sustainability-promoting behavior at each wave, and ideally the organizational exchange too. Analyze with an Actor-Partner Interdependence Model to separate actor and partner effects and to test influence in both directions; a gain spiral predicts that each one's earlier behavior is related to the other's later behavior, with levels rising over time.

🧰 What to do with it, according to the authors

Employees at the border (actors and partners): recognize your own sustainability demands and resources and craft your job to create room for sustainability; invest in understanding your partner's demands and resources, through more effective and transparent communication, so that partner-focused regulation fits and the response is more collaborative. Leaders: model proactive and sustainable behavior and promote it through resource-providing and empowering leadership. Organizations: select team leads of buyers and salespeople on these criteria or train them; give buyers and salespeople extra resources for sustainability initiatives and reduce the trade-off with other performance outcomes, for instance by including sustainability performance in the key performance indicators (the measures on which people are judged); offer job crafting training. Supply chain managers: encourage resource-sharing across all organizational levels.

🌍 The place of context

The bottom-up focus does not replace top-down models. Local work designs are shaped by context at several levels: global and industrial (government regulation, technology), organizational (strategy, initiatives, climate) and team (social dynamics, leadership). According to the authors, context works mainly through social norms, which communicate what sustainability behavior is expected and whether proactivity is valued, and which shape both self-focused and partner-focused regulation. Context can also disturb the balance directly, through crises such as the pandemic, technological disruptions or new regulations.

⚠️ How sure can we be?

This is a conceptual article. Its propositions are derived from Job Demands-Resources theory, from studies of colleagues inside organizations, and from supply chain research at the level of firms. The step to individual employees in two different organizations is the authors' proposal, and they present the propositions as a research agenda. The note under Figure 1 adds two limits: influence between actor and partner rarely is a direct exchange between two individuals, because it is part of a broader exchange between their organizations, which both can influence only to some degree; and the macro-level process of that exchange (negotiations, market forces) is outside the model. The authors do believe the framework reaches beyond sustainability: in a study of 225 manufacturer-distributor pairs, resource-providing behavior between salespeople and individual buyers was more strongly related to beneficial exchange between the organizations than the same behavior between top executives (Huang and colleagues, 2016).

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

How can employees proactively drive green initiatives within their organizations? This article's answer, and it reaches across the organization's border: by being a motivated initiator with enough resources (proposition 1); by self-focused regulation, that is optimizing the own sustainability demands and seeking sustainability resources (proposition 8); and by partner-focused regulation, that is imposing sustainability demands on the partner together with providing sustainability resources (proposition 6), guided by knowledge of what the partner can handle. What obstacles do sustainability champions face? Sustainability demands on top of regular duties and trade-offs with cost; a work environment that does not support it (little autonomy, managerial support, training, capital, latitude); voluntary investments that the organization does not replace; dependence on a partner who may lack means or motivation; the short-term cost of helping the partner (proposition 7); and the risk of a loss spiral when exchange is unbalanced. How can they deal with them? Secure the own balance first, learn the partner's situation, give resources together with demands, and ask the organization for support: leadership that provides resources, sustainability in the performance measures, and job crafting training.

Changing organizational energy consumption behaviour through comparative feedback

Siero, F. W., Bakker, A. B., Dekker, G. B., & van den Burg, M. T. C. (1996). Changing organizational energy consumption behaviour through comparative feedback. Journal of Environmental Psychology, 16, 235–246.

STEP1
Who wrote it, and what they want to know

Can comparing your unit with others save energy?

❓ This step answers: Why study energy behavior at work, why should comparative feedback help, and what was predicted?
🔎 Think like the researchers: six short decisions. Each one fills a box of the reasoning.
Authors
Frans Siero, Arnold Bakker, Gerda Dekker and Marcel van den Burg, University of Groningen, the Netherlands
Type
Empirical article in the Journal of Environmental Psychology (1996): a field study with a quasi-experimental design in a metallurgical company
Question
Does adding comparative feedback (information about how other units perform) to a behavioral change programme reduce energy-wasting behavior more than the programme alone?
Main message
Yes: the unit that also saw other units' results wasted clearly less energy, still half a year later, with hardly any change in attitudes

Some words first. Feedback is information about how well you are doing. Comparative feedback adds how other groups are doing. A field study takes place in a real setting, here a company, not a laboratory. A metallurgical company works with metals, here drilling, rolling and bending steel. In the picture: six empty boxes: two about the problem, two about why the idea should work, one risk and the prediction.

Think of a school recycling league (our example): as soon as your class is compared with class 5B, "we" want to win. The study tries that at work. Saving energy at home pays off on your own bill; in households behavior change saved up to 30 per cent. At work the saving goes to the company, so employees profit only indirectly. And new technology (insulation, efficient machines) is not worth much unless employees use the equipment well. So the question is how a company can motivate its employees to save, and the authors test comparative feedback.

They expected two routes. Identity: seeing another group makes your own group stand out; by social identity theory (Tajfel, 1978) people want their group to look good, because it is part of who they are, and a shared identity can make people cooperate even without personal gain. Competition, explained by social comparison theory (people judge their performance by comparing with others): earlier studies found that just expecting to be compared, or seeing others' scores on a wall chart, improved performance. In plain words: "us" becomes visible, and "us" wants to win.

The direction of the comparison could not be controlled. A unit that does better gets a positive self-image; a unit that does worse may try harder. But a unit that keeps losing despite doing its utmost has no way to feel good about itself, and may become demoralized and perform worse. So the effect was not certain in advance. If a unit kept losing, the authors planned to split it into better and worse groups to separate upward comparison (with a better group) from downward comparison (with a worse group).

The main hypothesis: comparative feedback added to the basic programme leads to an extra reduction of energy-wasting behavior, with more competitive feelings and identification. Thoughts such as attitudes and norms were measured, but apart from reported behavior no changes in them were expected. Next: the basic programme both units received.

⚠ The trap✗ The study compares a unit with comparative feedback to a unit that received nothing.✓ Both units received the full basic programme (information, goal-setting, feedback on their own unit, supervision and control). The comparison unit got comparative feedback on top. So the study tests the extra effect of comparison.
📝 Exam-style questionExplain with two mechanisms why the authors expected comparative feedback to reduce energy-wasting behavior, and name the risk they saw.

Model answer: (1) Social identity: information about another group makes the own group stand out (salient); by social identity theory people strive for a positive self-image, of which group membership is part, so identification with the group grows and people put in effort for the group. (2) Competition (social comparison theory): comparison creates competitive feelings, more attention to feedback and a wish to perform better than the other group. Risk: if a unit performs worse than the comparison group for a long time despite full effort, it cannot reach a positive self-evaluation, which may lead to demoralization and lower performance.

STEP2
What both units received

Tell, target, track, supervise

❓ This step answers: What were the four elements of the behavioral change programme, and how was the goal set?
🧩 Build the programme: place four cards on their tiles, then calculate one goal yourself.

Goal-setting means agreeing on a concrete target. Supervision and control means managers checking that the behavior happens. A habit is behavior that runs automatically in a familiar situation. In the picture: four tiles for the four elements, and a line for the goal you will calculate.

Think of a fitness plan (our example): you learn why exercise helps, you get a weekly target, a step counter shows your progress, and a coach checks in at first. The basic programme had the same four parts, based on an earlier study in which this kind of programme improved the energy-saving driving of a transport company's drivers (Siero and colleagues, 1989). The authors call it a programme because the parts only work together: a goal does little without feedback, and some supervision is always needed.

Information tells employees that the company wants different behavior and why: 12 weeks of energy bulletins and magazine items on the social and economic sides of saving, and on lights, heating, compressed air and machines. But information is necessary and seldom sufficient, because work behavior is often a habit. Goal-setting: one common goal for the unit, because at work you appeal to the collective interest. Higher and more precise goals lead to better performance (Locke & Latham, 1990), if people accept them and find them feasible. Employees of another unit judged 75 per cent of the possible saving feasible, so the goal was: a quarter of the pretest waste may remain.

Feedback: every week a chart showed the unit's savings as a percentage of what was possible (100% = no waste, 75% = goal reached, 0% = as wasteful as before), with the goal line, so employees could make temporal comparisons: better than before? close to the goal? Exact numbers were added for workstation lights and compressed air leaks only. Supervision and control: first-line managers agreed to encourage and check energy behavior in their departments, until the behavior holds without control.

The basic programme is the baseline of the experiment. Both units got it in the same way, and it worked on its own (step 4). The only planned difference was the extra comparison. Next: how the study was run and measured.

⚠ The trap✗ The goal was to save 75 per cent of the energy the units used.✓ The goal was about energy-wasting behavior, not total energy: 75 per cent of the possible savings, so the observed waste had to fall to a quarter of its pretest level (for example 68.9% → 17.2%).
📝 Exam-style questionAt the pretest, 34.1 per cent of the observations of "remaining" energy behaviors in a unit were wasteful. What was the unit's goal, and why was a goal combined with weekly feedback?

Model answer: The goal was that 75 per cent of the possible saving is realized, so a quarter of the pretest waste may remain: 0.25 × 34.1 ≈ 8.5 per cent (the article reports 8.5). Information alone is seldom enough for routine work behavior; a combination of goal-setting and regular feedback about reaching the goal is needed. Feedback lets employees compare their results with earlier results and with the goal; a goal without feedback is not very effective.

STEP3
How they tested it

Two units, observed at random moments

❓ This step answers: What was the design, how was energy-wasting behavior measured, and what else was measured?
🧪 Run the study: six design decisions, including one calculation as the observer.

The independent variable is what the researchers change: here, comparative feedback added or not. The dependent variable is what they measure as the result: here, energy-wasting behavior. A pretest is measured before the intervention, a post-test after it. Random assignment means chance decides who gets which condition. In the picture: two factory units, an observer, and a timeline.

Think of a referee (our example) who walks past at random moments and notes: nobody at the machine, machine running. Enough random moments give an accurate percentage of waste. That is how this study measured behavior, because the company had only a rough meter for energy use. The comparison was between two existing units, so it is a quasi-experimental design.

Two units of a Dutch metallurgical company, 200 miles apart, were highly comparable in production processes, communication, structure, staff and strategy. Both got the basic programme; the second also got comparative feedback: each week its chart also showed the savings of the basic unit and of a third unit not included in this report. Employees were not told that the units were treated differently. A weak point: the basic unit had 135 employees, the comparison unit 50.

Multi Moment Recording: at random moments on random days an observer noted, for each selected object (machines, workstation lights, other equipment), whether an employee was present and whether it used energy. Waste = "absent and on" divided by all moments the employee was absent. The measure was about objects, not persons. Two independent observers agreed on 96.3 per cent of 1016 observations. Compressed air leaks were left out for technical reasons.

Pretest 10 weeks (September to November), campaign 20 weeks (November to March, analysed as four periods of 5 weeks), post-test 1 directly after (4 weeks, April), post-test 2 half a year later (5 weeks, September). Questionnaires before and after the campaign measured behavioral beliefs, attitudes, social norms, identification with the own unit and reported behavior, plus implementation checks.

Strengths: observed behavior instead of self-report, high observer agreement, and a follow-up half a year later. Weaknesses: no random assignment, one company, two units of different size. Next: what the observations showed.

⚠ The trap✗ The dependent variable was the company's energy consumption in kilowatt hours.✓ It was observed energy-wasting behavior (percentage of moments that equipment was on while nobody was there), because the company only had a rough measure of energy consumption.
📝 Exam-style questionWhy is this study called quasi-experimental, and which alternative explanation does that leave open?

Model answer: The conditions were given to two existing units of the company; employees were not randomly assigned to conditions. The researchers therefore had to show that the units were comparable (processes, structure, staff, strategy, and no pretest differences in attitudes, norms or reported behavior). An alternative explanation remains open because the units differed in size (135 versus 50 employees): a smaller unit might communicate more, be easier to supervise or feel more committed. The authors discuss and argue against this explanation.

STEP5
Table 1 · checks · alternative explanations

Behavior moved. Did minds move too?

❓ This step answers: What changed in people's thoughts, was the comparison really received, could something else explain the result, and why did comparative feedback work?
🧐 Interpret like the authors: six questions about Table 1, the checks and a critic.

An attitude is how positive or negative you feel about a behavior. A social norm is what you think important others expect, here multiplied by how much you care about their opinion. A manipulation is the condition the researchers create; an implementation check tests whether it really reached people. In the picture: arrows for before and after on four measures, and three verdicts below.

Think of taking the stairs because your office started a stairs league (our example), not because you changed your opinion about lifts. Many theories say attitudes must change first, then intentions, then behavior (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1991). In this study behavior changed a lot and attitudes hardly at all. The authors conclude that attitude change is not a prerequisite when the tools aim at behavior itself, such as feedback and comparison. They note that attitudes were already quite positive at the start. The questionnaires in Table 1 are how this was checked: the same questions before and after the campaign, in both units.

Table 1, four findings. Attitudes and social norms did not change differently in the two units. Beliefs about savings: after the campaign the comparison unit believed more strongly than the basic unit that switching off machines and lights saves energy (machines 4.6 v 4.0, lights 4.6 v 4.1; for lights only borderline). Reported behavior rose in the comparison unit (3.1 → 3.7), not in the basic unit. Identification changed differently, but the significant part was a drop in the basic unit (4.2 → 3.6); the comparison unit went 3.8 → 4.0. In plain words: heads changed a little, behavior a lot.

The checks show the comparison was received: the comparison unit was more curious about (3.9 v 3.3), more aware of (3.6 v 2.3) and more competitive about (4.4 v 3.8) other units' results. The critic's alternative, unit size, is argued away: communication and commitment did not differ, the comparison unit felt less supervision (2.1 v 3.0), starting attitudes were equal, and its lower starting waste made saving harder. One more difference, significant: the comparison unit knew the goal better and found it more attainable (4.3 v 3.4 and 4.0 v 3.2). The authors think the comparison itself caused this, through extra attention to the goal, and read it as a sign of felt competence; but it is a second way the units differed, and could also be part of why the comparison unit did better.

If people only wanted to win, the effect should have stopped with the feedback. It lasted half a year. The authors' explanation: comparison also strengthened identification with the own group, so people kept up the effort for their group and the organization, were intrinsically motivated to save, and by repeating the behavior formed new habits. The transfer to behaviors without exact feedback fits this. These are the authors' interpretations; the questionnaire data support them only partly.

Comparative feedback is a powerful tool, but two limits matter. The study could not test demoralization, because the comparison unit never had a long losing streak, so it does not show that comparison cannot demoralize. And the tool needs behavior that is specified and measured so that employees see the feedback as reliable and contingent on (clearly caused by) their own behavior. Next: the article in one view.

🧰 What organizations can do with it

Combine information, a common goal, regular feedback and supervision: this programme saves energy by itself. Add feedback that compares units for a larger and longer-lasting effect. ⚠️ How sure can we be?

A strong field study for its time: observed behavior, high observer agreement, a follow-up half a year later. But it compared only two units of one company, not randomly assigned and of different size; the authors argue the size difference away but cannot rule it out by design. The article is also inconsistent about what it expected: the introduction predicts no change in attitudes, while the discussion says attitude change was expected to come first. Outcome evaluations were dropped from the post-test because pretest answers were already extreme with little spread, which fits the point that attitudes were positive from the start. The study says nothing about units that keep losing.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

Why does comparative feedback change organizational energy consumption? It makes the own group salient and creates a competitive orientation: people pay more attention to the feedback and want to do better than the other group. The lasting effect suggests that it also strengthened identification with the own group and intrinsic motivation, so the behavior was repeated until it became a habit. It changes behavior directly, without first changing attitudes. How can organizations increase pro-environmental behaviors? STEP1
Who wrote it, and what they want to solve

Not for the planet, for your own goals

❓ This step answers: Why do the usual ways of promoting green behavior often fail, and what third approach do the authors propose?
🌍 Sort five cases: which approach is this, and where does it fail?
Authors
K. L. Unsworth and I. M. McNeill; page numbers on this page refer to the accepted manuscript you have (38 pages of text, tables and figure at the end)
Type
Empirical article in the Journal of Applied Psychology (2017): one time-lagged correlational study and two experiments
Question
Can people be motivated to act more sustainably by linking green behaviors to their own personal goals, even goals that have nothing to do with the environment?
Main message
Yes: helping people connect a green behavior to goals that matter to them raised its self-concordance and their intentions to do it, beyond their environmental values (small effects, mostly intentions)

Some words first. Pro-environmental or environmentally sustainable behavior is behavior that helps the environment, such as saving energy or taking the bus. A value is a broad principle you find important (family, respect). An intention is what you plan to do. Extrinsic motivation means doing something for an outside reward or to avoid punishment. In the picture: three tiles for three approaches, with what goes wrong under each.

Think of getting a teenager to tidy their room (our example): "do it for the planet" falls flat, a reward works until it stops, but "a tidy room means you find your football boots before the match" works. The article starts from a gap at work: research increasingly studies green strategies and human resource procedures, but their effects are underwhelming, because they depend on whether employees go along. Reasons employees do not: a mismatch with their values, low priority of sustainability causing conflicting goals, and cynicism when the organization says one thing and does another.

Most research tries to make people help the environment: changing environmental values, norms, knowledge and attitudes. But people tend to brush aside, or even push back against, messages that clash with what they already believe and want, so this fails with those who deny climate change, find it unimportant, or whose political identity opposes it. That is a large group: in a study of 1001 Americans 28% did not believe in global warming or that humans could reduce it; in a 2014 poll 38% of Australians and 36% of Americans said they were tired of all the attention for the environment. And environmental goals can conflict with other goals, such as privacy or the organization's priorities.

The second approach, from economics and behavioral finance, uses rewards or compliance. Problems: the effect of rewards does not last, and compliance strategies can backfire and cause rebound effects. The authors' verdict on both approaches: mixed effects at best.

The third approach builds on goal hierarchy and goal systems theories. Increase the self-concordance of the green behavior (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999): the degree to which it serves the person's own values, identities and long-term goals. People act by their own choice, so the downside of extrinsic motivation is avoided, and they pick their own goals, so a lack of interest in the environment does not block it. The authors predict this works even after controlling for environmental concern and political ideology.

The question is no longer "how do we make people care about the planet?" but "how do we show people that green behavior serves what they already care about?". Next: what self-concordance is, and why it can be changed.

⚠ The trap✗ The self-concordance approach works by making people care more about the environment.✓ It works by linking the green behavior to the person's own goals, which may have nothing to do with the environment (saving money, health, an easy life). That is why it should also work for people who do not hold environmental goals.
📝 Exam-style questionA company wants climate-skeptical sales staff to drive less. Explain why an environmental campaign may fail and what the self-concordance approach would do instead.

Model answer: People tend to brush aside, or even push back against, messages that clash with what they already believe and want, so a campaign built on climate goals is likely to be ineffective, or even backfire, for staff who reject climate change. Rewards would fade once they stop, and rules can cause rebound effects. The self-concordance approach would let each employee link driving less to goals they already hold (saving money, time to read or work on the train, fitness, being a good colleague), so that the behavior becomes more self-concordant and they intend to do it by their own choice.

The theory · Figure 1 · the hypotheses

One behavior, many goals

❓ This step answers: What is a goal hierarchy, what exactly is self-concordance, why can it be changed, and what did the authors predict?
🗂️ Explore Figure 1: six cards; each answer draws a new link in the academic's goal hierarchy.

A goal is something you want to reach. Higher-order goals are the more abstract goals above a behavior in your hierarchy: projects, identities, values. An identity is a role you see yourself in (teacher, father). To activate a goal is to make it active in your mind. In the picture: four rows, from values at the top to daily tasks at the bottom, with lines that help (green) or hinder (red).

Think of a train station (our analogy): the more of the lines you want to travel stop there, the more worth it it is to go there. Your goal hierarchy is a map of everything you strive for, at work and at home, in four layers: broad values (for example creativity), roles you identify with (for example researcher), longer projects (for example publishing a paper) and everyday tasks (for example answering emails). Figure 1 shows part of an academic's hierarchy: values such as respect, helpfulness and family; identities such as researcher, teacher and father; projects such as conducting research and teaching a course; tasks such as statistics and turning off office lights.

The levels are linked. A facilitative link means the behavior helps reach the goal: activating one activates the other. The academic sees statistics as helping him publish and as part of being a researcher, so wanting to publish brings statistics to mind. An inhibitive link means the behavior blocks the goal: activating one suppresses the other. The same academic sees statistics as taking time from teaching, so while doing statistics his teaching goal is pushed aside ("forgotten").

Self-concordance is the motivational pull a behavior gets from those links, combining two things: (1) more helping and fewer hindering links to higher-order goals, and (2) the personal importance of those goals. Unlike self-determination theory, which names goals that matter for everyone, it leaves it to each person to decide which goals count. A worked example (ours): video meetings help family time (very important) and hinder personal contact (slightly important), so most weight lands on the helping side. Two reasons why it motivates: utilitarian (one action pays off for many goals at once: more value for the same effort) and self-consistency (an action that serves many goals is easier to keep up and helps the parts of your personality fit together, which goes with well-being).

The links are malleable: situations and learning can activate, strengthen or weaken them. In Figure 1, turning off office lights helps only one goal (teaching students about green behavior), so its self-concordance is weak to moderate. If the academic realizes he can use it as a measure in his research, a new helping link appears and self-concordance rises, without any environmental goal. That is the lever: raise self-concordance through goals people already have. Why test it at all? Earlier self-concordance research covered behaviors with direct personal payoffs (work performance, coping, hazard planning); green behaviors mainly benefit society and pay off much later, so those findings could not simply be carried over.

Two earlier studies fit, though neither measured self-concordance. Framing climate change as a public health risk raised more supportive emotions than framing it as an environmental or national security risk (Myers and colleagues, 2012). Asking climate skeptics to link green citizenship behaviors to interpersonal warmth or societal development raised intentions more than linking them to fewer climate hazards (Bain and colleagues, 2012). The authors' explanation: most people hold such goals, while a climate goal clashes with many people's goals. But both studies prescribed one goal; the authors want people to choose their own.

Three hypotheses. H1: self-concordance of green behavior is positively related to intentions and actual behavior. H2a: getting people to connect green behaviors (energy use, commuting) to their higher-order goals increases intentions. H2b: that effect is mediated by self-concordance: the intervention works through raising it. Next: Study 1 tests H1.

⚠ The trap✗ Self-concordance means that a behavior fits the three basic needs of self-determination theory.✓ Self-concordance here does not prescribe which goals matter. It is calculated from the person's own goals: how helpful the behavior is for each, weighted by how important each goal is to that person.
📝 Exam-style questionUse the idea of a goal hierarchy to explain how a manager's self-concordance for video meetings instead of flying could be raised without mentioning the environment.

Model answer: In the manager's goal hierarchy, video meetings may now have few helping links and perhaps hindering ones (less personal contact). Connections are malleable, so an intervention can activate new facilitative links between video meetings and goals the manager already finds important: more time with family (value), being an effective leader (identity), finishing a project on time (project goal). Self-concordance combines the number of helping minus hindering links with the importance of the goals, so it rises, and by H2 so should the intention to choose video meetings. (The video-meeting case is our example.)

STEP3
A time-lagged correlational study · Tables 1 and 2

Does self-concordance go with green action?

❓ This step answers: How was self-concordance measured, how was real behavior captured, and what did Study 1 find?
🧮 Run Study 1: score one goal yourself, then follow the petitions and the analysis.

A correlational study measures things as they are, without changing anything; it shows whether they go together, not what causes what. Time-lagged means measured at different moments. Logistic regression predicts a yes/no outcome (signed or not) from several variables at once. A control variable is included to rule it out as the explanation ("controlling for" it). The p-value is the chance of finding a result this strong if there were no real effect; below .05 is called significant. B shows the direction and size of a predictor's link with the outcome; above zero means more of it goes with a higher chance of signing. In the picture: month 1, month 2, and the result table.

Think of checking whether students who see more reasons to study actually visit the library a month later (our example). Study 1 asks the same for green energy use, with 305 Australian working adults from a survey panel who completed two surveys one month apart (528 completed the first; 303 could be matched). Spreading the measures over time reduces response biases.

Participants rated how important 12 goals were to them (1 to 5): for example financial goals, frugality, being helpful to others, protecting the environment, looking after our children's future, having an easy life, reducing climate change. Then they rated how helpful environmentally sustainable energy use was for each goal (1 = very unhelpful, 7 = very helpful). Each helpfulness score was multiplied by its importance and the 12 products added up: a score from 12 to 420. It correlated with climate change beliefs (.22) and environmental identity (.49), as a related measure should (convergent validity), but not with political orientation (.02), a broader attitude (divergent validity).

At the end of the second survey, people could sign a petition to the government for 100% renewable energy (the measure), and a second one to scrap the carbon tax (a decoy against social desirability). They could decline, sign the standard text, change it, or write their own. Of 181 who answered, 29.3% were not interested; the rest signed, so the outcome was signed versus not.

Self-concordance predicted signing (B = .012, p = .007: each extra point raised the odds of signing by about 1%, and the score runs from 12 to 420). The controls covered environmental goals at three levels, from broad to specific: free-market ideology, environmental identity and climate change beliefs, plus age, gender and political orientation, to rule out that green goals rather than self-concordance drive the behavior (Table 2). Political orientation also mattered; the green beliefs did not, probably because they overlap with self-concordance. Counting the 124 people who skipped the question as non-signers, and four other model variants, gave the same result. H1 supported.

Study 1 shows a link between self-concordance and real behavior, but it is correlational: it cannot show cause, or that self-concordance can be changed. Next: an experiment.

STEP4
An experiment · Tables 3 to 5 · mediation

Tell and ask: can self-concordance be raised?

❓ This step answers: What was the intervention, did it raise intentions to save energy, and did it work through self-concordance?
🎲 Run the experiment: five decisions, then read the mediation picture.

In an experiment, the researchers change one thing and use random assignment so groups differ only by chance; then differences can be read as caused by the change. A control condition gets no intervention. Effect size d shows how large a difference is (by a common rule of thumb, not from the article, around .2 is small). R² is the share of the differences in an outcome that the predictors explain. Mediation means the effect runs through a middle variable. In the picture: the design, then a bar chart, then the mediation triangle.

Think of a coach who says: "running also helps you sleep and meet friends; how could it help you?" (our example). Study 2 tested this with 507 Australian adults from a survey panel, none from Study 1: 256 randomly assigned to the self-concordance condition, 251 to control.

The intervention had two parts. Tell: green behaviors have many other benefits: energy saving saves money, commuting gives time to read, careful waste means cleaner beaches for your holidays, socially responsible products mean less exploitation, walking and cycling mean fewer accidents. Ask: participants wrote how such behaviors could help them reach their own goals. The written answer was the manipulation check: 53 blank or "don't know" answers were removed, and 42 people who finished in under 8 minutes. 412 remained (172 and 240). Most answers were about energy, so the study focused on energy behavior.

The answers went far beyond the environment: money was mentioned most (51 answers, not always selfishly, such as saving money to treat one's children), environment or climate 37 times. Why it matters: if people had only linked saving energy to the planet, this would just be approach 1 in disguise. Table 3: compared with control, the experimental group saw saving energy as more helpful for green goals (protecting the environment, children's future, climate) and for non-green goals (financial goals, an easy life, being a good citizen, changing the world).

Intentions to save energy (1 to 5) were higher after the intervention: 3.46 versus 3.23 (p = .021, d = .23), and self-concordance higher too (262 versus 247, p = .045). Controlling for age, gender, education, political orientation, social desirability and environmental values, self-concordance still added explained variance (Table 5: R² from .25 to .39), and the strong effect of environmental values (.46) shrank to non-significant (.11). H2a supported.

Back to the coach: did the extra running come because people saw more reasons? Mediation checks this in steps. Total effect: the tell-and-ask group intended more (B = .23). Step 1: the intervention raised self-concordance (B = 15.17, p = .046). Step 2: higher self-concordance went with higher intentions (B = .01, p < .001). Step 3: comparing people with equal self-concordance, the intervention added nothing extra (direct effect B = .11, p = .17). So the whole effect travelled through self-concordance: full mediation. A bootstrap test (re-running the analysis on thousands of resamples) put the indirect effect between .03 and .24 with 95% confidence; that range excludes 0, so the indirect route is real. H2b supported.

A short written tell-and-ask exercise caused a small rise in green intentions, through self-concordance, for many kinds of goals. But it was compared with doing nothing. Next: the usual climate message as competition.

An experiment with three conditions

Your goals versus carbon facts

❓ This step answers: Does the self-concordance intervention also work for commuting, and does it beat the usual climate change message?
🚌 Run the experiment: predict the order of three groups, then check how solid the difference is.

A scenario is an imagined situation participants are asked to put themselves in. A contrast compares two specific groups. p = .056 means just above the usual .05 cut-off: often called marginal. In the picture: three conditions and a bar for each.

Think of two posters at a bus stop (our example): one with carbon numbers, one asking "what would you do with 40 free minutes a day?". Study 3 compared these approaches for a behavior many people dislike: commuting by public transport. 300 full-time employees from the UK and US (Prolific; students screened out, home workers removed; most drove to work) read: your company removes most parking spaces to build offices but will heavily subsidize public transport.

Random assignment to three conditions. Self-concordance: a list of seven advantages (saving money, time to read, time to catch up on work, exercise, helping others by reducing cars, no status differences, and one green benefit: better for the environment), a reminder of the person's own most important value and goals, then writing how public transport would help their personal goals. Climate change: carbon facts, then writing how it would help reduce climate change. Control: writing their thoughts about public transport. After removing non-participants: 49, 60 and 65 people. Intentions were four items (1 to 7), including two reversed ones, such as planning to avoid public transport whenever possible. Self-concordance was measured as before, with 13 goals ("being successful" added), and the inconvenience of public transport was also measured. 174 people were analysed.

In the self-concordance condition only 7 people wrote about the environment; most wrote about money (24) or leisure (relaxing, reading, talking). Controlling for environmental values, self-concordance of public transport was higher than in the climate (p = .033) and control conditions (p = .019). Intentions: 4.49, versus 3.92 for climate (p = .056) and 3.67 for control (p = .006).

The difference with control is clear. The difference with the climate message is marginal (p = .056, which the authors call significant), and without controlling for environmental values it was not significant (p = .16 for self-concordance, .088 for intentions). The authors note the means stayed similar and blame small groups and more variation; a careful reader treats the advantage over climate messages as promising, not proven. Why can removing a control variable raise p? More unexplained differences between people within each group make the same gap in averages less certain.

Across three studies: self-concordance went with real green behavior (Study 1), raising it raised intentions (Study 2), and it did at least as well as a climate message (Study 3). Next: what it means and where the limits are.

⚠ The trap✗ Study 3 clearly shows that the self-concordance message beats the climate change message.✓ It clearly beats the control condition (p = .006). Against the climate message the difference in intentions was marginal (p = .056) and not significant without the control variable (p = .088), although the means stayed about the same.

Model answer: Study 2 only compared the intervention with doing nothing. The climate change condition tests it against the traditional approach of motivating people by how their behavior helps mitigate climate change. Intentions were highest after the self-concordance intervention (4.49), then climate (3.92), then control (3.67). The advantage over control is clear; over the climate message it is marginal. 🆕 What the article adds

The first tests that self-concordance can be changed and that changing it has a causal effect: earlier tests were all correlational. Self-concordance also works for behaviors on the edge of people's self-serving goals, such as green behavior. And it explains earlier findings (Myers; Bain): those interventions prescribed one goal (public health, warmth, or the needs of self-determination theory) and so only work for people who value that goal; letting people choose their own goals reaches more people. Self-determination-based campaigns belong to approach 1.

🧰 What organizations can do with it

Design campaigns that show how a green behavior serves many common, non-environmental goals, and then let employees think through how it serves their goals. At work, link the behavior to workplace goals such as being a good employee or colleague, getting a promotion or building skills. This may be especially useful in organizations with mixed motives about the environment (like the fictional firm of Study 3, which removed parking to build offices but paid for public transport) or in industries that seem to contradict green values, such as oil companies: there, environmental appeals may backfire because people see a mismatch between words and deeds.

⚠️ How sure can we be? The limits the authors name

Small to medium effects, though the authors argue the tests were conservative: the petition is partly an activist act; Study 2 targeted a broad range of green behaviors; commuting by public transport is widely disliked. Not tested on skeptics as a group: samples came from the general population, although environmental goals were controlled for. Intentions, not behavior, in the experiments: intentions predict behavior, but not perfectly; adding action plans (when and where you will do it) could close the gap. A closed list of goals, limited to avoid fatigue; an open-ended approach should be tested. Unclear mechanism over time: did people form new links or become aware of existing ones? So it is unclear how long the effect lasts without reminders, although research in other domains (Carr & Walton, 2014) suggests such links can last.

🧩 The meeting's questions, from this reading

Why does self-concordance help to increase environmentally sustainable behaviors? A behavior that is connected to many goals that matter to a person, through helping links and few hindering ones, has more motivational pull: it serves several goals at once ("bang for the buck"), is easier to regulate and fits the person as a whole. Because the links are malleable, an intervention can create or highlight them, for any goals people hold, so it does not depend on environmental values and avoids the backlash and the fading effects of the other two approaches. How can organizations increase pro-environmental behaviors? By telling employees how green behaviors serve common goals and asking them how the behaviors serve their own, linked to workplace goals.
